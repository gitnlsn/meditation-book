This chapter teaches the body scan and examines two promises made in its name
and in the name of similar practices: better sleep and less pain. The short
answer is that both have real, modest evidence.

- **Sleep:** meditation improves sleep a little, but no more than the
  dedicated treatment for insomnia does.
- **Chronic pain:** meditation reduces pain a little, with low-quality
  evidence.
- **Blood pressure:** the signal is also small and still uncertain.

In pain research, the most interesting thing is not how much the pain goes
down. It is *what* changes: the relationship to the sensation, more than the
sensation itself. It is the second arrow again, this time in the body.

## What the Body Scan Is

The **body scan** is a practice in which attention moves slowly through the
body, from one end to the other. At each region it stops to notice what is
there: warmth, pressure, tingling, tension, pain, or nothing you can detect.
You don't try to relax the region, change it or imagine it differently. You
just notice, and move on.

The practice, in the form it became known in, comes from the MBSR program
(defined in chapter {{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}). The
program's book for the general public, Jon Kabat-Zinn's *Full Catastrophe
Living* (1990), presents it as one of the course's central practices, done
lying down.

It is related to the tradition, but it is not a copy of it. In the
*Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta*, the body is the first of the four foundations of
mindfulness (introduced in chapter {{cap:observar-sem-agarrar}}). The text
includes contemplations of the body part by part, with a purpose: seeing the
body arise and pass away, without clinging to anything. MBSR keeps the body
as an object of attention but swaps that purpose for a clinical goal:
learning to be with sensations, including difficult ones. It is an
adaptation, and it is presented as one here.

Why the body? There are two reasons, one practical and one theoretical.

- **The practical one:** bodily sensations are always present and happen
  *now*. A sensation in your foot is not in the past or the future, the way a
  thought can be. That makes the body a natural anchor.
- **The theoretical one:** perceiving the internal state of the body —
  interoception, defined in chapter {{cap:estresse-o-alarme-que-nao-desliga}}
  — plays a part in how the brain constructs emotions and assesses threats.
  Training that perception would be, in principle, training one of the
  inputs to the alarm system. "In principle" matters: this is a plausible
  theoretical argument, not a measured effect of the body scan. The technical
  version of it is in Barrett and Simmons (2015).

## How to Do It

The version below takes ten to twenty minutes. You can do it lying down or
sitting. Lying down is more comfortable and, for that very reason, makes you
sleepier, which will be an advantage or a problem depending on the time of
day.

1. **Lie on your back**, arms at your sides, or sit with support. Close your
   eyes if that's comfortable. Take three slightly slower breaths and then
   let the breath find its own rhythm.
2. **Start with the toes of your left foot.** Bring your attention to them
   and notice whatever is there: contact with the sheet, temperature,
   tingling. If you feel nothing, note "nothing" — that is an observation
   too.
3. **Move up slowly:** sole of the foot, heel, ankle, shin, knee, thigh. Stay
   with each region for the length of one or two breaths.
4. **Repeat on the right side**, then hips, abdomen, back (from bottom to
   top), chest, shoulders, arms down to the hands, neck, face, top of the
   head.
5. **When your attention wanders off** — and it will, to a plan, a memory, a
   comment about the practice — notice where it went. Then come back to the
   last region you remember. It is the same move of coming back taught in
   chapter {{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}}.
6. **When you meet discomfort**, don't push it away, and don't linger on it
   out of duty. Notice the qualities of the sensation: a stab or a weight?
   Steady or throbbing? Sharp-edged or diffuse? Then notice, separately, your
   reaction to it ("this again," "it's going to get worse"). That separation
   is the exercise.
7. **Finish with the whole body**: for a few breaths, feel the body as a
   whole, lying there, breathing.

**Before sleep.** Done in bed with the lights off, the scan can skip the last
step. If sleep comes partway through, the practice has done what you wanted
from it that night. Two cautions:

- **Don't use the scan to *force* sleep.** The more it becomes a task with a
  goal, the more it resembles the anxious wakefulness you are trying to let
  go of.
- **If you want to train attention, and not just fall asleep**, also do a
  session at another time of day, sitting up, with the aim of staying awake.

**If the body is a difficult place.** Some people have a history of trauma,
intense pain or anxiety about bodily sensations (as in panic). For them,
turning attention inward to the body can increase discomfort instead of
reducing it. In that case, open your eyes and shorten the practice. Use
external anchors, such as sounds or the soles of your feet on the floor. And
see chapter {{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}}.

## Sleep: What the Research Shows

**Short answer:** meditation improves sleep a little compared with
control activities (**moderate** evidence). But it does not beat the
dedicated treatment for insomnia, which is CBT-I (**strong** evidence).

**Insomnia** is persistent difficulty falling asleep or staying asleep, or
waking too early and being unable to get back to sleep. It takes a toll the
next day: tiredness, irritability, trouble concentrating. One bad night is
not insomnia. Chronic insomnia is a pattern that lasts for weeks or months.

The fact that organizes the rest: **the first-choice treatment for
chronic insomnia in adults is neither medication nor meditation. It is
CBT-I.**

**CBT-I** is cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia. It is a structured
treatment lasting a few weeks. It works on the habits and beliefs that keep
insomnia going: when you go to bed and get up, time spent in bed, what you
do when you can't sleep, worry about your own sleep. An American internal
medicine guideline (2016) recommends it as the initial treatment for all
adults with chronic insomnia. The European guideline of 2023 recommends it
as first-line treatment for adults of any age, in person or digital. The
evidence is **strong** because two independent guidelines, based on
systematic reviews, reach the same conclusion.

That is the background against which the meditation studies should be read.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Rusch and colleagues, published in 2019. A meta-analysis of 18
randomized trials, with 1,654 participants, on mindfulness meditation and
sleep quality. The authors separated two kinds of comparison: against
*nonspecific* active controls (activities that match time and attention, to
control for the placebo effect) and against *specific* active controls
(sleep treatments with proven efficacy).

**What it found.** Against nonspecific controls, meditation improved sleep
quality, with an effect size of 0.33 right after the intervention and 0.54
at follow-up — evidence of moderate strength, according to the authors.
Against specific sleep treatments, there was no difference (an effect size
of 0.03, with a confidence interval crossing zero).

**Strength of evidence:** moderate for "meditation improves sleep a little";
low, but consistent with the guidelines, for "it does not beat the dedicated
treatment for insomnia." The authors call the findings preliminary.
:::

The effect on sleep is small to moderate (the terms are in chapter
{{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}). It is noticeable on average, but far
from transformative.

The most impressive result comes from a single study. A 2015 trial randomly
assigned 49 older adults with moderate sleep problems. One group did six
weeks of mindfulness practice; the other, sleep hygiene classes. The
meditation group improved more in sleep, with a large effect, and also in
symptoms of insomnia, depression and fatigue. The control was active and
reasonable. But it is a small, single trial, and large effects in small
studies tend to shrink as other studies are added (the reason is in chapter
{{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}). Strength: **preliminary**.

Not every review agrees. The 2014 review that accepted only active controls
(described in chapter {{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}})
rated the evidence for sleep as low or insufficient. The difference comes,
in large part, from which studies and which controls each review included.
"The meta-analysis showed" depends on which meta-analysis.

In practice, a simple rule:

- **If you sleep badly now and then**, a body scan before bed is a
  reasonable, cheap, low-risk practice.
- **If you have chronic insomnia, seek CBT-I.** Meditation can accompany the
  treatment, not replace it.
- **If poor sleep comes with low mood or other symptoms**, that is a matter
  for a doctor, not for an app.

## Pain: What the Research Shows

**Short answer:** in chronic pain, meditation reduces pain a little, with
low-quality evidence (**moderate to preliminary**). In the lab, the effect is
sharper, but it was measured in healthy people with brief pain
(**preliminary**).

**Chronic pain** is pain that persists for more than three months. That is
the criterion proposed for the ICD-11, the international classification of
diseases. In the form that classification calls chronic *primary* pain, it
comes with emotional distress or significant limits on what the person can
do. It is not acute pain that has lasted too long: it is a condition with a
life of its own.

The history of clinical mindfulness begins here. The paper that founded MBSR
(Kabat-Zinn, 1982) described 51 patients with chronic pain who had not
improved with standard medical care. They went through a ten-week program,
and most reported less pain and less emotional distress. The study had no
control group, and the paper itself presents its results as preliminary. It
matters for the history, not as proof.

Kabat-Zinn's hypothesis, though, deserves attention. He proposed that
meditation produces an "uncoupling" of the sensory side of pain from the
alarm reaction that comes with it. That is, almost word for word, the
distinction between the first and the second arrow. It is also the
distinction between intensity and unpleasantness in Rainville's study,
presented in chapter {{cap:as-quatro-nobres-verdades-como-diagnostico}}.

More than forty years later, what do we know?

- **In chronic pain, the effects are small and the evidence is low
  quality.** A 2017 systematic review pooled 38 randomized trials. It found
  a *small* reduction in pain compared with all types of control, and rated
  the quality of that evidence as low. The effects on depression and quality
  of life were clearer than the effects on pain. The 2014 review that
  accepted only active controls reached a similar picture. Strength:
  **moderate to preliminary**, depending on how much weight you give to
  study quality.
- **In the lab, the effect is sharper — and further from real life.** Zeidan
  and colleagues (2015) randomly assigned healthy volunteers to four days of
  training. The groups meditated, used a placebo cream, did a sham
  meditation (instructions that imitated the practice without teaching it)
  or listened to a book. Faced with heat on the skin, all the active groups
  felt less pain. Meditation reduced intensity and unpleasantness *more*
  than placebo and more than the sham meditation. Strength: **preliminary**.
  The participants were healthy, the pain was brief and induced, and the
  result does not show efficacy in chronic pain.

Zeidan's study also measured the brain. It found different patterns for
meditation and for placebo. The correct reading is modest: the two effects
appear not to be the same effect. The exaggerated reading — "meditation
activates the brain's pain control center" — is reverse inference, the error
described in chapter {{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}.

## Why Understanding the Mechanism Changes Your Relationship to Pain

**Pain modulation** is the name for a well-established fact: the pain you
feel is not a direct readout of what is happening in the tissue. The nervous
system amplifies or dampens signals depending on attention, expectation,
beliefs and context.

This book's two pain studies show this by different routes. In Rainville, a
hypnotic suggestion changed unpleasantness. In Zeidan, meditation, placebo
and even a sham meditation all reduced pain to some degree. The authors
themselves read the result as a sign that the brain has several mechanisms
for modulating pain.

This has a practical consequence worth more than the effect size. Pain has
at least two layers: the sensation and the reaction to it. If you understand
that, you gain a place to work when the sensation cannot be changed.

![The two layers of pain and the names each side gives them. The second arrow and unpleasantness are approximations of the same layer, not the same thing: the text's arrow is wider and slower than the unpleasantness measured in the lab.](../illustrations/figures/fig-duas-camadas-dor-en.svg)

Step 6 of the body scan trains exactly this. First, describe the sensation
precisely ("stabbing, on the right, throbbing"). Then notice, separately,
the layer of alarm, prediction and resistance ("this isn't going to go
away," "I can't take any more"). Neither the tradition nor the lab promises
that the first arrow disappears.

Three cautions, so this doesn't turn into guilt:

1. **"The pain is all in your head" is a misreading.** Saying the brain
   modulates pain is not saying the pain is imaginary. All pain is processed
   by the brain, including the pain of a broken bone.
2. **If the practice doesn't reduce your pain, you did nothing wrong.** The
   average effects are small; for many people, they don't show up.
3. **Meditation is not a substitute for treatment for pain.** New pain, pain
   that changes its pattern, or pain that comes with other symptoms needs a
   medical evaluation. For chronic pain, meditation comes in as a complement
   to care guided by a health professional, not in place of it.

## Blood Pressure: What the Research Shows

**Short answer:** meditation may lower blood pressure by a few
millimeters of mercury (**preliminary** evidence). Whether the effect lasts,
or prevents heart attacks and strokes, is not known.

**Blood pressure** is the force of the blood against the walls of the
arteries. It is measured in millimeters of mercury (mm Hg), as two numbers:
systolic, when the heart contracts, and diastolic, between one beat and the
next. High blood pressure is a leading risk factor for cardiovascular
disease. It is where the stress of chapter
{{cap:estresse-o-alarme-que-nao-desliga}} meets a number doctors track.

Transcendental Meditation, a meditation technique different from the ones
this book teaches, has studies of its own on the subject. A 2022 meta-analysis of 18 studies found a fall of about
3 mm Hg in systolic pressure. But the effect waned after three months.

For mindfulness, there is a recent trial worth examining, MB-BP. It found
an advantage of a few mm Hg. But it was run by the program's developer,
against a control with no equivalent activity.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** The MB-BP trial (Loucks and colleagues, 2023), a phase 2
trial: an intermediate trial, run before a definitive test. It assigned by
lot 201 adults with elevated blood pressure (120/80 mm Hg or higher) to a
mindfulness program adapted for high blood pressure or to enhanced usual
care (the same home blood pressure monitor and an educational brochure,
with no equivalent program). The people who measured blood pressure did
not know which group each participant was in. Follow-up: six months.

**What it found.** Systolic pressure fell 5.9 mm Hg in the mindfulness
group and ended 4.5 mm Hg below the control group's, with a confidence
interval of −9.0 to −0.1 mm Hg, close to zero at the upper end.

**Strength of evidence:** preliminary. A single trial, with 201 people,
against a control with no equivalent activity, with 17.4% loss to
follow-up and a sample that was 81.1% non-Hispanic White. The first author
developed the program and was one of the study's three instructors. He
reports no financial compensation from it; the primary outcome was
preregistered and analyzed by an independent statistician blind to the
groups.
:::

The reviews confirm a signal and underline its limits.

- **A 2026 meta-analysis** pooled 21 trials of mindfulness and found a fall
  of about 6 mm Hg in systolic pressure. The authors call the effect large.
  That conclusion goes further than the quality of the reviews in this area
  allows, as the next review shows. None of the trials reported adverse
  effects.
- **A review of the reviews, also from 2026,** rated all 11 it examined as
  low or critically low in quality, except one. The exception, a
  high-quality Cochrane review, found a fall of about 6 mm Hg in systolic
  pressure against active controls. But the margin of uncertainty around
  that number includes no effect. The developer of the MB-BP program
  is among the authors, which calls for the care due to anyone judging a
  field they work in themselves.

**What is not yet known, and why.** Whether the effect lasts is not known,
because long-term data are lacking; with Transcendental Meditation, it waned
within three months. Nor whether it reduces heart attacks and strokes,
because the trials measure blood pressure, not those outcomes.

People with high blood pressure should not swap their medication or the
recommended lifestyle changes for meditation. Nor should they stop a
medication without talking to their doctor. Finally, studies reporting no
adverse effects does not mean there are none. It only means none were
recorded (see chapter {{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}}).

## What the Tradition Says and What Science Says, Side by Side

| | What the tradition claims | What the research shows |
|---|---|---|
| Attention to the body | The body is the first foundation of mindfulness; contemplating it leads to seeing impermanence | The body scan is a central MBSR practice; the studies cited evaluate whole programs, not the scan in isolation |
| Sleep | The texts cited in this book do not treat sleep as a goal | Small to moderate improvement; does not beat CBT-I |
| Blood pressure | The texts cited in this book don't address it | A fall of a few mm Hg in small, short trials; unknown whether it lasts or prevents disease |
| Pain | The second arrow can cease; the first cannot | Small reduction in chronic pain, low-quality evidence; in the lab, intensity and unpleasantness go down |

The convergence is in the last row, and it is the same one as in the chapter
on the Four Noble Truths: tradition and science both separate pain from the
reaction to pain. The divergence is there too. The tradition speaks of a
second arrow that *ceases*. Science measures a second layer that *decreases
a little*, on average, in some people.

## What This Chapter Established

- The **body scan** moves attention through the body, noticing sensations
  without trying to change them. It comes from MBSR, which adapted
  contemplations of the body found in the tradition to a clinical goal.
- **Sleep:** meditation improves sleep quality a little compared with
  nonspecific controls (moderate), but it does not beat the dedicated
  treatment. The first-choice treatment for chronic insomnia is **CBT-I**
  (strong).
- **Chronic pain:** a small reduction, with low-quality evidence (moderate to
  preliminary). In the lab, with healthy volunteers, meditation reduced
  intensity and unpleasantness more than placebo (preliminary).
- **Pain modulation** is real and well established: attention, expectation
  and context change the pain you feel. That does not make the pain
  imaginary.
- What the practice trains is separating the sensation from the reaction to
  it — the same distinction as the second arrow, now with the body as the
  object.
- **Blood pressure:** meditation may lower blood pressure by a few mm Hg
  (preliminary). Whether the effect lasts, or prevents heart attacks and
  strokes, is not known.
- Meditation is not a substitute for treatment for insomnia, for pain or for
  high blood pressure.
