This chapter teaches the body scan, the practice of moving attention through
the body part by part, and examines what research says about two promises
made in its name and in the name of similar practices: better sleep and less
pain. The short answer is that both have real, modest evidence. Meditation
improves sleep a little, but no more than the dedicated treatment for
insomnia does; and it reduces chronic pain a little, with low-quality
evidence. The most interesting thing pain research shows is not how much the
pain goes down but *what* changes: the relationship to the sensation, more
than the sensation itself. It is the second arrow again, this time in the
body.

## What the Body Scan Is

The **body scan** is a practice in which attention moves slowly through the
body from one end to the other — from the feet to the head, for example —
stopping at each region to notice what is there: warmth, pressure, tingling,
tension, pain, or nothing you can detect. You don't try to relax the region,
change it or imagine it differently. You just notice, and move on.

The practice, in the form it became known in, comes from the MBSR program
(defined in {{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}). The
program's book for the general public, Jon Kabat-Zinn's *Full Catastrophe
Living* (1990), presents it as one of the course's central formal practices,
done lying down.

It is related to the tradition, but it is not a copy of it. In the
*Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta*, the body is the first of the four foundations of
mindfulness (introduced in {{cap:observar-sem-agarrar}}), and the text
includes contemplations of the body part by part. The MBSR scan keeps the
idea of using the body as an object of attention, but sets aside the purpose
the sutta gives those contemplations — seeing the body arise and pass away,
without clinging to anything — and replaces it with a clinical goal:
learning to be with sensations, including difficult ones. It is an
adaptation, and it is presented as one here.

Why the body? There are two reasons, one practical and one theoretical.

- **The practical one:** bodily sensations are always present and happen
  *now*. Unlike a thought, a sensation in your foot is not in the past or
  the future. That makes the body a natural anchor.
- **The theoretical one:** perceiving the internal state of the body —
  interoception, defined in {{cap:estresse-o-alarme-que-nao-desliga}} — plays
  a part in how the brain constructs emotions and assesses threats. Training
  that perception is, in principle, training one of the inputs to the alarm
  system. "In principle" matters: this is a plausible theoretical argument
  (Barrett and Simmons 2015 develop the technical version of it), not a
  measured effect of the body scan.

## How to Do It

The version below takes ten to twenty minutes. You can do it lying down or
sitting; lying down is more comfortable and, for that very reason, makes you
sleepier — which will be an advantage or a problem depending on the time of
day.

1. **Lie on your back**, arms at your sides, or sit with support. Close your
   eyes if that's comfortable. Take three slightly slower breaths and then
   let the breath find its own rhythm.
2. **Start with the toes of your left foot.** Bring your attention to them
   and notice whatever is there: contact with the sheet, temperature,
   tingling. If you feel nothing, note "nothing" — that is an observation
   too.
3. **Move up slowly:** sole of the foot, heel, ankle, shin, knee, thigh. Stay
   with each region for the length of one or two breaths.
4. **Repeat on the right side**, then hips, abdomen, back (from bottom to
   top), chest, shoulders, arms down to the hands, neck, face, top of the
   head.
5. **When your attention wanders off** — and it will, to a plan, a memory, a
   comment about the practice — notice where it went and come back to the
   last region you remember. It is the same move of coming back taught in
   {{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}}.
6. **When you meet discomfort**, don't push it away, and don't linger on it
   out of duty. Notice the qualities of the sensation (a stab or a weight?
   steady or throbbing? sharp-edged or diffuse?) and notice, separately, your
   reaction to it ("this again," "it's going to get worse"). That separation
   is the exercise.
7. **Finish with the whole body**: for a few breaths, feel the body as a
   whole, lying there, breathing.

**Before sleep.** Done in bed with the lights off, the scan can skip the last
step: if sleep comes partway through, the practice has done what you wanted
from it that night. Two cautions. First, don't turn the scan into a tool for
*forcing* sleep; the more it becomes a task with a goal, the more it
resembles the anxious wakefulness you are trying to let go of. Second, if
you want to train attention, and not just fall asleep, also do a session at
another time of day, sitting up, where the aim is to stay awake.

**If the body is a difficult place.** For people with a history of trauma,
intense pain or anxiety about bodily sensations (as in panic), turning
attention inward to the body can increase discomfort instead of reducing it.
In that case, open your eyes, shorten the practice, use external anchors
(sounds, the soles of your feet on the floor), and see the chapter
{{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}}.

## Sleep: What the Research Shows

**Insomnia** is persistent difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or
waking too early and being unable to get back to sleep, with consequences
the next day — tiredness, irritability, trouble concentrating. One bad night
is not insomnia; chronic insomnia is a pattern that lasts for weeks or
months.

Before talking about meditation, the fact that organizes everything else:
**the first-choice treatment for chronic insomnia in adults is neither
medication nor meditation. It is CBT-I.** **CBT-I** is cognitive behavioral
therapy for insomnia, a structured treatment lasting a few weeks that works
on the habits and beliefs that keep insomnia going: when you go to bed and
get up, time spent in bed, what you do when you can't sleep, worry about
your own sleep. The American College of Physicians guideline (Qaseem and
colleagues, 2016) recommends CBT-I as the initial treatment for all adults
with chronic insomnia, and the European guideline updated in 2023 (Riemann
and colleagues) recommends it as first-line treatment for adults of any age,
in person or digital. Strength of evidence: **strong**, in the sense that two
independent clinical guidelines, based on systematic reviews, converge.

That is the background against which the meditation studies should be read.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Rusch and colleagues, published in 2019. A meta-analysis of 18
randomized trials, with 1,654 participants, on mindfulness meditation and
sleep quality. The authors separated two kinds of comparison: against
*nonspecific* active controls (activities that match time and attention, to
control for the placebo effect) and against *specific* active controls
(sleep treatments with proven efficacy).

**What it found.** Against nonspecific controls, meditation improved sleep
quality, with an effect size of 0.33 right after the intervention and 0.54
at follow-up — evidence of moderate strength, according to the authors.
Against specific sleep treatments, there was no difference (an effect size
of 0.03, with a confidence interval crossing zero).

**Strength of evidence:** moderate for "meditation improves sleep a little";
low, but consistent with the guidelines, for "it does not beat the dedicated
treatment for insomnia." The authors call the findings preliminary.
:::

An effect size of 0.33 is small to moderate (the terms are in
{{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}): noticeable on average, far from
transformative. The most impressive number in the literature comes from a
single study. Black and colleagues (2015) randomized 49 older adults, with
an average age of 66 and moderate sleep problems, to six weeks of
mindfulness practice or to sleep hygiene education. The meditation group
improved more on the sleep quality index, with an effect size of 0.89, and
also on symptoms of insomnia, depression and fatigue. It is a good result,
with a reasonable active control — but it is a small, single trial, and
large effects in small studies tend to shrink as other studies are added
(the reason is in {{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}). Strength:
**preliminary**.

There is one more tension to record. The meta-analysis by Goyal and
colleagues (2014), restricted to active controls, rated the evidence for
sleep as low or insufficient. The difference from Rusch comes, in large
part, from which studies and which controls each one included — a reminder
that "the meta-analysis showed" depends on which meta-analysis.

In practice, this gives you a simple rule. If you sleep badly now and then,
a body scan before bed is a reasonable, cheap, low-risk practice. **If you
have chronic insomnia, seek CBT-I**; meditation can accompany the treatment,
not replace it. And if poor sleep comes with low mood or other symptoms,
that is a matter for a doctor, not for an app.

## Pain: What the Research Shows

**Chronic pain** is pain that persists for more than three months — that is
the criterion in the classification proposed by the International
Association for the Study of Pain for the ICD-11 (Nicholas and colleagues,
2019). In the form that classification calls chronic *primary* pain, it
comes with emotional distress or significant limits on what the person can
do. It is not acute pain that has lasted too long: it is a condition with a
life of its own.

The history of clinical mindfulness begins right here. The paper that
founded MBSR (Kabat-Zinn, 1982) described 51 patients with chronic pain who
had not improved with standard medical care and who went through a ten-week
program — mostly for low back, neck and shoulder pain, and headache. Most
reported less pain and less emotional distress. The paper presents itself
as a preliminary result, and the study had no control group: it matters for
the history, not as proof. But Kabat-Zinn's hypothesis deserves attention.
He proposed that meditation produces an "uncoupling" of the sensory
dimension of pain from the affective alarm reaction that comes with it. That
is, almost word for word, the distinction between the first and the second
arrow, and between intensity and unpleasantness in Rainville's study,
presented in {{cap:as-quatro-nobres-verdades-como-diagnostico}}.

More than forty years later, what do we know?

- **In chronic pain, the effects are small and the evidence is low
  quality.** The systematic review by Hilton and colleagues (2017) pooled 38
  randomized trials and found, in 30 of them, a *small* reduction in pain
  compared with all types of control, with low-quality evidence under the
  GRADE system. The effects on depression and quality of life were clearer
  than the effects on pain. The meta-analysis by Goyal and colleagues (2014)
  reached a similar picture: moderate evidence of a small improvement.
  Strength: **moderate to preliminary**, depending on how much weight you
  give to study quality.
- **In the lab, the effect is sharper — and further from real life.** Zeidan
  and colleagues (2015) randomly assigned 75 healthy volunteers to four days
  of meditation, a placebo cream, a sham meditation (instructions that
  imitated the practice without teaching it) or a control in which they
  listened to a book. All the active groups felt less pain from heat applied
  to the skin; meditation reduced intensity and unpleasantness *more* than
  placebo and more than the sham meditation. Strength: **preliminary**. The
  participants were healthy, the pain was brief and induced, and the result
  does not show efficacy in chronic pain.

Zeidan's study also measured the brain and found different patterns for
meditation (more activity in regions associated with the cognitive
modulation of pain) and for placebo. The correct reading is modest: the two
effects appear not to be the same effect. The exaggerated reading —
"meditation activates the brain's pain control center" — is reverse
inference, the error described in {{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}.

## Why Understanding the Mechanism Changes Your Relationship to Pain

**Pain modulation** is the name for a well-established fact: the pain you
feel is not a direct readout of what is happening in the tissue. The nervous
system amplifies or dampens signals depending on attention, expectation,
beliefs and context. This book's two pain studies show this by different
routes: in Rainville, a hypnotic suggestion changed unpleasantness; in
Zeidan, meditation, placebo and even a fake meditation all reduced pain to
some degree. The authors themselves read the result as confirmation that the
brain has multiple cognitive mechanisms for modulating pain.

This has a practical consequence worth more than the effect size. If you
understand that pain has at least two layers — the sensation and the
reaction to it — you gain a place to work when the sensation cannot be
changed. Step 6 of the body scan trains exactly this: describing the
sensation precisely ("stabbing, on the right, throbbing") and noticing,
separately, the layer of alarm, prediction and resistance ("this isn't going
to go away," "I can't take any more"). What the tradition calls the second
arrow and what the lab calls unpleasantness are different approximations of
that second layer. Neither promises that the first arrow disappears.

Three cautions, so this doesn't turn into guilt:

1. **"The pain is all in your head" is a misreading.** Saying the brain
   modulates pain is not saying the pain is imaginary. All pain is processed
   by the brain, including the pain of a broken bone.
2. **If the practice doesn't reduce your pain, you did nothing wrong.** The
   average effects are small; for many people, they don't show up.
3. **Meditation is not a substitute for treatment for pain.** New pain, pain
   that changes its pattern, or pain that comes with other symptoms needs a
   medical evaluation. For chronic pain, meditation comes in as a complement
   to care guided by a health professional, not in place of it.

## What the Tradition Says and What Science Says, Side by Side

| | What the tradition claims | What the research shows |
|---|---|---|
| Attention to the body | The body is the first foundation of mindfulness; contemplating it leads to seeing impermanence | The body scan is a central MBSR practice; the studies cited evaluate whole programs, not the scan in isolation |
| Sleep | The texts cited in this book do not treat sleep as a goal | Small to moderate improvement; does not beat CBT-I |
| Pain | The second arrow can cease; the first cannot | Small reduction in chronic pain, low-quality evidence; in the lab, intensity and unpleasantness go down |

The convergence is in the last row, and it is the same one as in the chapter
on the Four Noble Truths: tradition and science both separate pain from the
reaction to pain. The divergence is there too. The tradition speaks of a
second arrow that *ceases*; science measures a second layer that *decreases
a little*, on average, in some people.

## What This Chapter Established

- The **body scan** moves attention through the body, noticing sensations
  without trying to change them. It comes from MBSR, which adapted
  contemplations of the body found in the tradition to a clinical goal.
- **Sleep:** meditation improves sleep quality a little compared with
  nonspecific controls (moderate), but it does not beat the dedicated
  treatment. The first-choice treatment for chronic insomnia is **CBT-I**
  (strong).
- **Chronic pain:** a small reduction, with low-quality evidence (moderate to
  preliminary). In the lab, with healthy volunteers, meditation reduced
  intensity and unpleasantness more than placebo (preliminary).
- **Pain modulation** is real and well established: attention, expectation
  and context change the pain you feel. That does not make the pain
  imaginary.
- What the practice trains is separating the sensation from the reaction to
  it — the same distinction as the second arrow, now with the body as the
  object.
- Meditation is not a substitute for treatment for insomnia or for pain.
