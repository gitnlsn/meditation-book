This chapter teaches the oldest and simplest practice in Buddhist meditation:
paying attention to the breath. The central idea is a single one, and the
rest of the chapter exists to support it: the practice is not about keeping
attention still, but about **coming back**. Attention drifts off, you notice
that it has, and you bring it back. Each return is a repetition, the way each
lift counts in strength training. By the end, you will know how to do ten
minutes a day, what the tradition says this practice produces, what
experiments have measured, and where the two stop lining up.

## The Text: The Discourse on Mindfulness of Breathing

The practice has its own name in Pali ({{cap:quem-foi-o-buda-e-quem-ele-nao-foi}}
explains what Pali is): **ānāpānasati**, literally "attention (*sati*) to the
in-breath and the out-breath (*ānāpāna*)." The term *sati* was introduced in
{{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}}; here it is enough to
remember that it means keeping something present in the mind, not
"relaxing."

The reference text is the *Ānāpānasati Sutta*, discourse number 118 in the
collection of middle-length discourses (*Majjhima Nikāya*). It begins with
the body. In Bhikkhu Sujato's translation:

> It's when a mendicant—gone to a wilderness, or to the root of a tree, or
> to an empty hut—sits down cross-legged, sets their body straight, and
> brings mindfulness to the present. Just mindful, they breathe in.
> Mindful, they breathe out.

Then comes a sentence that sets the tone for everything that follows:
breathing in long, the practitioner *knows* they are breathing in long;
breathing in short, they *know* they are breathing in short. (The Pali says
*dīgha* and *rassa*, "long" and "short"; Sujato prefers "heavily" and
"lightly.") The text does not tell you to lengthen the breath, or to
control it. It tells you to know how it is.

The discourse goes on for sixteen steps, in four groups of four. The first
group deals with the body and the breath; the second, with the pleasant
feelings that may arise; the third, with the mind; the fourth, with
impermanence, fading away, cessation and letting go. The text itself links
each group to one of the four foundations of mindfulness in the
*Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta*, the subject of {{cap:observar-sem-agarrar}}, and says
that the practice, once developed, leads to "knowledge and freedom."

Two consequences for a beginner. First, this chapter uses only the first
steps: noticing the breath as it is. The rest of the discourse is a path
that, in the tradition, takes years. Second, in the text the breath was
never a relaxation exercise. It is the entrance to a training whose stated
goal is liberation from suffering in the Buddhist sense.

## Counting: A Tool from the Commentary, Not the Sutta

The sutta says nothing about counting. **Counting** — numbering the breaths
to give the mind a simple support — comes from a much later text, the
*Visuddhimagga* ("the path of purification"), written in Pali in Sri Lanka by
the monk Buddhaghosa in the first quarter of the fifth century. It is the
great meditation manual of the Theravāda school and, to this day, a
reference for how the tradition *interpreted* the suttas.

The *Visuddhimagga* recommends that the beginner start by counting, with
precise rules: "he should not stop short of five or go beyond ten or make
any break in the series." Stopping short of five, the text says, means "his
thoughts get excited in the cramped space, like a herd of cattle shut in a
cramped pen." Going beyond ten makes the mind lean on the number rather than
the breath. And when do you stop counting? When, without counting,
attention stays settled on the breath, because "counting is simply a device"
for fixing attention and cutting off distraction.

This distinction matters for the sake of honesty: counting is a technique of
the tradition, but not of its oldest layer. It is a small example of
something the book will keep returning to — Buddhism is a living tradition,
one that added and debated methods over centuries.

## The Anchor, and Why Attention Drifts

Modern vocabulary calls the breath, in this practice, the **anchor**: the
object attention comes back to whenever it slips loose. The word is a good
one, because an anchored boat does not sit motionless. It drifts, and the
anchor keeps it from going off. The anchor doesn't have to be the breath; it
can be a sound, the contact of your feet with the floor or of your hands in
your lap. The breath is the classic choice because it is always present,
changes all the time and takes no effort.

Attention will drift. Not "it may": it will. {{cap:a-mente-que-divaga}}
showed that the mind spends a large part of its time away from what it is
doing, and a person sitting and watching the breath is no exception. The
useful question is not how to stop it from drifting, but what to do once it
has.

{{cap:a-mente-que-divaga}} described the study by Wendy Hasenkamp and
colleagues (2012), which gave shape to this cycle: fourteen practitioners
meditated on the breath inside a scanner and pressed a button each time they
noticed they had drifted from it, and the practice was divided into four
phases — wandering, noticing, redirecting, sustaining. The study is small,
and its readings of brain networks carry the risk of reverse inference. But
the model is useful to a practitioner for another reason. It shows that even
experienced practitioners wander — if they didn't, there would be no button
to press — and that the training lies in the noticing and redirecting
phases. **Noticing and coming back is the exercise**, not a failure in the
exercise.

## The Practice

Ten minutes a day, for one week. A fixed time helps more than motivation;
{{cap:uma-pratica-para-a-vida}} deals with that.

**Posture** is the way you sit, and in the tradition it has a function: to
keep the body stable and alert, without strain. The sutta speaks of crossed
legs and an upright body, but a chair works just as well.

1. **Sit** on a chair with your feet on the floor, or on a cushion with your
   legs crossed. Spine upright without stiffness, as if a thread were gently
   pulling up the top of your head. Hands in your lap. Eyes closed or half
   open, resting on the floor a meter in front of you.
2. **Set an alarm** for ten minutes. That way you don't need to check the
   clock.
3. **Find the breath** where it is clearest: at the nostrils, in the chest
   or in the belly. Pick one place and stay with it for the session.
4. **Don't change the breath.** It will change on its own as you watch it;
   that's fine. Don't try to make it longer, deeper or more "correct."
5. **Count at the end of each out-breath**: in, out, "one"; in, out, "two";
   up to "ten." Then start again at "one." The counting is mental, in
   silence.
6. **When you notice you've lost count** — or that you're at "seventeen,"
   or planning lunch — note the fact without comment and start again at
   "one." Starting over is not losing: it is exactly the repetition that
   trains.
7. **At the end of the week**, if counting has become easy, try dropping it
   and simply following the breath, as the *Visuddhimagga* suggests. If the
   mind starts scattering a lot again, go back to counting.

Some signs that you are doing it right, even if it doesn't feel that way:
you lost count many times and noticed; you felt bored, sleepy or restless
and noticed; you got irritated with your own mind and noticed that too. One
sign that you are doing it the hard way: fighting every thought as if it
were a mistake.

## What the Practice Isn't: "Emptying the Mind"

The most widespread idea about meditation is that it means stopping
thinking. Neither the sutta nor the commentary says so. The sutta asks you to
*know* how the breath is — an act of knowing, not of erasing. The
*Visuddhimagga* uses counting precisely because it assumes the mind will
scatter. And Hasenkamp's model shows experienced practitioners wandering
again and again.

Treating an "empty mind" as the goal has two bad effects. The first is
frustration: the beginner thinks during the session, concludes they "can't
meditate" and gives up. The second is subtler: trying to push thoughts out
is a form of resistance, the same thing
{{cap:as-quatro-nobres-verdades-como-diagnostico}} described as the second
arrow. The instruction is different: the thought shows up, you notice, you
come back.

## What the Science Says the Practice Does

Here the book changes register: from what the texts claim to what
experiments have measured. The terms *focused attention* and *open
monitoring* were defined in {{cap:atencao-emocao-e-habito}}; breath counting
is a typical example of focused attention.

**Sustained attention: moderate evidence, at a high dose.** The best study is
the Shamatha Project (MacLean and colleagues, 2010), described in
{{cap:atencao-emocao-e-habito}}: a three-month retreat, with about five
hours a day of attention on a chosen object such as the breath, improved
vigilance in a randomized trial with a waitlist. What the study shows is that
sustained attention is trainable. It does not show how much training an
ordinary person needs, and the dose has nothing in common with ten minutes a
day.

**Short doses: preliminary evidence.** Here the studies are smaller and the
results more fragile.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Amishi Jha and colleagues, 2015. Three military groups
preparing for deployment: one received an eight-hour course over eight weeks
centered on practical attention exercises (40 people); another, a course of
the same length centered on lectures about stress and resilience (40); the
third, no training (24). A group of 45 civilians served as a comparison. The
groups were randomized by platoon, not by individual, and the authors
acknowledge that this may leave differences at baseline.

**What it found.** On a sustained-attention test, the group with no training
got worse over the preparation period, and the practical-course group held
steady — with fewer lapses than the no-training group and the lecture group.
The most interesting detail: participants were supposed to practice thirty
minutes a day, but the practical group logged, on average, about 178 minutes
*in total* over the eight weeks.

**Strength of evidence:** preliminary. A small sample, randomization by
group, and the creator of the program is a coauthor (according to the
paper, she took no part in data collection or analysis). The result suggests
that practicing, more than hearing about practice, is what counts — and that
even modest doses may protect attention under pressure. It does not prove a
minimum dose.
:::

Other studies point the same way, all with limits; the five-day study with
Chinese university students is in {{cap:atencao-emocao-e-habito}}. And breath
counting itself has been used as a measuring instrument: Daniel Levinson and
colleagues, in Richard Davidson's lab, showed in four studies with more than
four hundred people that making fewer counting errors goes along with less
mind wandering and more awareness of one's own mental state, and that four
weeks of online breath-counting training reduced mind wandering compared
with working-memory training and with no training. That gives counting a
double role: it is an exercise and it is a ruler.

The honest summary: sustained attention improves with focused-attention
training (**moderate**); that ten minutes a day is enough for a measurable
effect is plausible, but still **preliminary**. And, as
{{cap:atencao-emocao-e-habito}} discusses, gains on a lab task do not always
carry over to life.

## Where Tradition and Science Part Ways

The two talk about the same practice, but not about the same goal.

| | Tradition (MN 118, *Visuddhimagga*) | Science (studies cited) |
|---|---|---|
| What it's for | Stabilizing the mind as a basis for seeing impermanence and letting go; "knowledge and freedom" | Improving sustained attention, reducing lapses and mind wandering |
| What is measured | Nothing is measured; it is described in stages of practice | Performance on computer tasks, self-report |
| Time | Years, with a teacher | Days to months |
| Context | Ethics, community, renunciation | Laboratory, army base, online course |

Science measures a slice of what the tradition describes: the part that can
be turned into a computer task. The final steps of the sutta — observing
impermanence, cessation, letting go — have, for now, no equivalent test.
That does not make them false. It makes them a claim in another register:
that of what the text proposes and what practitioners report, not of what
experiments have shown.

## Cautions

The breath is the most widely used anchor, but it is not neutral for
everyone. For some people, especially those with panic attacks, asthma or a
history of trauma, paying attention to the breath can increase anxiety
instead of calming it. If that happens, switch anchors: the sounds around
you, the weight of your body on the chair, your hands. Opening your eyes
also helps.

The adverse effects of meditation are real and documented, even in
eight-week programs: a careful study of participants in mindfulness-based
cognitive therapy found lasting bad effects in a minority, associated with
signs of dysregulated arousal and dissociation. Ten minutes a day is a low
dose, but it is not a zero dose. {{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}}
describes the signs and when to stop. And no practice in this book is a
substitute for treatment of anxiety, depression or any diagnosis.

## What This Chapter Established

- **Ānāpānasati** is the attention to the breath described in the sutta MN
  118: knowing how the breath is, without controlling it, as the first step
  of a sixteen-stage path that, in the tradition, aims at liberation.
- **Counting** from one to ten comes from the *Visuddhimagga* (fifth
  century), not from the sutta; it is a temporary support.
- The **anchor** is the place attention comes back to. The practice is the
  cycle of wandering, noticing and coming back, and **each return is a
  repetition**, not a mistake.
- "Emptying the mind" is not the goal in any of the texts.
- Sustained attention improves with focused-attention training
  (**moderate**, at high doses); the effects of short doses are
  **preliminary**.
- Science measures one part of what the tradition proposes. The rest is
  textual claim and practitioner report, and should be read as such.
