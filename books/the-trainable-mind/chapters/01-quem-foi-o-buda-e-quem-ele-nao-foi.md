This chapter answers a question that comes before any practice: what is
Buddhism? The short answer is that it is a religion in the full sense, with
rites, devotion, monastic communities and a view of the cosmos that includes
rebirth, practiced today by hundreds of millions of people in schools that
disagree with one another. The version that usually reaches Western readers,
a "rational," "scientific" Buddhism centered on meditation, is a modern and
selective reading of that tradition, with a history of its own. This book
makes a selection too, and the chapter explains which one and why.

## A Man and a Title

**Buddha** is not a name; it is a title. The word means "awakened": someone
who has discovered the way to the end of suffering and taught it to others.
The tradition holds that there were other buddhas in the past and that there
will be others in the future. Modern scholars generally stay agnostic about
this and focus on the historical individual, known in the tradition as
Gautama (in Pali, Gotama), who is regarded as the founder of Buddhism.
**Pali** is the ancient language in which the canon of the Theravāda school
was preserved. When this book uses a Pali term, it is because the English
word alone would mislead, and the book says why.

What we know about him with reasonable confidence is little, and it helps to
separate the layers.

**What is fairly settled.** According to the summary by the philosopher Mark
Siderits in the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, Gautama was born into a
family of some wealth and power, of the Śākya clan, in the area of the present
border between India and Nepal. As an adult he left household life (and,
according to the story, his wife and infant son) to look for a way out of
suffering. He spent time with wandering ascetics, the *śramaṇas*, who claimed
to know that way out. Dissatisfied with them, he went on alone and, through a
combination of understanding and meditative practice, reached what the
tradition calls awakening. He spent the rest of his life teaching.

**What is contested.** The dates. The tradition says he lived to eighty. For
a long time something like 560–480 BCE was accepted; today many scholars
think he died around 405 BCE. A gap of almost a century is not a detail, and
it shows how large the uncertainty is.

**What is a matter of faith.** Many Buddhists attribute superhuman status
and powers to the buddhas, and the tradition speaks of other buddhas before
and after Gautama. Academic research stays agnostic about these claims. So
does this book: it neither treats them as historical facts nor dismisses them
as ornament. They are part of what the tradition teaches, and they should be
read as teaching.

There is a structural reason for the uncertainty. The Buddha's teachings
reached us in texts that **were written down only several centuries after
his death**, in languages (Pali, and Chinese translations from Sanskrit) that
were probably not the one he spoke, and that disagree with one another on
important points. Before that, they were memorized and recited. Siderits
notes that oral transmission need not have been careless, but that the
differences between versions suggest insertions and cuts driven by disputes
between schools. When this book says "the Buddha taught," the exact meaning
is: "the surviving texts attribute to the Buddha."

## Dharma, Sangha and the Text Called a Sutta

Three words come up in every conversation about Buddhism.

- **Dharma** (in Pali, *Dhamma*) is the Buddha's teaching and, at the same
  time, the order of things that the teaching describes. When a Buddhist
  talks about "practicing the Dharma," they mean living in line with that
  description.
- **Sangha** means "community." In traditional usage, it refers to the
  monastic community: those who have renounced household life to live under
  the rules of the order. Buddha, Dharma and Sangha are called the "Three
  Refuges" or the "Three Jewels." The word comes back, in a practical sense,
  in chapter {{cap:uma-pratica-para-a-vida}}.
- **Sutta** is a discourse attributed to the Buddha or to a close disciple.
  The suttas of the Pali canon are gathered into collections, and this book
  cites them by the name of the collection and the number (SN 56.11, MN 10),
  as scholars do.

The Three Refuges already say something about this chapter's question. A
technique does not ask you to take refuge, and a philosophy rarely has a
monastic order. Buddhism has both.

## One Tradition, Many Schools

There is no single "Buddhism," just as there is no single "Christianity."
The Pew Research Center estimated about 488 million Buddhists in the world in
2010, close to 7% of the world's population. Almost all of them (nearly 99%)
live in the Asia-Pacific region, and half live in a single country, China.
Affiliation by school is almost never measured in censuses, but the division
into three major branches is the one most often used:

| Branch | Where it predominates (according to Pew) | A distinctive trait |
|---|---|---|
| **Theravāda** | Thailand, Myanmar, Sri Lanka, Laos, Cambodia | preserves the Pali canon |
| **Mahāyāna** | China, Japan, South Korea, Vietnam | its own scriptures, the *bodhisattva* path, emptiness |
| **Vajrayāna** | Tibet, Nepal, Bhutan, Mongolia | Tibetan Buddhism; sometimes counted within the Mahāyāna |

In plain words:

- **Theravāda** is the school that kept the suttas in Pali, and most of the
  texts quoted in the coming chapters come from it. This is a choice of
  convenience (the Pali canon is old and has public-domain translations),
  not a judgment that it is "the original Buddhism."
- **Mahāyāna** refers to a broad family of schools with their own
  scriptures, among them the *Perfection of Wisdom* literature. Two ideas
  mark it: the gradual path of the *bodhisattva*, which runs from the
  aspiration to awakening to its realization, and emptiness, the claim that
  nothing has a nature of its own, independent of everything else. Pew notes
  that the Mahāyāna is probably the largest of the three branches.
- **Vajrayāna** is the branch concentrated in Tibet and the surrounding
  regions, which is why it is also called Tibetan Buddhism. Some scholars
  count it as part of the Mahāyāna rather than as a third branch.
- **Zen** is a Mahāyāna school. The name comes from the Chinese *Chán*,
  which in turn transliterates the Sanskrit *dhyāna*, "meditation." The
  school chose that name because, according to the tradition, the Buddha
  himself awakened through meditation. Being the school "of meditation" does
  not make it a technique: Zen has revered masters and a vast literature of
  its own.

The schools do not differ only in emphasis. They disagree about which texts
have authority, about what awakening is and about how to reach it. Chapter
{{cap:impermanencia-e-nao-eu}} shows one of these disagreements from the
inside. When a book says "Buddhism teaches that...," the right question is
always: which Buddhism, in which text, read by whom?

## Religion, Philosophy or Technique?

The Sri Lankan monk and scholar Walpola Rahula, author of a classic
introduction, *What the Buddha Taught* (1959), answered the question this
way: "The question has often been asked: Is Buddhism a religion or a
philosophy? It does not matter what you call it." The answer has an obvious
appeal, but it hides what most Buddhists actually do.

For most Buddhists, historically, religious life has been made up of rites,
chanting, devotion and acts that earn merit, with goals such as ethical
cultivation and a good rebirth; monks, besides practicing, served as priests
and ritualists. Meditation was long considered a monastic practice, hard to
pursue under the conditions of lay life. That is the reading of the
historian David McMahan, whose *The Making of Buddhist Modernism* (2008) is
this chapter's reference on the subject.

So this book's answer is: **Buddhism is a religion**, in the full sense of
the word, and within it are sophisticated philosophies and highly developed
techniques of mental training. Taking only the techniques is possible and can
be useful. But it is taking a part, and it has to be said that it is a part.

## The "Scientific" Buddha

If Buddhism is a religion, where does the idea, so common today, come from
that it is "a science of the mind" that "anticipated neuroscience"? The idea
has a history, and three authors have told it well.

**McMahan: Buddhist modernism.** **Buddhist modernism** is the name scholars
give to the forms of Buddhism that emerged from the mid-nineteenth century
on, in the encounter between Asia and the West. According to McMahan, they
were shaped by the pull of modernity: rationalism and scientific naturalism,
which led people to set the mythologies aside; the Romantic and psychological
heritage, which values the inner life; and a process that privatized
meditation and detached it from institutions. Raising meditation above rites,
chanting and devotion, McMahan notes, **was not simply a Western product**:
Asian reformers led it. The historian Robert Sharf (2015) sums up the
movement's central trait: the notion that Buddhism is a rational, empirical
and therapeutically oriented tradition, compatible with modern science. From
the 1970s on, the scholars Richard Gombrich and Gananath Obeyesekere gave one
strand of it the name "Protestant Buddhism."

**Lopez: a history full of twists.** The historian Donald Lopez, in
*Buddhism and Science* (2008), shows that the debate over whether Buddhism
and science are compatible began in encounters with Christian missionaries
in Asia. One of the first was in 1552, when the Jesuit Francis Xavier
criticized the Japanese for not knowing that the sun orbits the earth; the
missionary's "science" was wrong. In 1873, in a debate with a Methodist
minister, a Sri Lankan monk argued that Mount Meru, the mountain at the
center of traditional Buddhist cosmology, did exist, even though it did not
appear on European maps; the proof was that compasses always point north.
Around the turn of the twentieth century, Buddhism was also drawn into the
racial science of the time: in 1914, the Sinhalese Buddhist Anagarika
Dharmapala called the Buddha "the great Aryan Savior." And the line about
Buddhism as "the religion of the future," attributed to Einstein and repeated
to this day, is something he never said.

Lopez's lesson is not that Buddhism is anti-scientific. It is that the claim
"Buddhism is compatible with science" has been made many times, for varied
reasons (defense against missionaries, nationalism, prestige), and that each
generation has remade it with whatever science it had at hand.

**Thompson: exceptionalism.** The philosopher Evan Thompson, who is
sympathetic to Buddhism and who co-wrote, in 1991, one of the founding books
of the dialogue between cognitive science and Buddhism (*The Embodied Mind*,
with Francisco Varela and Eleanor Rosch), uses the term **Buddhist
exceptionalism** for the belief that Buddhism is superior to other religions
because it is inherently rational and empirical, or that it is not a
religion at all but a "mind science" or a therapy. In *Why I Am Not a
Buddhist* (2020), he argues that this belief confuses two kinds of concept:
Buddhist concepts are *normative* (they say what one should seek and how),
while scientific ones are *descriptive*. Thompson traces the idea of
Buddhism as a "mind science" to the writings of a twentieth-century German
monk, Nyanaponika Thera, who appears again in chapter
{{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}}. For Thompson, the critique
does not lessen the value of Buddhism. It lessens the value of selling it as
science.

Rahula himself is a good example of the phenomenon. His book is useful and
is still read today, but it presents Buddhism as rational and in tune with
science, which also makes it a document of Buddhist modernism rather than a
neutral look at the tradition.

## What This Changes for This Book

This book is itself a modern and selective reading. It will give meditation
more room than most Buddhists do, it will read the Four Noble Truths as
psychology, and it will compare 2,000-year-old texts with laboratory
experiments. That is legitimate as a use, as long as three safeguards are
kept:

1. **Say what is left out.** Rebirth, karma, cosmology, rites and devotion
   are part of Buddhism. When this book sets them aside, it says so.
2. **Keep three registers apart.** What a Buddhist text *claims*, what
   practitioners *report* and what experiments *show* are different things.
   A claim can be true in one register and unsupported in another. Each
   chapter says which one it is in.
3. **Do not use science as a seal of authenticity.** If an experiment
   converges with a Buddhist idea, that is interesting. It does not prove
   that "the Buddha was right," and a Buddhist idea does not need an
   experiment to be taken seriously as an idea.

Chapter {{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}} comes back to this
point from the side of practice: what happens to meditation when it leaves
the whole it was part of.

## What This Chapter Established

- **Buddha** is a title ("awakened"). The historical Gautama lived in
  northern India; his dates are contested (between c. 560–480 BCE and a death
  around 405 BCE), and the texts that preserve his teaching were written
  centuries later.
- **Dharma** is the teaching and the order it describes; **Sangha** is the
  community; a **sutta** is a discourse from the canon. Buddha, Dharma and
  Sangha are the Three Refuges.
- Buddhism is diverse: **Theravāda**, **Mahāyāna** (including **Zen**) and
  **Vajrayāna** disagree about texts, goals and methods. About 488 million
  people in 2010, almost all of them in Asia.
- It is a **religion** in the full sense, with philosophies and techniques
  inside it; for most Buddhists, intensive meditation was not the center of
  religious life.
- "Scientific" Buddhism is a product of **Buddhist modernism**, from the
  mid-nineteenth century on. The thesis that Buddhism is a science of the
  mind has a history, motives and serious critics.
- This book makes a modern selection and says that it does.
