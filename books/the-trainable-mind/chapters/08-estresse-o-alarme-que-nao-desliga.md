This chapter explains how a thought reaches the body. The path has three
steps: the body has an alarm system built for physical emergencies; that
alarm also goes off in response to threats that exist only in the head (an
evaluation, a memory, a worry); and when it stays on for too long, it takes
a physical toll. This is where the second arrow, introduced in chapter
{{cap:as-quatro-nobres-verdades-como-diagnostico}}, gets a physiology. At the
end, the chapter separates what is well known (the physiology of stress)
from what is still a promise (the effect of meditation on hormones,
inflammation and cellular aging) and from a very popular theory that the
experts reject.

## The Alarm and What It's For

The **stress response** is the set of changes the body makes when it
detects a threat: the heart speeds up, breathing changes, sugar and fat are
released into the blood to fuel the muscles, and functions that can wait
(digestion, tissue repair, reproduction) are set aside. It is an ancient
system, shared with other mammals, and it is very good at what it does.

The biologist Robert Sapolsky summed up the idea in the title of a book:
*Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers* (Sapolsky 2004). A zebra chased by a lion
turns on the alarm, runs, and, if it escapes, the alarm switches off. The
emergency lasts minutes. The problem for human beings, Sapolsky argues, is
not acute stress. It is that we can turn on the same system for weeks,
because of things that are not happening right now: a debt, a meeting on
Thursday, what someone might have thought of us.

In other words: the alarm is not the defect. The defect is that it doesn't
switch off.

## Two Systems, Two Speeds

The alarm uses two pathways, one fast and one slow.

The fast pathway is the **autonomic nervous system**: the part of the
nervous system that regulates, without your deciding, the heart, the blood
vessels, breathing, digestion and the glands. It has two branches that work
like an accelerator and a brake:

- the **sympathetic** branch, the accelerator, which prepares the body to
  act, and which tells the inner part of the adrenal glands (two small
  glands sitting on top of the kidneys) to release adrenaline;
- the **parasympathetic** branch, the brake, which favors rest, digestion
  and recovery. The main nerve of this branch is the **vagus nerve**, which
  runs from the brainstem to the heart, the lungs and the gut.

The slow pathway is hormonal. The **HPA axis** is the chain of command
among three structures: the hypothalamus (a region at the base of the
brain), the pituitary (a gland just below it) and the adrenals; the
abbreviation comes from *hypothalamus*, *pituitary*, *adrenal*. The
hypothalamus signals the pituitary, which signals the adrenals, which
release **cortisol**: the main stress hormone in humans. Cortisol mobilizes
energy, adjusts the immune system and, under normal conditions, helps
switch off the alarm itself, because the brain detects its level and scales
back the command once it rises high enough.

| | Fast pathway | Slow pathway |
|---|---|---|
| System | Autonomic nervous system (sympathetic branch) | HPA axis |
| Main messenger | Adrenaline and nerves | Cortisol |
| Speed | Seconds | Minutes to hours |
| Example | Heart pounding when you brake suddenly | Tension that lasts all afternoon after an argument |

This physiology is among the best-established facts of human biology. The
details are in any textbook, and Sapolsky's synthesis is still the best way
in, even though some points in it have aged. **Strength of evidence:
strong.**

## An Imagined Threat Sets Off a Real Alarm

Now the part that answers the chapter's question. If the alarm went off
only in the presence of lions, thoughts would do the body no harm. But it
goes off in response to *psychological* threats, and this has been
measured.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Dickerson and Kemeny, published in *Psychological Bulletin*
in 2004. A meta-analysis of 208 laboratory studies in which people were
exposed to acute psychological stressors (usually performance tasks under
some kind of pressure) and had their cortisol measured.

**What it found.** Psychological stressors raised cortisol, but the effect
varied widely across tasks. The largest cortisol responses, and the slowest
recoveries, came from tasks that combined two things: lack of control over
the outcome and **social-evaluative threat**, the possibility of being
judged negatively by others. The authors conclude that cortisol does not
respond equally to every stressor.

**Strength of evidence:** strong for "psychological threat raises
cortisol"; moderate for the specific explanation (control and evaluation),
which is the authors' interpretation drawn from studies that differ greatly
from one another.
:::

The result matters for a precise reason: no one in these tasks was in
physical danger. What switched on the HPA axis was an appraisal: "I might
fail, and they'll see." The alarm doesn't distinguish well between a
predator and a disapproving look.

The second step is time. In 2006, the psychologists Jos Brosschot, William
Gerin and Julian Thayer proposed the **perseverative cognition
hypothesis**: worry and rumination (repetitive thinking about what went
wrong, described in chapter {{cap:a-mente-que-divaga}}) prolong the body's
activation before and after the stressful event. The meeting lasts an hour;
the worry about it starts three days before and ends two days after. Their
review brings together studies in which worry, rumination and anticipatory
stress are associated with greater cardiovascular, hormonal and immune
activity. The authors themselves call the support **preliminary**: much of
the data is correlational, and showing that rumination *causes* disease,
rather than merely accompanying the activation, is much harder.

This is a good place to keep the registers apart, as this book promises to
do:

- **What the Buddhist text claims:** the ordinary person, struck by pain,
  adds a second arrow: distress, lament, resistance.
- **What experiments show:** appraisals and thoughts about a threat are
  enough to trigger the hormonal stress response, and thinking about the
  threat over and over seems to prolong it.

The two claims are not the same. The sutta speaks of mental suffering; the
physiology speaks of cortisol and heart rate. But they point in the same
direction: much of the cost of a bad event comes from what the mind does
with it afterward. That doesn't prove Buddhism. It shows that the
distinction between the two arrows has a measurable correlate.

## When the Alarm Won't Switch Off: Allostatic Load

The neuroendocrinologist Bruce McEwen gave the cost a name. In 1998, in a
review article in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, he described two
faces of the same stress mediators (cortisol, adrenaline, immune signals):

- **Allostasis** is the body's ability to maintain stability *by changing*:
  raising blood pressure when you stand up, releasing energy when you need
  to run. It is protective.
- **Allostatic load** is the accumulated wear and tear when these responses
  are triggered too often or don't switch off once the threat has passed.
  It is the price of using the emergency system as the normal operating
  mode.

The idea is simple and useful: the same hormone that saves you in an
emergency does damage when it stays high for months. Chronic stress is
associated with cardiovascular, metabolic and immune problems, and
allostatic load is the framework that links those associations to a
mechanism.

**Strength of evidence:** strong as a theoretical framework. Weaker in the
details: the indices used to measure allostatic load in a person vary from
study to study, and there is no single number that says "your load is X."

## Interoception: The Body Sensed from Within

So far, the flow has run from the top down: the thought turns on the alarm,
the alarm changes the body. But the flow also runs the other way. The brain
constantly receives information about the state of the body: heartbeat,
breathing, muscle tension, stomach, temperature. **Interoception** is this
sensing of the body's internal state, and much of it is unconscious.

The neuroscientist Lisa Feldman Barrett argues that the brain doesn't just
*receive* these signals: it *predicts* them, and what we feel is largely
the prediction, corrected by the signals that arrive (Barrett & Simmons
2015). In her book *How Emotions Are Made* (Barrett 2017), she goes further
and proposes that emotions are constructed by the brain out of these
predictions about the body, learned concepts and culture, rather than
triggered by ready-made circuits for "fear" or "anger." **This theory is
one side of an open debate**: researchers in the "basic emotions" tradition
disagree with it, and the 2015 paper is explicitly an opinion article that
proposes a model. Treat it as a serious hypothesis, not an established
fact. **Strength of evidence: contested.**

Why does this matter for meditation? Because several practices, such as the
body scan and attention on the breath, train precisely this attention to
internal signals. If the theory is right, sensing the body more accurately
could change how the alarm is interpreted: a racing heart read as "I'm in
danger" or as "my heart is racing." It is a plausible hypothesis, not a
result. Chapter {{cap:o-corpo-o-sono-e-a-dor}} comes back to it.

## Polyvagal Theory: Popular, and Rejected by Physiology

Anyone who searches the internet for meditation, yoga or body-based therapy
soon runs into **polyvagal theory**, proposed by the psychiatrist Stephen
Porges (Porges 2011). It claims that the vagus nerve has two branches with
distinct evolutionary roles: a "ventral" one, tied to safety and social
connection, and a "dorsal" one, tied to paralysis and shutdown. From
this comes a widely used vocabulary ("activate the ventral vagus," "dorsal
state") and the promise that certain breathing techniques or practices
"put you in safety mode."

The problem is that **the theory's physiological premises are rejected by
most experts on the autonomic nervous system**. In 2023, the
psychophysiologist Paul Grossman examined the theory's five basic premises
and argued that each one is untenable or highly implausible. One example:
the theory treats the variation of heart rate with breathing (respiratory
sinus arrhythmia) as something unique to mammals and as a direct measure of
"vagal tone," and both, according to Grossman, are wrong; the second
confuses a rough indicator with the phenomenon it indicates. In 2026, an
international assessment signed by dozens of experts reached the same
conclusion (Grossman et al. 2026).

This doesn't mean that slow breathing doesn't calm you down, or that the
vagus nerve plays no part in regulating the heart. It does. It means that
the polyvagal *explanation* doesn't hold up, and that phrases like "this
practice activates your ventral vagus" are marketing vocabulary, not
physiology. **Strength of evidence: contested, and largely refuted in its
physiological premises.**

## What Meditation Does to the Alarm

The natural question is: if thought turns on the alarm, does training your
relationship with thoughts turn it off? The honest answer is that the data
are promising, uneven and still **preliminary**.

**In favor.** A 2017 meta-analysis (Pascoe and colleagues) pooled 45
randomized trials that compared meditation with an active control, the kind
of comparison whose importance chapter {{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}
explains. With all forms of meditation taken together, the authors
found reductions in cortisol, systolic blood pressure, heart rate,
triglycerides and two markers of inflammation (C-reactive protein and
TNF-alpha). It is a result in favor, with a limit: an average over 45
studies of very different kinds of meditation, populations and measures
says little about what a specific practice does in a specific person, and
the next study shows how much that matters.

**The complication.** One of the largest longitudinal studies of secular
mental training, the *ReSource Project*, carried out in Leipzig and Berlin,
measured how 313 people reacted to a standardized social stress test over
nine months of training (Engert et al. 2017). All three types of practice
reduced the stress participants *reported*. But only the modules centered
on social skills (compassion and perspective taking) reduced cortisol
release, by up to 51%. The module on attention to the present moment and
the body, the one most like ordinary mindfulness programs, changed the
report, not the hormone. The autonomic and immune markers measured did not
change with any type of practice.

The two results don't cancel each other out, but they show that
"meditation lowers cortisol" is too big a sentence. It depends on the type
of practice, the type of measure, and who is being compared with whom. And
"meditation" is not a single intervention, a point that comes back in
chapter {{cap:bondade-e-compaixao-como-treino}}.

**Telomeres and inflammation.** Telomeres are the protective caps at the
ends of chromosomes; their shortening accompanies cellular aging and has
been linked to chronic stress. The idea that meditation could protect them
comes from a 2009 review article whose title is a question: *Can meditation
slow rate of cellular aging?* (Epel et al. 2009). The article proposes a
hypothesis; it tests none. Later reviews say that the mechanisms, and the
conditions under which any effect would occur, remain poorly specified
(Conklin et al. 2019). On the immune system, the first systematic review of
randomized trials found great heterogeneity across studies and results
that the authors themselves call **tentative**, pending replication (Black
& Slavich 2016). Headlines like "meditation rejuvenates your cells" are
many steps ahead of the data. **Strength of evidence: preliminary.**

Blood pressure deserves separate treatment, because there is a medical
guideline on it; chapter {{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}
shows what it says.

A health caveat, stated plainly: severe chronic stress, anxiety that gets in
the way of your life, persistent insomnia and unexplained physical symptoms
are reasons to see a health professional. Meditation can be part of care;
it is not a substitute for treatment.

## What This Chapter Established

- The **stress response** is an emergency alarm with two pathways: the
  **autonomic nervous system** (fast, with a sympathetic accelerator and a
  parasympathetic brake) and the **HPA axis**, which releases **cortisol**
  (slow).
- The alarm goes off in response to psychological threats: a meta-analysis
  of 208 studies shows that social evaluation and lack of control raise
  cortisol with no physical danger at all. Thinking about the threat over
  and over seems to prolong the activation (preliminary).
- The cost comes from duration: **allostatic load** is the wear and tear of
  an emergency system used as a routine.
- **Interoception** is the sensing of the body's internal state. The idea
  that the brain *predicts* the body, and that emotions are constructed from
  those predictions, is an influential and contested theory.
- **Polyvagal theory** is popular, but its physiological premises are
  rejected by most experts.
- The effect of meditation on cortisol, inflammation and telomeres is
  **preliminary**: there are signs in favor, there is a large study in which
  attention practice changed reported stress and not the hormone, and there
  are many headlines ahead of the data.
- The second arrow of the Buddhist text and the prolonged activation
  measured in the lab point in the same direction. That is convergence, not
  proof.
