This chapter explains how a thought reaches the body. The path has three
steps:

1. The body has an alarm built for physical emergencies.
2. That alarm also goes off in response to threats that exist only in the
   head: an evaluation, a memory, a worry.
3. When it stays on for too long, it takes a physical toll.

This is where the second arrow, introduced in chapter
{{cap:as-quatro-nobres-verdades-como-diagnostico}}, gets a physiology. At the
end, the chapter separates what is well known (the physiology of stress) from
what is still a promise (the effect of meditation on hormones, inflammation
and cellular aging). It also examines a very popular theory that the experts
reject.

## The Alarm and What It's For

The **stress response** is the set of changes the body makes when it detects
a threat. The heart speeds up, breathing changes, sugar and fat go into the
blood to fuel the muscles. Functions that can wait, such as digestion, tissue
repair and reproduction, are set aside. It is an ancient system, shared with
other mammals, and very good at what it does.

The biologist Robert Sapolsky summed up the idea in the title of a book: *Why
Zebras Don't Get Ulcers* (Sapolsky 2004). A zebra chased by a lion turns on
the alarm and runs. If it escapes, the alarm switches off. The emergency
lasts minutes.

The problem for human beings, Sapolsky argues, is not acute stress. It is
that we can keep the same system on for weeks, over things that are not
happening right now: a debt, a meeting on Thursday, what someone might have
thought of us.

In other words: the alarm is not the defect. The defect is that it doesn't
switch off.

## Two Systems, Two Speeds

The alarm uses two pathways, one fast and one slow.

The fast pathway is the **autonomic nervous system**: the part of the nervous
system that regulates, without your deciding, the heart, the blood vessels,
breathing, digestion and the glands. It has two branches, which work like an
accelerator and a brake:

- the **sympathetic** branch, the accelerator, prepares the body to act. It
  makes the adrenal glands (two small glands sitting on top of the kidneys)
  release adrenaline;
- the **parasympathetic** branch, the brake, favors rest, digestion and
  recovery. Its main nerve is the **vagus nerve**, which runs from the
  brainstem to the heart, the lungs and the gut.

The slow pathway is hormonal. The **HPA axis** is the chain of command among
three structures: the hypothalamus (a region at the base of the brain), the
pituitary (a gland just below it) and the adrenals. The abbreviation comes
from *hypothalamus*, *pituitary*, *adrenal*.

The hypothalamus signals the pituitary, which signals the adrenals, which
release **cortisol**: the main stress hormone in humans. Cortisol mobilizes
energy and adjusts the immune system. Under normal conditions, it also helps
switch off the alarm itself: the brain notices that the level has risen high
enough and scales back the command.

| | Fast pathway | Slow pathway |
|---|---|---|
| System | Autonomic nervous system (sympathetic branch) | HPA axis |
| Main messenger | Adrenaline and nerves | Cortisol |
| Speed | Seconds | Minutes to hours |
| Example | Heart pounding when you brake suddenly | Tension that lasts all afternoon after an argument |

**Strength of evidence: strong.** This physiology is among the
best-established facts of human biology. The details are in any textbook.
Sapolsky's synthesis is still the best way in, even though some points in it
have aged.

## An Imagined Threat Sets Off a Real Alarm

Now the part that answers the chapter's question. If the alarm went off only
in the presence of lions, thoughts would do the body no harm. But it goes off
in response to *psychological* threats, and this has been measured.
**Strength of evidence: strong.**

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Dickerson and Kemeny (2004) pooled 208 laboratory studies. In
them, people did stressful tasks, usually performance under pressure, and had
their cortisol measured.

**What it found.** The tasks raised cortisol, but the effect varied widely.
The largest responses, and the slowest recoveries, came from tasks that
combined two things: lack of control over the outcome and **social-evaluative
threat**, the chance of being judged badly by others. Cortisol does not
respond equally to every stressor.

**Strength of evidence:** strong for "psychological threat raises cortisol."
Moderate for the explanation (control and evaluation), which is the authors'
interpretation drawn from studies that differ greatly from one another.
:::

The decisive point: no one in these tasks was in physical danger. What
switched on the HPA axis was an appraisal: "I might fail, and they'll see."
The alarm doesn't distinguish well between a predator and a disapproving
look.

The second step is time. Worry and rumination (repetitive thinking about what
went wrong, described in chapter {{cap:a-mente-que-divaga}}) prolong the
body's activation before and after the event. The meeting lasts an hour. The
worry about it starts three days before and ends two days after.

This hypothesis was proposed in 2006, with support the authors themselves
called preliminary (Brosschot, Gerin & Thayer 2006). Ten years later, a
meta-analysis of 60 studies in healthy people measured the link (Ottaviani
and colleagues, 2016). Thinking over and over about a threat went with higher
blood pressure, heart rate and cortisol. The effects were small to moderate.

The same work found lower **heart rate variability**. This is the natural
variation in the interval between one heartbeat and the next. It is used as a
marker of autonomic nervous system function and is associated with physical
and mental health.

- **In the short term**, thinking over and over about a threat keeps the body
  activated. **Strength of evidence: moderate**: many studies, including
  experiments, point the same way.
- **In the long term**, it is not yet known whether rumination *causes*
  disease. The long-term data are almost all correlational, and people who
  ruminate more may differ from those who ruminate less in many other ways.
  **Strength of evidence: preliminary.**

This is a good place to keep the registers apart, as this book promises to
do:

- **What the Buddhist text claims:** the ordinary person, struck by pain,
  adds a second arrow: distress, lament, resistance.
- **What experiments show:** thoughts about a threat are enough to trigger
  the hormonal stress response, and thinking them over and over prolongs it.

The two claims are not the same. The sutta speaks of mental suffering; the
physiology speaks of cortisol and heart rate. But they point in the same
direction: much of the cost of a bad event comes from what the mind does with
it afterward. That doesn't prove Buddhism. It shows that the distinction
between the two arrows has a measurable correlate.

## When the Alarm Won't Switch Off: Allostatic Load

The neuroendocrinologist Bruce McEwen gave the cost a name, in a 1998
article. He described two faces of the same stress mediators (cortisol,
adrenaline, immune signals):

- **Allostasis** is the body's ability to maintain stability *by changing*:
  raising blood pressure when you stand up, releasing energy when you need to
  run. It is protective.
- **Allostatic load** is the accumulated wear and tear when these responses
  are triggered too often, or don't switch off once the threat has passed. It
  is the price of using the emergency system as the normal operating mode.

The idea is simple and useful: the same hormone that saves you in an
emergency does damage when it stays high for months. Chronic stress is
associated with cardiovascular, metabolic and immune problems. Allostatic
load is the framework that links those associations to a mechanism.
**Strength of evidence: strong**, as a theoretical framework.

The framework also makes a prediction that can be measured, and it holds up
in part. **Strength of evidence: moderate** for "high allostatic load goes
with higher mortality." In a meta-analysis of follow-up studies, people with
high load had a 22% higher risk of dying from any cause (Parker and
colleagues, 2022).

Two caveats change how to read that number:

- It is an association, not proof of cause.
- The studies varied a great deal, partly because each one measures the load
  its own way. There is still no single number, agreed on by everyone, that
  says "your load is X."

## Interoception: The Body Sensed from Within

So far, the flow has run from the top down: the thought turns on the alarm,
the alarm changes the body. But the flow also runs the other way. The brain
constantly receives information about the body: heartbeat, breathing, muscle
tension, stomach, temperature. **Interoception** is this sensing of the
body's internal state, and much of it is unconscious.

The neuroscientist Lisa Feldman Barrett argues that the brain doesn't just
*receive* these signals: it *predicts* them. What we feel would largely be
the prediction, corrected by the signals that arrive (Barrett & Simmons
2015). That paper is explicitly an opinion article: it proposes a model; it
doesn't test it.

In her book *How Emotions Are Made* (Barrett 2017), she goes further. She
proposes that the brain constructs emotions out of these predictions about
the body, learned concepts and culture, rather than triggering them through
ready-made circuits for "fear" or "anger." **Strength of evidence:
contested.** This theory is one side of an open debate: researchers in the
"basic emotions" tradition disagree with it. Treat it as a serious
hypothesis, not an established fact.

Why does this matter for meditation? Because several practices, such as the
body scan and attention on the breath, train attention to internal signals.
If the theory is right, sensing the body more accurately could change how the
alarm is interpreted. A racing heart could be read as "I'm in danger" or
simply as "my heart is racing."

But the idea that practice makes this perception more *accurate* has not
held up. **Strength of evidence: contested.** It is a clear case of the
difference between what practitioners report and what experiments show.
Meditators *report* sensing their bodies better.

In one experiment, experienced meditators and nonmeditators tried to detect
their own heartbeat, including with the heart sped up by a drug similar to
adrenaline (Khalsa and colleagues, 2020). The meditators did no better. The
experiment is small, and the weight rests on the meta-analysis in the same
paper: with more than 700 participants, it also found little evidence of a
difference.

If practice changes something here, it may be how the signal is interpreted,
not its accuracy. That has not yet been measured convincingly. Chapter
{{cap:o-corpo-o-sono-e-a-dor}} comes back to the subject.

## Polyvagal Theory: Popular, and Rejected by Physiology

**Strength of evidence: contested, and largely refuted in its physiological
premises.**

Anyone who searches the internet for meditation, yoga or body-based therapy
soon runs into **polyvagal theory**, proposed by the psychiatrist Stephen
Porges (Porges 2011). It is popular, and, as we'll see, physiology rejects
its premises. It claims that the vagus nerve has two branches with distinct
evolutionary roles:

- a "ventral" one, tied to safety and social connection;
- a "dorsal" one, tied to paralysis and shutdown.

From this comes a widely used vocabulary, such as "activate the ventral
vagus" or "dorsal state." And from it comes the promise that certain
breathing techniques or practices "put you in safety mode."

The problem is that the theory's physiological premises are rejected by a
broad group of experts on the autonomic nervous system. In 2023, the
psychophysiologist Paul Grossman examined the five basic premises and argued
that each one is untenable or highly implausible (Grossman 2023). One
example: the theory treats one part of heart rate variability as a direct
measure of vagal activity. For Grossman, this confuses a rough indicator with
the phenomenon it indicates.

In 2026, Grossman and 38 other experts on the physiology and evolution of
the vagus nerve went further (Grossman et al. 2026). Many of them had been
cited by the polyvagal literature itself as support. Their conclusion: the
theory is untenable in light of what is known today about neurophysiology
and evolution.

Porges replied that the critique attacks a reconstructed version of the
theory (Porges 2026). The reply is conceptual: it brings no new data against
the anatomical objections.

This doesn't mean that slow breathing doesn't calm you down, or that the
vagus nerve plays no part in regulating the heart. It does. It means that the
polyvagal *explanation* doesn't hold up. Phrases like "this practice
activates your ventral vagus" are marketing vocabulary, not physiology.

## What Meditation Does to the Alarm

The natural question: if thought turns on the alarm, does training your
relationship with thoughts turn it off? The short answer:

- people who meditate *report* less stress, quite consistently;
- the body follows only a little, and unevenly;
- it is not yet known whether these small changes make a difference to
  health.

Marker by marker, the picture is this:

| Marker | What the research shows | Strength of evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Cortisol | Small-to-moderate but uneven average reduction; depends on the practice, the measure and who practices | preliminary |
| Inflammation | Perhaps a small reduction; the most cautious analysis finds almost nothing | preliminary |
| Heart rate variability | No detectable effect at rest | preliminary |
| Telomeres | An 18-month trial found no protection | contested |
| Epigenetic clock | No difference in a comparative study; a trial is still missing | preliminary |

## Cortisol

**Strength of evidence: preliminary.** The average reduction is small to moderate, shows
up more in people who are ill or under heavy stress, and does not show up in
every study.

Syntheses of randomized trials find, on average, a small-to-moderate reduction in
cortisol in people without a diagnosis (Rogerson and colleagues, 2024). In
trials of MBSR alone, it still shows up, smaller, against active controls,
the comparison whose importance chapter {{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}
explains (Johnson and colleagues, 2026). One author of that synthesis
develops mindfulness programs, and lower cortisol is not, by itself, better
health.

The result depends on who practices and how it is measured. In blood, the
reduction appeared only in people with a physical illness. In saliva, there was
no detectable effect (Koncz and colleagues, 2021).

And the broadest synthesis found no effect on the body. With more than
23,000 employees, it saw improvements in reported stress, anxiety and sleep.
But it saw no detectable difference in cortisol, blood pressure, heart rate
variability or inflammation (Khanal and colleagues, 2026).

Part of the contradiction comes from the measure. Cortisol in saliva and
blood shows the level at one moment. Cortisol in hair reflects the exposure
accumulated over the previous months. A single project shows the difference.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** The *ReSource Project*, in Leipzig and Berlin, one of the
largest studies of secular mental training. Up to nine months of near-daily
practice, in three-month modules: attention to the present moment and the
body (the one most like ordinary mindfulness programs), compassion, and
perspective taking. Engert and colleagues (2017) tested 313 people in a
standardized social stress test: the acute *reaction*. Puhlmann and
colleagues (2021) measured cortisol in hair: the *exposure* over months.
Participants knew they were training (an open-label trial), and a comparison
group only took the tests.

**What it found.** In the acute reaction, all three modules reduced
*reported* stress. Only the social modules reduced cortisol release, by up to
51%. The attention module changed the report, not the hormone, and no
practice changed the autonomic and immune markers. In hair, cortisol fell
over the first three to six months in every group that trained, including
one that started with the attention module. It stayed stable in the group
with no training. It fell more in those who practiced more often, and it did
not track the change in reported stress.

**Strength of evidence:** preliminary. A single project and research group,
with healthy people. The type of practice seems to matter for the acute
reaction, perhaps not for long-term exposure. Whether the drop lasts, or
improves health, is not known.
:::

"Meditation lowers cortisol," then, is too big a sentence. It depends on the
type of practice, the measure, and who is being compared with whom. And
"meditation" is not a single intervention, a point that comes back in chapter
{{cap:bondade-e-compaixao-como-treino}}.

## Variability, Inflammation, Aging

**Heart rate variability. Strength of evidence: preliminary**: for now, there
is no evidence that meditation increases it. Much of what is said about
meditation and "vagal tone" assumes that it does. A meta-analysis of 19
randomized trials found no effect on resting variability (Brown and
colleagues, 2021). The apparent effect, already indistinguishable from chance, fell by half when a single study
with an extreme result was removed.

**Inflammation. Strength of evidence: preliminary**: there may be a small
reduction. The first review of trials called its own results tentative, and
asked for them to be repeated (Black & Slavich 2016). A later analysis
pooled more than a hundred trials of psychological therapies. For
mindfulness, it found a small-to-moderate reduction, with studies that
differed widely (Ballesio and colleagues, 2023). The most cautious one, with its
analysis plan registered in advance, found a very small reduction, at the
edge of chance, and signs of publication bias (Grasmann and colleagues,
2023).

**Telomeres. Strength of evidence: contested**: in a direct test, meditation
did not protect them. **Telomeres** are the protective caps at the ends of
chromosomes. Their shortening accompanies cellular aging and has been linked
to chronic stress.

The idea that meditation could protect them comes from a 2009 article whose
title is a question: *Can meditation slow rate of cellular aging?* (Epel et
al. 2009). The article proposes a hypothesis; it tests none. The signals in
favor come from few studies, most of them not randomized: a 2021 review found
only five (Dasanayaka and colleagues, 2021).

The direct test came later, in a secondary analysis of the Age-Well trial
(Kaliman and colleagues, 2025). People aged 65 or older were assigned by lot to three groups: 18
months of meditation, 18 months of foreign-language classes, or no activity.
There was no difference between the groups in telomere length.

**Epigenetic clock. Strength of evidence: preliminary.** The epigenetic clock
is biological age estimated from chemical marks on DNA. In a comparative
study, it did not differ, on average, between meditators and nonmeditators
(Chaix and colleagues, 2017). The meditators were not assigned by lot to
meditate, and a randomized trial is still missing.

Headlines like "meditation rejuvenates your cells" are many steps ahead of
the data.

## On Balance

It is well established that people who meditate report less stress. Whether
the body follows is much less clear. This is not yet known because:

- the studies are mostly small and short;
- they measure different things under the same name;
- none has followed people long enough to see whether the small biological
  changes translate into less disease.

A health caveat, stated plainly: intense chronic stress, anxiety that gets in
the way of life, persistent insomnia and unexplained physical symptoms are
reasons to see a health professional. Meditation can be part of care; it is
not a substitute for treatment.

## What This Chapter Established

- The **stress response** is an emergency alarm with two pathways: the
  **autonomic nervous system** (fast, with a sympathetic accelerator and a
  parasympathetic brake) and the **HPA axis**, which releases **cortisol**
  (slow).
- The alarm goes off in response to psychological threats: social
  evaluation and lack of control raise cortisol with no physical danger at
  all (strong). Thinking over and over about the threat keeps the body
  activated in the short term (moderate); whether this causes disease over
  the years is not yet known (preliminary).
- The cost comes from duration: **allostatic load** is the wear and tear of
  an emergency system used as routine. High load goes with higher mortality
  (moderate), but there is still no standard measure.
- **Interoception** is the sensing of the body's internal state. The theory
  that emotions are constructed from predictions about the body is
  contested; and meditators do not detect their heartbeat more accurately
  (contested).
- **Polyvagal theory** is popular, but its physiological premises are
  rejected by a broad group of experts on the vagus nerve; its author
  disputes the critique, with no new data (contested).
- **Meditation and the body:** reported stress falls consistently; cortisol
  falls slightly to moderately and unevenly, and inflammation may fall slightly
  (preliminary); there is no demonstrated effect on **heart rate
  variability** (preliminary); an 18-month trial on **telomeres** found no
  protection (contested).
- **What is not yet known:** whether the small biological changes last,
  whether they improve health, and which type of practice produces them.
- The second arrow of the Buddhist text and the prolonged activation
  measured in the laboratory point in the same direction. That is
  convergence, not proof.
