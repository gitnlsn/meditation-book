This chapter closes the book with a practical question: how do you make a
meditation practice outlast the enthusiasm of the first few weeks? The answer
it argues for is that motivation doesn't sustain anything for long; what
sustains a practice is structure — a fixed time and place, people to
practice with, someone to guide you, and a reason you understand. The
chapter shows what is known about each of these pieces, how to choose
between a program, a tradition or a group, what precautions to take with
retreats, and ends with two things: an eight-week plan for you to put
together and a summary of the argument of the whole book.

## Why Motivation Isn't Enough

The idea that habits form through willpower is intuitive and, according to
habit research, misplaced. A habit, as defined in
{{cap:atencao-emocao-e-habito}}, is a response the context triggers almost
without a decision. The psychologist Wendy Wood, one of the leading
researchers on the subject, sums up the literature in a book for general
readers (*Good Habits, Bad Habits*, 2019): habits are associations between a
context and a response, and changing your environment usually works better
than relying on willpower. If you depend on "feeling like it," you decide
all over again, every day, whether to practice — and one day you decide not
to. If you always practice after coffee, in the same chair, you have less to
decide.

The study by Lally and colleagues (2010), presented with its limits in
{{cap:atencao-emocao-e-habito}}, adds two useful pieces of information: the
time it took for a daily behavior to become automatic ranged from **18 to
254 days** — there is no magic number — and **missing a day did not
meaningfully disrupt** habit formation.

The practical upshot: instead of asking "how do I stay motivated?", ask
"what in my day is going to trigger the practice?"

## The Four Pieces of Structure

### Time and Place

Pick a moment that already exists in your routine and tie the practice to
it: after brushing your teeth in the morning, when you get home, before bed.
Use the same place and, if you can, the same cushion or chair. Start with a
length you can manage on a bad day — five or ten minutes — not the one you
would like to manage on a good day. A short daily practice builds the
context; a long, sporadic one doesn't.

### Community: The Sangha in Practice

The chapter {{cap:quem-foi-o-buda-e-quem-ele-nao-foi}} defined the
**Sangha** as the community — in traditional usage, the monastic community.
**In practice**, for a beginner, sangha means something simpler: a group you
meditate with regularly — a neighborhood meditation group, a class, a center
connected to a tradition, even two or three people who agree to practice
together over video call.

A group helps in three ways. It creates a schedule that doesn't depend on
your remembering. It makes it visible that everyone has a wandering mind,
which eases the feeling that you are doing it wrong. And it offers a place
to talk about difficulties, which the previous chapter showed to be
important.

The direct evidence on "practicing in a group" is less solid than the
intuition. Two findings point in the same direction. In a meta-analysis of
individual data from 13 trials and 2,371 adults, Galante and colleagues
(2023) found that group-based, in-person, instructor-led mindfulness
programs reduced psychological distress compared with passive controls, with
a small to moderate effect size
({{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}) — which does not
isolate the effect of the group, but it is the format in which the evidence
exists. And in the ReSource Project, a nine-month German study with 362
participants, Kok and Singer (2017) tested practices done in pairs and found
engagement similar to that of solitary meditation and an increase in the
sense of social closeness. Strength: **moderate to preliminary**.

### Guidance: A Teacher

A **teacher**, here, is anyone with training and experience who follows your
practice: a certified MBSR or MBCT instructor, an authorized teacher within
a Buddhist tradition, a psychologist trained in mindfulness-based
interventions. In Buddhist traditions, the relationship with the teacher is
usually central and takes very different forms from one school to another,
almost none of them resembling an eight-week course. For a beginner, the
minimum role is to answer questions, correct misunderstandings, and
recognize when something isn't going well.

This book can't give a seal of approval, but it can give criteria for
prudence. A good sign is a teacher who:

1. says clearly what their training is and in which lineage or program they
   learned;
2. talks openly about difficulties and adverse effects, and knows where to
   refer someone when the practice destabilizes them;
3. doesn't promise a cure, quick enlightenment or guaranteed transformation;
4. doesn't ask you to stop a treatment;
5. doesn't create dependence, secrecy, or pressure for money, time or
   loyalty.

The opposite signs are a reason to look elsewhere. In Lindahl and
colleagues' study of difficult experiences, relationships — including the
one with the teacher — appear among the factors that influenced how those
experiences unfolded ({{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}}).

### A Reason You Understand

The fourth piece is the one this book has tried to provide. A practice held
up only by promises ("it will change your brain," "it will end your
anxiety") doesn't survive the first month in which the promise isn't kept. A
practice held up by understanding — I know what I'm training, I know what
the evidence says and what it doesn't, I know why coming back to the breath
is the repetition that counts — can last through weeks with no visible
result, because it doesn't depend on visible results.

## Program, Tradition or Group: How to Choose

There are three main paths, and they are not mutually exclusive.

| Path | What it is | Who it makes sense for | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **MBSR** | An eight-week course, with weekly group sessions and practice at home | People who want a secular, structured format with an instructor | Check the instructor's training |
| **MBCT** | The same format, combined with cognitive therapy | People who have had recurrent depression and want to reduce the risk of relapse — that is where the evidence is | Not a treatment for an acute crisis; done with professional follow-up |
| **Buddhist tradition** (Theravāda, Zen, Tibetan and other centers) | Practice within the ethical, philosophical and often religious context it came from | People who want the whole path, not just the technique | Each tradition has its own assumptions; ask what they are beforehand |

MBSR and MBCT were defined in {{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}.
The difference between them and a tradition is not one of quality but of
purpose. The programs take meditation out of the whole it was born in and
use it for health goals; the chapter
{{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}} showed that, in the
tradition, meditation is one of three parts of a training that includes
ethics and wisdom. Neither choice is the "true" version for everyone. It is
honest, though, to know which of the two you have chosen.

Apps and books (this one included) are useful for getting started and for
keeping up a routine. Most of the clinical evidence this book has cited,
however, comes from instructor-led programs, not from solitary practice with
an app.

## Retreats, with Caution

A **retreat** is a period away from your routine devoted to practice: it can
be a morning, a full day, a weekend or several weeks. The intensive retreat,
lasting many days with many hours of practice a day, was defined in
{{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}}. Short retreats are an excellent way to
deepen your practice and get to know a group. Long ones are something else.

Three cautions:

1. **Go from short to long.** Do a day before a weekend, a weekend before a
   week. In Schlosser and colleagues' survey (2019), having attended
   retreats was associated with more unpleasant experiences — an
   association, not proof of cause, but a good reason not to start with the
   most intense option.
2. **Disclose your history.** A careful retreat should ask about mental
   health before accepting someone. If it doesn't ask, that in itself tells
   you something.
   A history of trauma, psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe depression is
   a reason to talk with whoever is treating you before you go.
3. **Know how to leave.** Before you start, find out whom to talk to if
   something goes wrong and whether you can scale back the practice or
   leave. A retreat that treats the wish to stop purely as resistance to be
   overcome is making the minimizing mistake.

## What to Expect in the Long Run

Here the book has to be especially honest. The psychologist Daniel Goleman
and the neuroscientist Richard Davidson, in *Altered Traits* (2017), propose
a useful distinction: there are **states** — what changes during and right
after practice — and **traits** — what changes in a person in a lasting
way. The caveat comes with it: it is a popular book written by a lab
director about his own field, and it was published before the large 2022
trial from Davidson's own lab (Kral and colleagues), which found no
structural change in the brain after eight weeks of MBSR
({{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}).

The central problem with studies of long-term practitioners is **selection
bias**. Someone who has meditated for twenty years was not randomly assigned
to meditate. They chose to, liked it, kept going — and may have been
different from the start: calmer, more patient, with more free time, with
other habits. Comparing these people with people who have never meditated
shows that the groups differ, not that meditation caused the difference.
Strength of evidence for long-term effects: **preliminary**.

What this means for you: practice for what the evidence shows over weeks
and months — a little less stress and anxiety, for many people — and for
what you observe yourself, not for the promise of becoming someone else.

## Your Eight-Week Plan

Eight weeks is the length of the most studied clinical programs, and it is
enough time to try each practice in the book and find out which one works
for you. Put the plan together like this:

1. **Choose the trigger:** the moment of the day that will set off the
   practice.
2. **Choose the place** and have it ready.
3. **Write down the minimum length** you can manage on a bad day.
4. **Choose a simple record:** an X on the calendar, a line in a notebook.
   Also write down one sentence about what you noticed.
5. **Make a plan with someone** — a friend, a group, a course — for at least
   one practice a week in company.
6. **Decide in advance what to do on a missed day:** nothing. Pick it up
   again the next day. Missing a day doesn't undo the habit.

A suggested sequence, which runs through the practices of Part III:

| Week | Main practice | Daily length | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Attention on the breath, counting | 10 min | {{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}} |
| 2 | Breath, without counting | 10–15 min | {{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}} |
| 3 | Noting ("thinking," "hearing," "planning") | 10–15 min | {{cap:observar-sem-agarrar}} |
| 4 | Noting + noticing the second arrow during the day | 15 min | {{cap:as-quatro-nobres-verdades-como-diagnostico}} |
| 5 | Mettā in four circles | 10–15 min | {{cap:bondade-e-compaixao-como-treino}} |
| 6 | Body scan | 15–20 min | {{cap:o-corpo-o-sono-e-a-dor}} |
| 7 | The practice that worked best | 15–20 min | — |
| 8 | The same, plus one group practice or a one-day retreat | 15–20 min | — |

At the end of the eighth week, answer three questions in writing: Which
practice am I going to keep? At what time? With whom? If at any point the
signs described in {{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}} appear, the plan
changes — shorten it, switch practices, or stop and seek guidance. And if
you are in treatment for any condition, this plan goes alongside it; it
does not replace it.

## The Book's Argument, in Brief

1. **Buddhism is a living, diverse religious tradition**, not an ancient
   science of the mind. The "scientific" version that reached the West is a
   modern, selective reading of it.
2. **Its central teaching has the form of a diagnosis:** there is
   unsatisfactoriness (dukkha), it has a cause (thirst, taṇhā), it can
   cease, and there is a path. The second arrow — the reaction we add to
   pain — is the mechanism in miniature.
3. **In the tradition, meditation is one part of a larger training**, along
   with ethics and wisdom. Clinical mindfulness is an adaptation, legitimate
   and different.
4. **Knowing whether something works requires comparison with an active
   control, randomization and pooling of studies.** Nearly every
   exaggeration about meditation comes from skipping one of these steps.
5. **The adult brain changes with use**, but less, more slowly and in ways
   harder to measure than is claimed. "Eight weeks rewire the brain" has not
   held up.
6. **The mind wanders a lot, the stress alarm responds to imagined threats,
   and attention, emotion and habit are trainable.** That is what meditation
   trains, not "the brain" in general.
7. **On the clinical balance, the evidence is moderate:** real and modest
   benefits for anxiety, stress and preventing depressive relapse, the same
   size as those of other active treatments, not larger.
8. **The basic practice is coming back.** Noticing that you have drifted and
   returning is the repetition that trains — with the breath, with noting,
   with kindness, with the body.
9. **Meditation can do harm**, in a minority of cases, and knowing how to
   recognize the signs is part of practicing.
10. **What sustains a practice is structure and understanding**, not
    motivation.

Where tradition and science agree — the separation between pain and the
reaction to pain, attention as something trainable, the weight of habit —
the evidence is enough to justify the practice. Where they don't agree, or
where the science is weak, the book has said so at the point where the claim
appeared. That is the reason it offers for sitting down: not a promise, but
an understanding.

## What This Chapter Established

- Habits form through repetition in a stable context, not through willpower
  (moderate); the time it takes varies widely, from weeks to many months,
  and missing a day doesn't undo the process (preliminary, Lally).
- The structure that sustains a practice has four pieces: **time and
  place**, **community** (the sangha in practice), **guidance** from a
  qualified teacher, and **a reason you understand**.
- MBSR and MBCT are secular eight-week programs; Buddhist traditions offer
  the complete path. The choice is one of purpose, not quality.
- **Retreats** go from short to long, with your health history disclosed and
  a way out known in advance.
- Long-term effects in experienced practitioners are **preliminary**: people
  who have meditated for decades selected themselves.
- The eight-week plan runs through the book's practices and ends with a
  choice: which practice, at what time, with whom.
