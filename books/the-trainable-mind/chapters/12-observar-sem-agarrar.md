This chapter teaches the second basic practice and answers a question the
word "mindfulness" tends to hide: what, exactly, do you do when you are
"being mindful"? The answer, in the sense of the Buddhist text the idea comes
from, is this: you observe whatever shows up — sensations, emotions,
thoughts — as events that arise and pass, without following them and
without pushing them away. The technique taught here is **noting**, giving a
brief name to each thing that shows up. The chapter shows where it comes
from, what science has found about putting feelings into words, why
observing is not the same as suppressing, and for whom this is not the best
way in.

## The Text: Four Places to Look

Chapter {{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}} introduced the
*Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta* (discourse number 10 in the collection of
middle-length discourses) and the term *satipaṭṭhāna*. Here what matters is
the practical content. The text organizes attention into **four
foundations** — four fields of experience it can be directed toward:

1. **The body**: the breath, the postures (walking, standing, sitting, lying
   down), the activities of the day, the parts of the body.
2. **Feelings**, in the technical sense of *vedanā*: not "emotions," but the
   tone of each experience — pleasant, unpleasant or neutral. It is the
   fastest layer of reaction, earlier than liking or disliking in words.
3. **The mind**: the overall state it is in — with greed or without, with
   hate or without, scattered or concentrated.
4. ***Dhammas***, which Sujato translates as "principles": patterns the
   tradition considers decisive, such as the obstacles to practice (sensual
   desire, ill will, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and remorse,
   doubt) and the factors that lead to awakening.

The way the text describes each foundation is what matters for practice. In
Bhikkhu Sujato's translation:

> It's when a mendicant who feels a pleasant feeling knows: 'I feel a
> pleasant feeling.' When they feel a painful feeling, they know: 'I feel a
> painful feeling.'

And about the mind, the practitioner "understands mind with greed as 'mind
with greed,'" and knows "scattered mind as 'scattered mind.'" There is no
instruction to change the state. There is an instruction to recognize it for
what it is.

Each section of the discourse ends with the same refrain, and this chapter
takes its title from it: the practitioner observes things originating and
vanishing, and they "meditate independent, not grasping at anything in the
world." At the start of the text, attention is described as "rid of
covetousness and displeasure for the world" — the two directions the mind
tends to run in the face of any experience: pulling it closer or pushing it
away.

## Noting: A Twentieth-Century Method

The sutta says *what* to recognize. It doesn't say *how* to train that
recognition minute by minute. One of the most influential answers to that
question came from Burma (now Myanmar), in the twentieth century, with the
monk Mahasi Sayadaw. His method is **noting**: silently naming, with a
simple word, whatever is happening at the moment it happens.

In the manual Mahasi published in Burmese, which came out in English in
1957, the instructions are concrete. The anchor is the movement of the
abdomen, noted as "rising" and "falling." When the mind wanders off, you
note it: "wandering, wandering, wandering." Thoughts are noted as
"thinking"; and also as "reflecting," "planning," "knowing," as the case may
be. Hearing is "hearing, hearing." And there are two caveats that almost
always get lost:

- the labels **are not spoken** aloud;
- "it is more important to know the object than to know it by a term or
  name."

The label, then, is a support, like the counting in
chapter {{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}}: it serves to make attention explicit, not to
replace it with words.

A historical note is worth making, because this book promises not to
flatten the tradition: much of what the West learned as "mindfulness" passed
through these Burmese reform movements, and the historian Robert Sharf
argues that the idea of "bare attention," free of concepts (the story is in
chapter {{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}}), is a modern emphasis, and a
debatable one, as a reading of older Theravāda doctrine. It is an
influential thesis, not a consensus. Noting is a legitimate tool, tested by
generations of practitioners — but it is an interpretation of the text, not
the text.

## Equanimity: Neither Following Nor Pushing Away

The attitude the practice cultivates has a name: **equanimity**. It is the
capacity to let an experience be present without pulling it in or pushing it
away. The *Ānāpānasati Sutta* describes the practitioner this way: "Having
seen with wisdom the giving up of covetousness and displeasure, they watch
over closely with equanimity."

Equanimity is not indifference. The indifferent person doesn't notice, or
doesn't care. The equanimous person notices clearly — anger is here, it is
strong, it is heating up my face — and does not add the second arrow
described in chapter {{cap:as-quatro-nobres-verdades-como-diagnostico}}: the
commentary, the justification, the fight with the anger.
Chapter {{cap:bondade-e-compaixao-como-treino}} presents equanimity as one of four
qualities the tradition deliberately trains, with its own Pali name.

## The Practice

Ten minutes, ideally after a few days of chapter {{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}},
because noting assumes you already know how to come back to an anchor.

1. **Sit** as in the breath practice: spine upright without stiffness, an
   alarm set for ten minutes.
2. **Start with the anchor** — the breath in the belly or at the nostrils —
   for a minute or two, silently noting "rising, falling" or "in, out."
3. **When something pulls your attention**, note it with a single word, at
   the moment you notice: "hearing" for a sound, "thinking" for a thought,
   "planning" for a plan, "remembering" for a memory, "itching," "tension,"
   "irritation," "boredom."
4. **Note it once, in a neutral tone**, like someone jotting down the
   license plate of a passing car. Don't investigate the content.
   "Planning" is enough; you don't need to know what about.
5. **Go back to the anchor** after the label. If the same thing is still
   there, note it again.
6. **If you don't know which label to use**, use "this" or "knowing." An
   imprecise label at the right moment is worth more than a precise one that
   comes late.
7. **Include the tone**, when you can: "pleasant," "unpleasant," "neutral."
   It is the sutta's second foundation, and it is often the most revealing.

After a few days, many people notice that the labels repeat — "planning,"
"planning," "judging" — and that this repetition shows the mind's habits
more clearly than hours of reflection. They also notice that a thought that
has been noted often loses force. It isn't a rule, and it isn't the goal;
but it is common.

## Observing Is Not Suppressing

This is the most dangerous misunderstanding about the practice. Noting
"anger" is not saying "I shouldn't feel anger." It is the opposite: it is
admitting, precisely, that anger is here.

Suppression is trying not to feel or not to think — closing the door.
Observation is leaving the door open and looking at who comes in. The two
can look calm from the outside, and that is why they get confused. The
difference shows in the inner tone: someone who suppresses notes in a hurry,
to get rid of the thing; someone who observes notes with curiosity, in no
hurry for the thing to end. If you notice you are using the label as a
broom, note that too: "pushing."

## What the Science Says

Changing register, from the text to the experiments: there are two findings
that matter here.

**Putting feelings into words reduces part of the reaction: preliminary
evidence.** The lab of the psychologist Matthew Lieberman studies what it
calls *affect labeling*. In the first imaging study, naming the emotion in a
negative photo reduced the response of the amygdala (a region linked to
threat detection, among other functions, met in chapter
{{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}) compared with other ways of
processing the same image, and increased activity in a region of the
prefrontal cortex. A later study with 39 older adults found that labeling
and deliberately reappraising the situation reduced amygdala activity to a
similar degree, with correlated drops in reported distress. The curious
point is that labeling is not meant to change the emotion, and it changes it
anyway.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Katharina Kircanski, Matthew Lieberman and Michelle Craske,
2012. Eighty-eight people with an intense fear of spiders were randomly
assigned to four groups of 22, and each person was repeatedly exposed to a
live tarantula. During the exposure, one group described out loud what they
felt ("I feel anxious the disgusting tarantula will jump on me"); another
reframed the situation in a less threatening way; another distracted
themselves; the fourth did the exposure alone.

**What it found.** A week later, facing a different spider in a different
place, the group that had labeled showed a smaller skin conductance response
(a physiological sign of arousal) than the other groups, and got slightly
closer to the spider than the distraction group. But it **did not report
less fear** than the others. And those who used more fear and anxiety words
during the exposure had a greater reduction in their response.

**Strength of evidence:** preliminary. A single study, with small groups,
in people afraid of spiders — not in meditators. The split between body and
report (less physiological reaction, the same declared fear) is a reminder
that "reducing reactivity" can mean different things depending on what is
measured.
:::

There is a leap here that needs naming. *Affect labeling* in the lab means
naming an emotion in response to a stimulus, usually once, in words. Noting
in meditation is continuous, silent, and covers everything — sounds,
thoughts, itches — not just emotions. Lieberman's studies make it plausible
that meditative noting helps regulate emotions. They did not test the
practice itself.

**Open monitoring and focused attention are different practices: moderate
evidence.** Chapter {{cap:atencao-emocao-e-habito}} defined the two terms, following
Lutz and colleagues (2008). The breath practice is focused attention: one
object, and back to it. Noting sits between the two: there is an anchor, but
attention opens to whatever shows up and registers it without reacting — the
core of open monitoring. The distinction is well accepted, and one of the
largest longitudinal studies of secular meditative training, the Max Planck
Institute's ReSource Project, concluded, in the words of the title of a 2019
synthesis, that "it matters what you practice": different practices trained
different capacities. The caveat is the usual one: the studies that separate
the two styles in the brain are, for the most part, small.

## Where Tradition and Science Part Ways

Science asks whether noting *reduces reactivity*. The sutta has a different
aim: to see that everything that shows up arises and passes away, and that
none of it is worth grasping — the subject of
chapter {{cap:impermanencia-e-nao-eu}}. For the tradition, feeling calmer is a
welcome side effect; the target is an understanding. No experiment cited
here measures that understanding.

There is also a divergence within the tradition itself. Mahasi insists that
the name matters less than knowing the object; Lieberman's research suggests
that it is precisely the word that produces part of the effect. Both can be
true at once, but they are not the same claim.

## When This Isn't the Way In

Opening attention to whatever shows up means that what is difficult shows up
too. For most people, this is tolerable and even useful. For some —
especially people with a history of trauma, dissociation or panic attacks —
it can bring up memories, sensations or states of disconnection that they
cannot regulate on their own.

The research supports caution. In a survey of 1,232 regular meditators,
about a quarter reported particularly unpleasant experiences they attributed
to meditation. They were more likely among those who practiced only
vipassanā-type styles — the family noting belongs to —, among those with a
lot of repetitive negative thinking, and among those who had been on a
retreat. It is a self-report study, with a single question, and it does not
establish cause (preliminary evidence). In an eight-week program, lasting
bad effects, in a minority, were associated with signs of dysregulated
arousal and dissociation. Both studies are covered in detail in
chapter {{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}}.

So, three practical guidelines:

- **Keep the anchor strong.** If noting opens up more than you can keep up
  with, go back to just the breath or, if that is uncomfortable too, to your
  feet on the floor, with your eyes open.
- **Shorten it.** Three minutes done well are worth more than ten of
  distress.
- **Stop and get help** if flashbacks show up, or a persistent sense of
  unreality or of being outside your body, or distress that doesn't pass
  after the session. Chapter {{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}} describes these
  signs. For people with trauma, a practice guided by a trained professional
  is a better way in than practicing alone — and no meditation is a
  substitute for treatment.

## What This Chapter Established

- In the *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta*, attention is directed to **four
  foundations**: the body, feelings (*vedanā*, the pleasant, unpleasant or
  neutral tone), states of mind, and *dhammas*. The instruction is to
  recognize each thing as it is and not grasp at anything.
- **Noting** is a twentieth-century method, systematized by Mahasi Sayadaw:
  one silent word per event. Mahasi himself says that knowing the object
  matters more than the name.
- **Equanimity** is letting experience be present without pulling or
  pushing. It is not indifference, and **observing is not suppressing**.
- Naming emotions reduces part of the emotional response in the lab
  (**preliminary**), but those studies did not test meditative noting.
- Open monitoring and focused attention train different things
  (**moderate**).
- The tradition aims at understanding impermanence; science measures
  reactivity.
- For people with trauma or dissociation, full openness may not be the best
  way in.
