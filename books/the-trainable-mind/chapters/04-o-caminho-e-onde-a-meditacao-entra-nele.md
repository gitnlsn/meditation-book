This chapter answers the question "Is meditating the same as being a
Buddhist?" by showing where meditation sits on the tradition's own map. The
answer, in brief:

- In the texts, meditation is one part of a larger training, which has three
  parts: ethics, concentration and wisdom.
- The word most tied to it today, *mindfulness*, translates a Pali term with
  a broader and more value-laden meaning than the modern version.
- Clinical mindfulness is an openly acknowledged adaptation. And the Buddhist
  discourse most often cited as a "manifesto of the scientific method" says
  something else.

## The Path in Eight Parts

The fourth of the Four Noble Truths, covered in chapter
{{cap:as-quatro-nobres-verdades-como-diagnostico}}, is a path. The **Noble
Eightfold Path** is the list of eight factors that, according to the texts,
lead to the end of dukkha. A short discourse, the *Magga-vibhaṅga Sutta* ("the
analysis of the path," *Saṃyutta Nikāya* 45.8), defines each one. In brief:

| Factor | What the text says it is |
|---|---|
| Right view | knowing suffering, its origin, its cessation and the path |
| Right intention | the intention of renunciation, of good will and of harmlessness |
| Right speech | refraining from lying, divisive speech, harsh speech and idle chatter |
| Right action | refraining from killing, stealing and unchastity |
| Right livelihood | giving up wrong ways of making a living and earning a living rightly |
| Right effort | preventing and abandoning harmful mental states; cultivating and sustaining beneficial ones |
| Right attention (*sammā sati*) | observing the body, feelings, the mind and phenomena, with diligence and clarity |
| Right concentration (*sammā samādhi*) | the four states of meditative absorption |

"Right" translates *sammā*. The corresponding Sanskrit term also means
"complete" or "whole." So some scholars consider "right" a poor translation. The idea is not to obey a rule but to do the thing fully.

Notice the proportions. Of the eight factors, only the last two (and, in
part, effort) describe what we now call meditation. The others are about
understanding the problem, about intentions, speech, action and work.

## Three Trainings: Sīla, Samādhi, Paññā

A dialogue in the canon, the *Cūḷavedalla Sutta* (*Majjhima Nikāya* 44),
groups the eight factors into three sets. The one asking is a layman; the
one answering is a nun, Dhammadinnā. At the end, the Buddha says
he would have answered exactly as she did. Her division is this:

- **Sīla** — ethics, conduct. It includes right speech, right action and
  right livelihood. It is the training of what you do and what you say.
- **Samādhi** — concentration, or, in Sujato's translation, "immersion." It
  includes right effort, right attention and right concentration. It is the
  training of the mind: stability, focus, unification. Dhammadinnā defines
  samādhi as "unification of the mind."
- **Paññā** — wisdom, discernment. It includes right view and right
  intention. It is the training of seeing things as the texts say they are:
  impermanent, unsatisfactory, without a fixed self (chapter
  {{cap:impermanencia-e-nao-eu}}).

![The Noble Eightfold Path's eight factors in the three trainings, as Dhammadinnā divides them in the *Cūḷavedalla Sutta*. The numbers follow the order of the list.](../illustrations/figures/fig-caminho-en.svg)

The same three-part division reached Japan. Zen philosophy inherits from
Indian Buddhism the idea that Buddhist study has three components: ethical
precepts, meditation and wisdom.

The three are not stand-alone options: the texts present them as parts of a
single path. The historian Robert Sharf (2015) says what, from a more
traditional Buddhist perspective, the modern mindfulness movement is
missing. It is missing precisely this gradual transformation, which involves
doctrine and a Buddhist way of life. Not because meditation on its own does
nothing. But because, within this framework, it stops being the same
training.

## Two Directions of Meditation: Samatha and Vipassanā

Within the training of the mind, the texts distinguish two aspects.

- **Samatha** is calm, serenity. It is the development of the mind's
  stability, linked in the texts to the states of absorption
  described under right concentration.
- **Vipassanā** is penetrating vision, *insight*. It is the development of a
  direct understanding of impermanence, unsatisfactoriness and not-self.

The monk and scholar Bhikkhu Anālayo wrote the standard commentary on the
*Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta* (2003), the discourse this chapter presents below. He
argues that in the suttas the two aspects are complementary. A concentrated
mind supports *insight*, and *insight* makes deeper concentration easier. Some scholars have read the two as separate
paths with different goals; Anālayo considers that reading mistaken.

This distinction helps make sense of a twentieth-century innovation that the
chapter returns to below. Some schools claimed you could reach *insight*
without going through the absorptions of calm.

## Sati: The Word That Became "Mindfulness"

The seventh factor of the path is *sammā sati*. Here is the word that, once
translated into English, gave its name to a global movement.

**Sati** is a Pali term related to the verb "to remember"; its Sanskrit
equivalent also means memory. In the suttas, *sati* sometimes appears in the
literal sense of recalling. A canonical definition, quoted by Sharf,
describes someone who has sati as "one who remembers, who recollects what was
done and said long before."

Anālayo reads this definition carefully. For him, sati is not exactly
memory. It is the quality that lets memory work well: a presence of mind
that keeps something in view. In the context of meditation, this becomes attention to
what is happening now, *without losing sight of* what matters.

**Mindfulness** is the English word used to translate *sati*. Today it also
names a set of secular practices and programs. The best-known definition is
that of the researcher Jon Kabat-Zinn. He created the stress-reduction
program that chapter {{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}
discusses:

> the awareness that emerges through paying attention on purpose, in the
> present moment, and nonjudgmentally to the unfolding of experience moment
> by moment.

The two, sati in the suttas and mindfulness in Kabat-Zinn's definition,
overlap, but they are not the same. The difference lies in the word
"nonjudgmentally."

## What Satipaṭṭhāna Is

The central discourse on sati is the *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta* (*Majjhima
Nikāya* 10). **Satipaṭṭhāna** combines *sati* and *upaṭṭhāna*, "being
present," "attending." Anālayo proposes translating it as "presence of
mindfulness" or "attending with mindfulness." Later commentaries read the
term as "foundation of mindfulness," but for Anālayo that origin of the word
is unlikely. In English, the term usually
appears as the "foundations of mindfulness."

The sutta opens by stating that the four *satipaṭṭhānas* are "the direct
path" (or "the path to convergence," in Sujato's translation) to four ends:

- the purification of beings;
- getting past sorrow and lamentation;
- ending pain and sadness;
- realizing nirvana.

The four fields of observation are the body, feelings, the mind and
phenomena (*dhammas*). Chapter {{cap:observar-sem-agarrar}} treats them as
practice.

Three details of the text help show the distance between it and modern
mindfulness:

1. **Observing the body is not just the breath.** The sutta describes six
   exercises for the body: the breath, the postures, awareness of what you
   are doing, the parts of the body, the elements and the corpse.
   - In the parts, the practitioner goes over their own body, from the feet
     to the head, naming 31 of them: hair, bones, kidneys, feces, pus, blood,
     among others. The text compares this examination to opening a bag of
     grains and recognizing each grain.
   - In the corpse, they compare their own body to a dead body left in the
     open, through the stages of decay: "This body is also of that same
     nature, that same kind, and cannot go beyond that."

   The text says nothing about relaxing. The refrain says the practitioner
   meditates "independent, not grasping at anything in the world." Anālayo
   reads the goal as a balanced and detached attitude toward the body, one
   that weakens desire and is a reminder of death. For him, the image of the
   grains asks for neutrality, not disgust. He also notes that the texts
   themselves record the risk. Monks who took the exercise to excess,
   overcome by revulsion at their own bodies, went as far as suicide.
2. **Observing phenomena is doctrinal.** The practitioner observes, among
   other things, the five aggregates, the Four Noble Truths and the five
   hindrances (sensual desire, ill will, dullness and drowsiness,
   restlessness and remorse, doubt). To do that, you need to know the
   categories of the teaching.
3. **The goal is awakening.** The sutta ends with a promise. Whoever
   develops the four satipaṭṭhānas for seven years (and, counting down, even
   for seven days) can expect awakening in this very life or the state of
   "non-return." It is a claim the text makes about liberation, not about
   stress.

## "Bare Attention": Where Modern Mindfulness Came From

If the sati of the suttas is loaded with doctrine and values, where did the
idea of a neutral, nonjudgmental attention come from? Sharf (2015) tells the
story.

In twentieth-century Myanmar, teachers from a reform lineage
developed *insight* meditation techniques designed for laypeople as well.
The most influential was Mahāsi Sayadaw (1904–1982). He taught people to
follow, moment by moment, whatever object arose in experience. And he claimed
something radical for the time: liberating *insight* did not first require
mastering concentration (*samatha*) or the absorptions.

The method did not require knowing Buddhist philosophy or giving up lay
life. It could be taught in relatively short retreats. That made it easy to export.
One of Mahāsi's students, the German monk Nyanaponika Thera (1901–1994),
coined the expression *bare attention* for this faculty, in a popular book
from 1954.

Sharf points out the problem: there is little that is "bare" about sati in
the texts and the early commentaries. Citing another scholar of the texts, he
notes that sati involves discerning the moral value of what arises: knowing
whether a state is beneficial or harmful. The "nonjudgmental" of the modern
definition is, on this point, the opposite of what the text asks for.

That is why, for this book, the claim that *mindfulness* is a good
translation of *sati* is **contested**. It is a possible translation, but it
carries a specific modern interpretation.

Sharf also observes that similar innovations had appeared before: in
medieval Chinese Zen and in Dzogchen, a Tibetan meditation tradition. There
too, quick results were promised and practice was opened to laypeople
without doctrinal training. In every case, traditional quarters met them
with suspicion. Modern
mindfulness is not an unprecedented betrayal. It is one more chapter in an
old argument within Buddhism itself.

## Clinical Mindfulness Is an Adaptation, and Says So

Kabat-Zinn does not hide what he did. In the same 2003 article that gives the
definition, he writes that the program "needed to be free of the cultural,
religious, and ideological factors associated with the Buddhist origins of
mindfulness." The aim was not to teach Buddhism. It was to give patients a
setting in which to deal with stress, pain and illness. He also states that
attention, being a human capacity, is universal: "there is nothing
particularly Buddhist about it."

The two statements sit in tension. If attention is universal, the specific
way of training it still comes from a tradition. And what gets removed along
with the "cultural, religious, and ideological factors" includes the ethics,
the doctrine and the goal of the path. This does not invalidate the clinical
programs: what they do or don't do is a question of evidence, taken up in
Part II. It means that **clinical mindfulness is a practice lifted out of the
tradition and redesigned for another purpose**, and it should be called that.

## The Kālāma Sutta Is Not the Scientific Method

One discourse is often cited as proof that the Buddha preached scientific
skepticism: the *Kālāma Sutta* (*Aṅguttara Nikāya* 3.65). The people of a
town are confused. Many teachers pass through, each praising their own
doctrine and disparaging everyone else's. They ask whom they should believe. The Buddha answers:

> Please, Kālāmas, don't go by oral transmission, don't go by lineage, don't
> go by testament, don't go by canonical authority, don't rely on logic,
> don't rely on inference, don't go by reasoned train of thought, don't go by
> the acceptance of a view after deliberation, don't go by the appearance of
> competence, and don't think 'The ascetic is our respected teacher.' But
> when you know for yourselves: 'These things are unskillful, blameworthy,
> criticized by sensible people, and when you undertake them, they lead to
> harm and suffering', then you should give them up.

Read carefully, the text does not say what the slogan says, for three
reasons:

1. **It also rejects logic and inference**, which are central tools of
   science. The criterion is not "test the hypothesis"; it is direct
   knowledge of what certain kinds of conduct lead to.
2. **The subject is ethics, not the physical world.** The "things" to examine
   are greed, hatred and delusion, and what they lead people to do: kill,
   steal, lie. The sutta goes on to the cultivation of good will and
   compassion. And it ends with four "consolations" about what happens "if it
   turns out there is another world" after death.
3. **Individual judgment is not enough.** The criterion includes what is
   "criticized by sensible people." Thanissaro Bhikkhu, who translated the
   text, notes that it is not a carte blanche for following your own sense
   of right and wrong. Conclusions have to be tested in practice *and*
   checked against the experience of wise people.

The "scientific" reading has a history. Rahula, in *What the Buddha Taught*,
calls the advice to the Kālāmas "unique in the history of religions." His
paraphrase goes from the list of what not to follow straight to "you know
for yourselves that certain things are unwholesome (akusala), and wrong, and
bad." The clause about sensible people disappears. This is not
falsification. It is the kind of selection that chapter
{{cap:quem-foi-o-buda-e-quem-ele-nao-foi}} called Buddhist modernism.

## So, Is Meditating Being a Buddhist?

No. Meditating means using a tool that the Buddhist tradition developed with
great sophistication. As Kabat-Zinn himself points out, it has parallels in
other traditions. But within Buddhism, meditation is one part of a training.
That training includes ethical conduct, an understanding of the doctrine
and, for many, monastic life and devotion. And it has a goal, awakening,
that is not stress reduction.

In Part III, this book will treat meditation mainly as training in attention
and in how you relate to your own experience. In each chapter, it will say
what is lost and what is gained by that narrowing. The question "Does
it work?" is a legitimate one. It just isn't the question the tradition asks.

## What This Chapter Established

- The **Noble Eightfold Path** has eight factors; only two or three describe
  meditation.
- The factors group into three trainings: **sīla** (ethics), **samādhi**
  (concentration) and **paññā** (wisdom). In the texts, they support one
  another.
- **Samatha** (calm) and **vipassanā** (*insight*) are two complementary
  aspects of training the mind.
- **Sati** comes from "to remember" and involves keeping what matters in
  view, including the moral value of what arises. **Mindfulness** is its
  English translation. The modern reading of it as "bare attention" and
  "nonjudgmental" comes from twentieth-century Burmese reforms. Whether it is
  a good translation is **contested**.
- **Satipaṭṭhāna**, "presence of mindfulness," is the practice described in
  MN 10: body, feelings, mind and phenomena, with liberation as the goal.
- Clinical mindfulness is a declared **adaptation**, lifted out of the
  tradition.
- The **Kālāma Sutta** is about ethics, rejects logic too and includes the
  judgment of the wise. It is not a manifesto of modern empiricism.
