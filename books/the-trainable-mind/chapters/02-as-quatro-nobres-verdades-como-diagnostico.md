This chapter explains the central teaching of Buddhism, the Four Noble
Truths. It shows that this teaching has the structure of a diagnosis: a
problem, the cause of the problem, the claim that it can end, and a
treatment. It then presents an image the Buddha himself used, the second
arrow. It is the most direct bridge between what Buddhism says and what
neuroscience measures.
By the end, you should be able to say in your own words what problem Buddhism
claims to solve, and why the solution it proposes is not "positive thinking."

## The Text Where It All Starts

According to the tradition, the Buddha's first teaching after his awakening
was given to five ascetics in a deer park near Varanasi, in northern India.
The text that records it is called, in Pali, the *Dhammacakkappavattana
Sutta*: "the discourse that sets the wheel of the Dhamma in motion." *Sutta*
and *Dhamma* are the words defined in chapter
{{cap:quem-foi-o-buda-e-quem-ele-nao-foi}}: a discourse from the canon, and
the teaching. This sutta is in the collection called the *Saṃyutta Nikāya*
("linked discourses"), as number 56.11.

The discourse is short. It begins by rejecting two extremes, the pursuit of
sensual pleasure and ascetic self-torment, and proposes a "middle way." Then
it states four claims. In Bhikkhu Sujato's English translation, abridged:

1. **Now this is the noble truth of suffering.** Rebirth is suffering; old
   age is suffering; illness is suffering; death is suffering; being coupled
   with the disliked is suffering; separation from the liked is suffering;
   not getting what you wish for is suffering.
2. **Now this is the noble truth of the origin of suffering.** It's the
   craving that leads to future lives, mixed up with relishing and greed,
   taking pleasure there wherever it alights.
3. **Now this is the noble truth of the cessation of suffering.** It's the
   fading away and cessation of that very same craving … giving it away,
   letting it go, releasing it, and not clinging to it.
4. **Now this is the noble truth of the practice that leads to the cessation
   of suffering.** It is simply this noble eightfold path.

The text says one more thing that tends to be forgotten: each truth comes
with a task. Suffering is to be *completely understood*; its origin is to be
*given up*; its cessation is to be *realized*; the path is to be *developed*.
The four truths are not a creed to accept. They are a work program.

## Four Words That Translation Spoils

### Dukkha Is Not Just "Suffering"

**Dukkha** is the word that translations render as "suffering," and the
translation is the first obstacle. If dukkha were just pain, the first truth
would be either false or trivial: false because life has many good moments,
trivial because no one needs a Buddha to know that illness hurts.

The text itself shows that the meaning is wider. The list includes "not
getting what you wish for" and ends with a technical sentence: "In brief, the
five grasping aggregates are suffering," where the word is *dukkha*. The five
aggregates are the Buddhist analysis of what makes up a person: form (the
body), feeling, perception, formations and consciousness. Chapter
{{cap:impermanencia-e-nao-eu}} deals with them. What matters here is the
consequence: dukkha includes *pleasant* experiences, because they too change
and end, and we cling to them.

A more faithful translation would be **unsatisfactoriness**: the background
discomfort of living in a world where nothing we care about stays put. It is
not the claim that life is bad. It is the claim that no experience, however
good, satisfies once and for all, and that this has a cause.

### Taṇhā Is Not "Desire" in General

**Taṇhā** literally means "thirst." The text describes it in three forms:
thirst for sensual pleasures, thirst for continued existence, and thirst for
nonexistence. The last one tends to surprise people: the wish for something
to end, to disappear, not to feel, is also taṇhā.

What the three have in common is not wanting things. It is the *urgency* of
the wanting: the sense that this experience has to be different, now. Wanting
lunch is not taṇhā in the technical sense; needing lunch to be exactly what
you pictured, and suffering because it wasn't, is.

This distinction comes back in chapter {{cap:atencao-emocao-e-habito}},
when the book turns to reward and habit. For now, it is enough to notice that
Buddhism does not locate the cause of suffering in the world out there, but
in an internal response to the world.

### Nirodha and Nirvana: Cessation, Not Anesthesia

**Nirodha** means "cessation." The third truth claims that if the thirst
ends, the dukkha it produces ends too. The state in which this is complete is
called **nirvana** (in Pali, *nibbāna*). The root of the word is associated
with the image of a flame going out for lack of fuel.

Nirvana is not a paradise or a state of permanent ecstasy, and the Buddhist
schools disagree about how to describe it. For the purposes of this book, the
minimal reading is enough. There is, according to the tradition, a way of
living in which experiences keep happening, painful ones included, without
the mind grabbing them or pushing them away.

### Magga: The Path Is Practical

**Magga** means "path." The fourth truth does not say "believe"; it says
"do." The Noble Eightfold Path has eight elements: right view, right
intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right
attention (*sammā sati*) and right concentration. Only the last two, and in
part effort, correspond to what we now call meditation. Chapter
{{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}} explains why that matters.

## The Structure of a Diagnosis

Now the central idea of the chapter. Set the four truths side by side with a
physician's reasoning:

| Medicine | Four Noble Truths |
|---|---|
| There is a disease | There is dukkha |
| The disease has a cause | The cause of dukkha is taṇhā |
| The disease can be cured | Dukkha can cease (nirodha) |
| This is the treatment | The treatment is the path (magga) |

The comparison is not a modern invention. An early discourse, preserved in a
Chinese translation (*Saṃyukta-āgama* 389), says that a great physician
masters four skills:

- knowing the disease;
- knowing the origin of the disease;
- knowing the cure;
- knowing how to keep the disease from coming back.

The Buddha, the text says, is a physician of that kind, for suffering. The
same correspondence appears in two classic meditation manuals. In the Pali
canon there is no equivalent discourse, but the Buddha is often called a
physician.

There is an interesting historical question here, and it is still open. Some
scholars have supposed that the Buddha borrowed a diagnostic scheme that
already existed in Indian medicine. The problem is that **there is no record
of a four-part medical scheme earlier than Buddhism**; the scholars who have
looked for one have not found it. The monk and scholar Bhikkhu Anālayo (2011)
sees another possibility. For him, the discourse seems to assume the audience
already knew this way of reasoning, perhaps as a popular practice rather than
a written theory. Neither position can be proved from what has
survived.

For you, as a reader starting out, the useful conclusion does not depend on
this dispute. The scheme shows **what kind of teaching this is**. It is not
an account of how the universe came to be, nor a revelation to be accepted on
faith. It is an analysis of human suffering with a proposed treatment. It was made
in a setting full of philosophers debating metaphysics. And it is precisely
this choice of practical analysis that makes Buddhism comparable, in part, to
psychology and neuroscience.

The "in part" matters. One detail, stated honestly: the first sermon speaks
of "craving that leads to future lives," and the full version of the first
truth begins with "rebirth is suffering." Traditional Buddhism assumes
rebirth, and the ultimate goal of the path includes leaving the cycle of
rebirths. Modern Western readings, this one included, usually set that aside
and keep the psychological part. That is legitimate as a use, but it is not
what the text says. Chapter {{cap:quem-foi-o-buda-e-quem-ele-nao-foi}}
explains where this "secular" version comes from.

## The Second Arrow

The diagnosis of the four truths is abstract. The canon itself has a concrete
image that shows the mechanism at work, in a discourse called the *Sallatha
Sutta* ("the arrow," *Saṃyutta Nikāya* 36.6).

The text opens with a question: both the ordinary person and the experienced
practitioner feel pleasant, painful and neutral feelings. So what is the
difference between the two?

The answer, in Sujato's translation:

> When an unlearned ordinary person experiences painful physical feelings
> they sorrow and wail and lament, beating their breast and falling into
> confusion. They experience two feelings: physical and mental. It's like a
> person who is struck with an arrow, only to be struck with a second arrow.

The practitioner, the text says, feels the first arrow and not the second.
The physical pain is still there. What does not come is the layer of
resistance, distress and lament that the person adds on top.

The sutta says one more thing, and it is the subtlest part of the argument.
When the ordinary person is struck by pain, they **resist** it. Next, they
**look for sensual pleasure** as a way out, "because an unlearned ordinary
person doesn't understand any escape from painful feeling apart from sensual
pleasures."

The two reactions, pushing the pain away and running toward pleasure,
correspond to two of the three forms of taṇhā described in the second truth.
One is thirst for sensual pleasures. The other is thirst for something to
stop existing. The second arrow is taṇhā in action.

Notice what the text does *not* say. It does not say that the practitioner
feels no pain, or that they should pretend not to. The first arrow is
unavoidable: bodies hurt, people die, plans go wrong. The second is a
response, and responses can be trained.

## What Neuroscience Has to Say About the Two Arrows

Here the book changes register: it leaves what the text claims and moves to
what experiments have shown. The two are not the same, and the bridge between
them has to be crossed with care.

Pain research has distinguished two dimensions of the experience of pain for
decades:

- **Sensory intensity**: how much it hurts, where, what kind of pain it is.
- **Affective unpleasantness**: how bad it is to feel this pain; how much it
  bothers you.

They seem like the same thing, but they can be separated in the laboratory.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Rainville and colleagues, published in *Science* in 1997.
Hypnotized participants received painful heat stimuli while their brains
were scanned. The hypnotic suggestions asked for the *unpleasantness* of the
pain to go up or down, without changing its intensity.

**What it found.** Participants reported changes in unpleasantness with no
change in perceived intensity. In the brain scan (positron emission
tomography), a region called the anterior cingulate cortex tracked the
unpleasantness. The primary somatosensory cortex, the region that maps the
body's sensations, did not change.

**Strength of evidence:** moderate for the general idea that the intensity
and the unpleasantness of pain are processed in partly separate ways. That
distinction is widely accepted in pain research. The study itself is small
and old. And "this region encodes unpleasantness" is a simplification: the
anterior cingulate takes part in many other functions.
:::

The match with the parable is tempting: the first arrow would be the
intensity, and the second would be the unpleasantness and everything that
follows it. That reading is useful as an image, but it has limits that need
to be stated:

1. **The sutta speaks of lament and distress, reactions that come after the
   pain.** The unpleasantness Rainville measured is part of the painful
   experience itself, almost immediate. The text's second arrow is wider and
   slower than the unpleasantness in the lab.
2. **The study used hypnosis, not meditation.** It shows that the affective
   dimension of pain can change without the sensory one changing. It does
   not show that meditation produces this effect. Whether it does, and how
   much, is the subject of chapter {{cap:o-corpo-o-sono-e-a-dor}}. The short
   answer: there is evidence, of moderate to preliminary strength, and the
   effects are smaller than is usually advertised.
3. **There is no "seat of suffering" in the brain.** Saying that Buddhism
   "discovered the anterior cingulate" would be exactly the kind of
   exaggeration this book promises to avoid.

What remains, after the caveats, is a real and modest convergence. The
tradition claims that pain and the reaction to pain are two things. Science
has found independent reasons to treat them as two things. The
second claim does not prove the first. But it makes it reasonable to take
the first seriously.

The same logic applies beyond physical pain. A curt email from your boss is
the first arrow. The next three hours spent chewing over the email,
rehearsing replies and foreseeing your firing are the second. Chapter
{{cap:estresse-o-alarme-que-nao-desliga}} shows that the body responds to
this second arrow, the imagined threat, with the same alarm system it uses
for real threats. That is where it takes its physical toll.

## Why This Is Not "Positive Thinking"

A hasty reading of the four truths concludes that Buddhism tells you to want
less and accept everything. There are three reasons this is wrong.

**First, the target is not desire; it is thirst.** Wanting things, making
plans, having preferences is not the problem. The problem is the urgency:
the demand that experience be different from what it is. You can very much
want a test to come back good without spending the week in agony.

**Second, the second arrow cannot be removed by a decision.** Telling
yourself "I won't get upset" does not work. The sutta describes the
difference between the ordinary person and the practitioner as the fruit of
training. That is why there is a fourth truth, and it is a path, not a piece
of advice. Positive thinking replaces one thought with another; what the
tradition proposes is to change your relationship with thoughts, the
positive ones included.

**Third, accepting is not agreeing.** Seeing the first arrow clearly includes
seeing what can be done about it. A person who is not drowning in the second
arrow is usually in a better position to pull out the first. Buddhism has
never recommended passivity in the face of an injustice or a treatable
illness.

## If You Want to Try

For a week, once a day, with something small: a slow line, a backache, an
irritating message.

1. When the discomfort shows up, name the first arrow silently in a few
   words: "line," "shoulder pain," "message."
2. Then watch what comes next, without trying to change it: comments,
   predictions, comparisons, the wish for it to end. That is the second
   arrow.
3. Do not try to get rid of the second arrow. Just notice that it is
   different from the first: that it has another texture, and that it comes
   *after*.

The goal is not to feel less. It is to begin to notice that there are two
things where there seemed to be one. That noticing is the raw material of
every practice in Part III.

The exercise is for small discomforts. A pain that is new, strong or won't
go away is a matter for a medical evaluation, and noticing the second arrow
is not a substitute for treatment.

## What This Chapter Established

- The **Four Noble Truths** are: there is dukkha; dukkha has a cause, taṇhā;
  dukkha can cease; the path to its cessation is the Noble Eightfold Path.
- **Dukkha** is wider than pain: it is the unsatisfactoriness of everything
  that changes, the pleasant included. **Taṇhā** is thirst, the urgency for
  experience to be otherwise, not simple wanting.
- Each truth comes with a task: understand, give up, realize, develop. It is
  a program, not a creed.
- The structure is that of a **medical diagnosis**, and the early tradition
  itself makes the comparison. Whether it was borrowed from an earlier
  medical scheme is an open historical question.
- Traditional Buddhism includes rebirth; this book's psychological reading
  is a selection, and it is presented as one.
- The parable of the **second arrow** distinguishes pain from the reaction to
  pain. Pain research also distinguishes intensity from unpleasantness: a
  modest and real convergence, not proof that Buddhism "was right."
