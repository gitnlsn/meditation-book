This chapter is about a kind of meditation whose target is not attention
but your disposition toward others: kindness and compassion, cultivated on
purpose. It establishes three things. First, that the Buddhist tradition
treats these qualities as trainable, with a method and an order. Second,
that science has found a moderate effect of this training on positive
emotions — and a much smaller, more fragile effect on behavior. Third, that
feeling another person's pain (empathy) and wishing someone who suffers well
(compassion) are not the same thing, and that confusing the two wears you
down. That last distinction has a curious parallel in a fifth-century
Buddhist manual.

## The Four Abodes

The tradition groups four qualities under the name **brahmavihāras**,
literally "divine abodes" or "sublime abodes": states the mind can dwell in.
They are:

- **Mettā** — goodwill, friendliness, the wish that a being be well and
  happy. It is usually translated as "loving-kindness"; Sujato translates it
  as "love."
- **Karuṇā** — compassion: the wish that a being be free from suffering, in
  the face of that being's suffering.
- **Muditā** — joy at another's good fortune, the opposite of envy. Sujato
  translates it as "rejoicing."
- **Upekkhā** — equanimity, the same attitude described in
  {{cap:observar-sem-agarrar}}, here directed toward beings: wishing all of
  them well without favoring some and shutting out others.

The suttas describe the practice as a radiating outward. Here is Bhikkhu
Sujato's translation of a discourse from the Middle-Length collection, "The
Simile of the Cloth" (MN 7):

> They meditate spreading a heart full of love to one direction, and to the
> second, and to the third, and to the fourth. In the same way above, below,
> across, everywhere, all around, they spread a heart full of love to the
> whole world—abundant, expansive, limitless, free of enmity and ill will.

The same text repeats the formula for compassion, rejoicing and
equanimity.

The best-known text on mettā is the *Karaṇīya Mettā Sutta* (in the
original, simply the *Metta Sutta*), a short poem in the collection called
the *Sutta Nipāta* (1.8). Two passages:

> May all beings be happy!

> Even as a mother would protect with her life her child, her only child, so
> too for all creatures unfold a boundless heart.

One detail modern readings tend to skip: the poem opens by saying the
practice is a way "to realize the state of peace," first asks for an ethical
life — being upright, easy to speak to, doing nothing the wise would
blame — and ends by saying that someone who develops it will "never return
to a womb again," that is, will not be reborn. In the tradition, mettā is
not a wellness technique. It is part of an ethical and religious path that
includes rebirth, as {{cap:as-quatro-nobres-verdades-como-diagnostico}}
explained.

## The Near Enemies

The *Visuddhimagga*, Buddhaghosa's manual introduced in
{{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}}, adds an idea that is not in the suttas and is
one of the most useful in this chapter. Each sublime abode has two enemies.
The **far enemy** is the obvious opposite: ill will for kindness, cruelty
for compassion. The **near enemy** is more dangerous, because it resembles
the quality and passes itself off as it:

| Quality | Near enemy (looks like it, but isn't) | Far enemy |
|---|---|---|
| Mettā | attachment, greed | ill will |
| Karuṇā | grief "based on the home life" | cruelty |
| Muditā | joy "based on the home life" | aversion, boredom |
| Upekkhā | indifference born of ignorance | greed and resentment |

The text explains that greed is the near enemy of kindness "since both
share in seeing virtues": someone who likes another person out of
attachment also sees good qualities in them, and so mistakes one thing for
the other. And grief is the near enemy of compassion "since both share in
seeing failure": faced with someone else's suffering, it is easy to slide
from compassion into distress. Hold on to this second pair. Neuroscience
arrived at a similar distinction by a different route.

## Empathy Is Not Compassion

Two definitions, in the sense the research uses:

- **Empathy** is sharing another person's emotional state: feeling, to some
  degree, what they feel. Wincing when you see someone get hurt is empathy.
- **Compassion** is a feeling of care in the face of another's suffering,
  together with the motivation to help. It does not require feeling the same
  pain. It includes warmth, not just distress.

The difference sounds philosophical, but it has practical consequences.
People who work close to suffering — nursing, medicine, social work, caring
for a sick relative — know the exhaustion that usually goes by the name
"compassion fatigue." Olga Klimecki and Tania Singer, of the Max Planck
Institute, proposed in 2011 that the name is wrong. What wears people down,
they argued, is not compassion but unregulated empathy: absorbing someone
else's pain as if it were your own, to the point of pulling back to protect
yourself. Hence the term they propose, which this book adopts: **empathic
distress fatigue**, the exhaustion of someone who repeatedly feels other
people's pain without a way to turn it into care.

The experiment that most supports this idea is in the box below.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Klimecki, Leiberg, Ricard and Singer, 2014 (along with an
earlier study by the same group, from 2013). Women only, to control for
gender differences. One group was trained first in empathy — resonating with
other people's suffering — and then in compassion; 25 of the 30 who started
completed the study. The control group did two memory trainings (28 of 33
completed). Before and after each training, all of them watched videos of
people suffering while inside a scanner.

**What it found.** Empathy training *increased* negative affect in response
to the videos and activity in regions linked to empathy for pain (the
anterior insula and anterior midcingulate cortex). The compassion training
that came afterward reversed the rise in negative affect, increased
self-reported positive affect, and activated a different network, associated
with reward and affiliation. The memory group showed none of these changes.

**Strength of evidence:** preliminary. The sample was small and all women;
the groups were recruited one after the other, not randomized; and the order
was always empathy before compassion, so we don't know what compassion would
do on its own. Reading "reward network" as "compassion feels good" is
reverse inference ({{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}). It also counts
against it that this line of research comes from only a few labs.
:::

The convergence with the *Visuddhimagga* is real and modest. The manual says
compassion has a near enemy that resembles it and is made of grief; the lab
found that training resonance with pain increases the suffering of the
person training, and that training care reduces it. The two claims are not
the same: Buddhaghosa is talking about a mental state described through
introspection and doctrine; Klimecki is talking about reported affect and
brain activity in an experiment. Neither proves the other. But they point in
the same direction, from completely different methods.

## The Practice: Mettā in Four Circles

The order comes from the *Visuddhimagga*, simplified. The manual says to
start with yourself, and explains why: not because the practitioner deserves
more, but as an example — "Just as I want to be happy and dread pain, … so
do other beings, too." Then someone dear; then someone neutral; last,
someone hostile. And it warns against starting with the hostile person or
the neutral one, because putting an antipathetic person in a dear one's
place "is fatiguing."

Ten to fifteen minutes.

1. **Sit** as in the earlier practices and spend a minute with the breath,
   to settle.
2. **First circle: you.** Silently and slowly repeat three or four phrases,
   for example: "May I be safe. May I be happy. May I be healthy. May I live
   with ease." Let the phrases follow the rhythm of the breath. Two to three
   minutes.
3. **Second circle: someone dear**, someone you feel affection for easily —
   a friend, a teacher, someone who was good to you. Bring the person to
   mind and direct the same phrases to them: "May you be safe…"
4. **Third circle: someone neutral** — the doorman, the cashier, someone you
   see without knowing. Same phrases. Notice that this person also wants to
   be happy and does not want to suffer.
5. **Fourth circle: someone difficult.** Start with someone *mildly*
   irritating, not the person who hurt you most. Same phrases. If anger comes
   up, note it as in {{cap:observar-sem-agarrar}} and go back to the phrases,
   or go back to the second circle for a moment.
6. **If you like, open it up:** "May all beings be safe and happy," as in
   the poem.
7. **Finish** with a few rounds of breathing.

Three observations. **The phrases don't have to produce a feeling.** The
instruction is to direct the intention, not to manufacture emotion; if you
feel nothing, the practice was still done. **The first circle is hard for a
lot of people.** If wishing yourself well stirs up sadness or harsh
self-criticism, start with the second circle and come back to yourself
later — a common adaptation, not the manual's order. **Wishing a difficult
person well is not agreeing with them, forgiving them or getting back
together with them.** It is recognizing that this person also wants to be
happy and does not want to suffer, and noticing what happens in you when you
do that.

## What the Science Says

Switching registers now, to what experiments have measured.

**Positive affect: moderate evidence.** A 2015 meta-analysis pooled 24
studies, with 1,759 participants, on loving-kindness meditation and reported
positive emotions. It found medium-sized effects on everyday positive
emotions, both in randomized trials with a waitlist and in non-randomized
studies, and smaller effects for interventions centered on compassion than
for those centered on loving-kindness. Another meta-analysis, from 2014,
with 22 trials, found moderate effects on reported depression, compassion
and self-compassion compared with passive controls — but inconclusive
results against active controls, low to moderate methodological quality, and
wide intervals, because the studies were small. The authors also note that
the practice may be challenging at first for some people.

The most cited study in the area is by Barbara Fredrickson and colleagues
(2008): 139 working adults, half of them randomly assigned to begin
practicing loving-kindness. The practice increased daily positive emotions,
and that increase was associated with gains in personal resources (purpose,
social support, fewer illness symptoms) that predicted greater life
satisfaction and fewer depressive symptoms. The caveat comes with it: the
control was a waitlist, the outcomes were self-reported, and the broader
theory behind Fredrickson's program — especially the idea of an ideal
"positivity ratio" — has been criticized. This book cites the trial, not the
theory.

**Empathy vs. compassion: preliminary evidence.** This is the line of work
described in the box. The largest study extending it is the ReSource Project,
at the Max Planck Institute: nine months of training in three modules —
attention to the present and the body, socio-emotional practices such as
compassion and kindness, and perspective-taking practices on oneself and
others. Singer and Engert's 2019 overview reports different effects
depending on the module, including on the cortisol response to a social
stress test, which fell after the social modules and not after the attention
module (the result is in {{cap:estresse-o-alarme-que-nao-desliga}}). It is
the best argument that "meditation" is not a single intervention. But it
comes, again, from a single research group.

**Real behavior: smaller than the questionnaires suggest.** This is where
the field exaggerates most. A 2018 meta-analysis by Kreplin, Farias and
Brazil pooled randomized trials in healthy adults (22 in the review, 16 in
the meta-analysis) on meditation and prosocial behavior. It found a moderate
increase overall — but only in compassion and empathy, **not** in
aggression, sense of connectedness or prejudice. And the increase in
compassion appeared only under two conditions: when the meditation teacher
was a coauthor of the study, and when the comparison group was passive. With
an active control, or without the teacher among the authors, the effect
disappeared. The authors themselves conclude that prosocial effects depend
on the type of behavior and on the quality of the study.

The honest summary: loving-kindness practice increases reported positive
emotions (**moderate**); empathy and compassion seem to have different, and
opposite, effects on the people who train them (**preliminary**); that the
practice makes someone kinder to others, in a way you can see in their
behavior, is **preliminary** and sensitive to bias.

## Where Tradition and Science Part Ways

For the tradition, the sublime abodes are states that deepen into meditative
absorption and belong to an ethical path; the *Visuddhimagga* devotes an
entire chapter to them, and the mettā poem ends with the end of rebirth. For
science, they are interventions lasting a few weeks, evaluated by
questionnaire, lab tasks and, sometimes, brain imaging. The tradition claims
that kindness transforms the person who practices it and, through that, the
way they treat others; the experiments confirm the first part with more
confidence than the second.

There is also a difference in starting point. The tradition puts ethics
before meditation: the poem asks for integrity before it asks for love.
Modern programs usually offer the practice without that frame. Whether that
changes the effect, none of the studies cited here has tested.

## Cautions

Directing kindness toward yourself can bring up sadness, shame or memories
of not having been cared for — which is one of the reasons why, for some
people, the practice "may initially be challenging." For anyone with a history of trauma or abuse, the
fourth circle should never begin with the person who caused the harm, and
there is no obligation ever to reach that person. If the practice stirs up
distress that does not pass after the session, stop and look at the signs
described in {{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}}. For caregivers who are
burned out, the distinction between empathy and compassion is useful, but it
is not a substitute for rest, support, or treatment for depression or
burnout.

## What This Chapter Established

- The **brahmavihāras** are four trainable qualities: **mettā** (kindness),
  **karuṇā** (compassion), **muditā** (joy at another's good fortune) and
  **upekkhā** (equanimity toward all). In the tradition, they are part of an
  ethical and religious path.
- The *Visuddhimagga* describes **near enemies**: attachment passes itself
  off as kindness; grief passes itself off as compassion.
- **Empathy** is feeling with the other person; **compassion** is caring in
  the face of their suffering. **Empathic distress fatigue** is the
  exhaustion that comes from the first without the second.
- Training empathy increased negative affect; training compassion reduced it
  (**preliminary**, few labs). The convergence with the Buddhist manual is
  real and modest.
- The practice increases reported positive emotions (**moderate**). Effects
  on behavior are **preliminary**, smaller than in questionnaires, and vanish
  with active controls.
- Mettā in four circles: you, someone dear, someone neutral, someone
  difficult — starting light.
