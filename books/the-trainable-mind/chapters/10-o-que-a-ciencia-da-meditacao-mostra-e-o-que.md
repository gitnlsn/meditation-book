This chapter takes stock. Having seen how a practice is evaluated, how the
brain changes and what meditation trains, the question is a direct one: is
meditating good for your health? The answer, checked study by study, is this:
there is **moderate** evidence of real benefits, small to moderate in size,
for anxiety, depression and stress; there is **stronger** evidence that one
specific program reduces relapse in people who have already had several
depressions; meditation programs, on average, tie with other active
treatments and do not beat them; and the most famous story about the brain —
"eight weeks of meditation change your gray matter" — did not hold up when it
was tested rigorously. At the end there is a table that sums it all up.

## The Two Programs Research Studies Most

Almost all clinical research on meditation uses one of two standardized
programs. Both are secular adaptations of Buddhist practices, and that needs
saying: they were taken out of their religious context on purpose, so they
could enter hospitals and clinics. What is lost and what is gained in that
adaptation was discussed in chapter {{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}}.

**MBSR** (*Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction*) is a group course, now in an
eight-week format with weekly meetings, created by Jon Kabat-Zinn in a
behavioral medicine program for chronic pain patients who had not improved
with standard medical care. The paper that introduced the program
(Kabat-Zinn 1982, in a ten-week version) had no control group, and the
author himself called its results preliminary: it matters for its history,
not as proof. The course teaches practices such as attention on the breath
and the body scan, described in chapter {{cap:o-corpo-o-sono-e-a-dor}}. Kabat-Zinn
himself described where the program came from and how it entered medicine
(Kabat-Zinn 2003).

**MBCT** (*Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy*) combines mindfulness
practices of the same kind with elements of cognitive therapy for one
specific problem: keeping depression from coming back. In the format used in
the large trials, it is eight weekly group sessions of a little over two
hours. The aim, as the researchers put it, is for people to learn to become
more aware of the bodily sensations, thoughts and feelings associated with
depression returning — rumination, covered in chapter {{cap:a-mente-que-divaga}}, is
one of them — and to relate to them constructively.

Understanding MBCT takes one term from psychiatry. **Relapse** is the return
of depressive symptoms after a person has improved. For many people,
depression is an illness that comes and goes, and someone who has had
several episodes is at high risk of having more. Preventing relapse is
therefore a large part of treatment — and it is where meditation has its
most solid result.

Throughout the chapter, the method terms — randomized trial, active control,
meta-analysis, effect size, publication bias — are the ones explained in
chapter {{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}. The question that keeps coming back is
always the same: *compared with what?*

## Anxiety, Depression and Stress: The Average

### The Review That Accepted Only Active Controls

In 2014, a Johns Hopkins University team led by Madhav Goyal published a
review in *JAMA Internal Medicine* with a demanding rule: it included only
randomized trials in which meditation was compared with an active control,
one capable of producing a placebo effect. Of more than 18,000 citations, 47
trials remained, with 3,515 participants.

The result: mindfulness programs had **moderate** evidence of improvement in
anxiety (an effect size of 0.38 at the end of eight weeks and 0.22 three to
six months later), in depression (0.30 and 0.23) and in pain (0.33). For
stress and mental-health-related quality of life, the evidence was **low**.
For positive mood, attention, substance use, eating habits, sleep and weight,
the evidence was low or insufficient. And, in the decisive sentence of the
abstract, the authors **found no evidence that meditation programs were
better than any active treatment** — drugs, exercise, other behavioral
therapies.

These effect sizes are small. They are real, and they are comparable to
those of other psychological interventions, but they are a long way from the
"proven to transform" of the headlines. At the time, some of the press
summed up the study as "meditation works as well as antidepressants," which
is not what it says.

### For People Who Aren't Ill

What about the general population, people without a diagnosis? Julieta
Galante and colleagues, at Cambridge, gathered 136 randomized trials in
2021, with 11,605 participants in 29 countries, on mindfulness programs
outside clinical settings. Compared with *doing nothing*, the programs on
average improved anxiety, depression, psychological distress and
well-being. But three caveats come from the study itself:

- the result holds "in most but not all scenarios"; the prediction intervals
  indicate that in some settings the program may not help;
- compared with specific active practices, there was no evidence of
  superiority;
- confidence in the evidence, under the grading system used in medicine
  (GRADE), ranged from moderate to very low, mainly because of inconsistency
  between studies and risk of bias.

In 2023, the same team asked the question again using the individual data of
2,371 participants from 13 higher-quality trials. Against passive groups,
in-person programs led by a teacher reduced psychological distress between
one and six months later, with a small to moderate effect (a standardized
difference of 0.32). The result was consistent across studies. The limit is
in the design: comparing with "nothing" does not separate the effect of
meditation from the effect of joining a group, having a teacher and
expecting to get better.

**Strength of evidence for anxiety, depression and stress: moderate.**

## The Toughest Comparison: Against Other Treatments

The question that matters most to someone deciding what to do is not "is
meditation better than nothing?" but "is it better than the alternatives?"
The most complete answer is the meta-analysis by Simon Goldberg and
colleagues (2018), with 142 samples and 12,005 people with psychiatric
disorders, presented in detail in chapter {{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}: the
effect size falls from 0.55 against no treatment to practically zero
(−0.004) against treatments with established evidence. It is the pattern of
almost the whole field: the stronger the comparison, the smaller the
advantage, until it disappears next to a treatment that already works.

The reanalysis by Goldberg and Tucker (2020), discussed in the same chapter,
added an uncomfortable detail. Mindfulness's small advantage over other real
therapies (g = 0.13, across 60 comparisons) was associated with
**researcher allegiance** — the stated preference of the people running the
study — and disappeared when that preference was absent or balanced. That is
why the critique of hype in the field by Van Dam and colleagues (2018) is
still needed.

**Conclusion:** mindfulness works about as well as other active treatments.
It does not work better. **Strength of evidence: moderate.**

## Recurrent Depression: The Most Solid Result

In 2016, Willem Kuyken and colleagues pooled the individual data from nine
randomized trials of MBCT for people with recurrent depression in full or
partial remission: 1,258 patients. Within 60 weeks of follow-up, 38% of
those who did MBCT relapsed, against 49% of those who did not. The risk of
relapse was about 30% lower (a hazard ratio of 0.69), and it was also lower
when MBCT was compared only with active treatments (0.79). The effect seemed
larger in people who still had more residual symptoms.

The most demanding test came earlier, in the United Kingdom:

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** PREVENT, Kuyken and colleagues, published in *The Lancet* in
2015. The participants were 424 adults who had already had three or more
episodes of depression and were taking a maintenance antidepressant,
recruited from 95 primary care practices. Half were randomly assigned to
stay on the antidepressant; the other half did MBCT with support to taper or
stop the medication. Follow-up lasted 24 months; the assessors did not know
which group each person was in.

**What it found.** 94 of 212 people in the MBCT group relapsed (44%), and
100 of 212 in the antidepressant group (47%). The difference was not
statistically significant (hazard ratio 0.89; p = 0.43). The authors
conclude that **there is no evidence that MBCT is superior** to maintenance
antidepressants, and that both groups did well.

**Strength of evidence:** moderate for "MBCT is an alternative of similar
efficacy for this group of patients": one large, well-conducted trial, but
only one. It does not support "MBCT is better
than medication," which is how the study sometimes appears in the press.
:::

**Strength of evidence for relapse prevention with MBCT: moderate to
strong**, and specific: it applies to people with *recurrent* depression,
not to depression in general, and not to an acute episode.

One caveat that cannot be left out: in PREVENT, the antidepressant was
tapered under medical supervision. **No one should stop an antidepressant on
their own in order to meditate.** Meditation is not a substitute for
treatment; in this study, it was a treatment, delivered by professionals,
within a health system.

## Anxiety: MBSR and Escitalopram

For anxiety disorders, the most cited study used a design of its own:
noninferiority. **Noninferiority** is a type of trial that does not ask
whether one treatment is better than another, but whether it is *not worse*
than the other beyond a margin fixed before the study. It is the right design
when the new treatment has other advantages (cost, side effects, preference)
and it is enough to show that it does not lose much in efficacy.

Elizabeth Hoge and colleagues (2023) randomly assigned 276 adults with a
diagnosed anxiety disorder, at three American medical centers, to eight
weeks of MBSR or of escitalopram, a first-line antidepressant for anxiety.
The main analysis included the 208 people who completed the study. Anxiety
severity, rated by interviewers who did not know each participant's group,
fell by 1.35 points with MBSR and 1.43 with escitalopram, on a clinical
severity scale. The difference stayed within the agreed margin, and MBSR was
declared noninferior. A secondary analysis, including everyone randomized,
reached the same conclusion. At least one study-related adverse event
occurred in 78.6% of the medication group and in 15.4% of the MBSR group.

It is an important result, and it has clear limits: it is a single trial;
the participants knew what they were receiving; and "noninferior" does not
mean "the same in every case," much less "better." Note too that 15% of the
meditation group reported some adverse event — which leads to a topic taken
up in chapter {{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}}. **Strength of evidence:
moderate.**

## Where It Didn't Work: Mindfulness in Schools

A large negative result is worth as much as a positive one, and the field
has one. The MYRIAD trial (Kuyken and colleagues, 2022), in the United
Kingdom, randomly assigned 85 schools to offer either a ten-lesson
mindfulness course or their usual social-emotional teaching. The analysis
covered 84 schools and 8,376 students aged 11 to 14. After a year, **there
was no evidence that the mindfulness course was better** than usual teaching
for risk of depression, social-emotional functioning or well-being: the
differences were practically zero.

The study was run by a team sympathetic to mindfulness, with a large sample
representative of the British population. It does not show that meditation
is useless for adolescents. It shows that offering a short course to *every*
student in a school, without selecting those who need it, produced no
measurable benefit. **Strength of evidence: moderate, for the absence of an
effect in this format** — a single trial, but a very large and well-conducted
one, which makes it unlikely that a meaningful benefit went unnoticed.

## The Brain: The Headline That Didn't Replicate

In 2011, Britta Hölzel and colleagues published a study that became the
basis for one of the most repeated lines about meditation: eight weeks of
MBSR would increase gray matter density (a term explained in
chapter {{cap:neuroplasticidade-sem-exagero}}) in the hippocampus and other regions.
The study compared 16 MBSR participants with 17 people on a waitlist, with
no random assignment between the groups.

In 2022, a team from Richard Davidson's lab — one of the most important
centers of meditation research — tested the same idea with far greater rigor
(Kral et al. 2022). It combined two randomized trials with 218 people who
had never meditated, randomly assigned to MBSR (75), to a health program
with the same format but no meditation (73), or to a waitlist (70), with
brain scans before and after. It found **no evidence** that MBSR changed
gray matter volume or density, or the thickness of the cortex (the brain's
outer layer), compared with either control — neither across the whole brain
nor in the regions singled out by earlier studies.

This does not prove that meditation never changes the structure of the
brain. The authors themselves note that much longer or more specific
training might show changes, and chapter {{cap:neuroplasticidade-sem-exagero}} shows
that learning sustained over years does change brain structure. What it
proves is that the "eight weeks" headline rested on a small study that the
more rigorous test did not confirm. **Strength of evidence for structural
change from short programs: contested — and, in the largest trial, absent.**

## Blood Pressure

In 2013, the American Heart Association, the leading cardiology association
in the United States, published a scientific statement on ways to lower
blood pressure without medication or diet (Brook et al. 2013). Each approach
received a class of recommendation and a level of evidence:

- **Transcendental Meditation**, a specific, standardized technique with its
  own teaching organization: Class IIb, Level B — "may be considered," with
  limited evidence of a modest reduction.
- **Other meditation techniques**: Class III, Level C — not recommended, for
  lack of sufficient evidence.
- For comparison, **aerobic exercise** received Class I, Level A, the
  highest.

The authors sum up the behavioral therapies, meditation included, as having
"modest, mixed, or no consistent evidence." Promotional materials connected
to the Transcendental Meditation organization often present this statement
as an endorsement, which it is not. The meta-analysis cited in
chapter {{cap:estresse-o-alarme-que-nao-desliga}} found reductions in systolic
pressure with meditation — a preliminary result that mixes very different
studies — but that does not change the clinical recommendation: people with
high blood pressure treat it with their doctor. **Strength of evidence:
preliminary for Transcendental Meditation; insufficient for the others.**

## What This Assessment Doesn't License

Three claims circulate widely, and none of them holds up against the data
above:

1. **"Meditation works better than medication."** No. In relapse
   prevention it tied with the antidepressant; in anxiety it was noninferior
   in one trial; in the meta-analyses it ties with established treatments.
2. **"Meditation changes your brain in eight weeks."** The largest and
   best-controlled test found no structural change.
3. **"Meditation is good for everyone."** The average effect is small, it
   varies between settings, it was zero in a large trial with adolescents,
   and there are documented adverse effects, discussed in
   chapter {{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}}.

And one that does hold up: for many people, a well-run meditation program
is a real option, with a benefit similar to that of other active treatments
— and, for people with recurrent depression, an alternative or a complement
with good evidence. That does not make it a substitute for treatment.
Anyone with depression, anxiety or any other diagnosis decides what to do
with a health professional.

## What This Chapter Established

- **MBSR** is the eight-week course created by Kabat-Zinn; **MBCT** is its
  adaptation, with cognitive therapy, to prevent **relapse** — the return of
  depression after improvement. Both are secular adaptations of Buddhist
  practices.
- For anxiety, depression and stress, the evidence of benefit is
  **moderate** and the effects are **small to moderate**.
- Against established treatments, meditation **ties, it does not win**; the
  small advantage over other therapies is tied to researcher allegiance.
- The most solid result is relapse prevention in recurrent depression with
  MBCT (38% vs. 49%), with efficacy similar to that of maintenance
  antidepressants (44% vs. 47%).
- In a **noninferiority** trial, MBSR was no worse than escitalopram for
  anxiety, with fewer adverse events.
- Universal mindfulness in schools had no effect in a very large trial.
- The idea that eight weeks of meditation change the structure of the brain
  was not confirmed in the most rigorous test.
- None of this makes meditation a substitute for treatment.
