This chapter takes stock: is meditating good for your health? The short
answer has four parts.

- Against *doing nothing*, meditation programs bring real benefits, small to
  moderate, for anxiety, depression, stress and pain (**moderate**
  evidence).
- Against treatments that already work, such as psychotherapy and
  antidepressants, they **tie, and do not beat them**.
- The most solid result is a program that reduces relapse in people who
  have already had several depressions (**moderate to strong**).
- Courses for every student in a school did not work; "eight weeks of
  meditation change your gray matter" did not hold up; and adverse effects
  exist and belong in the count.

At the end there is a table and a list of what is not yet known.

## The Two Programs Research Studies Most

Almost all clinical research on meditation uses one of two standardized
programs. Both are secular adaptations of Buddhist practices, and that needs
saying. They were taken out of their religious context on purpose, so they
could enter hospitals and clinics. What is lost and what is gained in that
adaptation was discussed in chapter {{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}}.

**MBSR** (*Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction*) is an eight-week group
course with weekly meetings. Jon Kabat-Zinn created it for chronic pain
patients who had not improved with standard medical care. The course
teaches practices such as attention on the breath and the body scan,
described in chapter {{cap:o-corpo-o-sono-e-a-dor}}.

The paper that introduced the program (Kabat-Zinn 1982, in a ten-week
version) had no control group. The author himself called its results
preliminary. It matters for its history, not as proof.

**MBCT** (*Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy*) combines mindfulness
practices with elements of cognitive therapy for one specific problem:
keeping depression from coming back. It is eight weekly group sessions of a
little over two hours. People learn to notice the bodily sensations,
thoughts and feelings that signal depression returning, and to relate to
them constructively. Rumination, covered in chapter {{cap:a-mente-que-divaga}},
is one of them.

Understanding MBCT takes one term from psychiatry. **Relapse** is the return
of depressive symptoms after a person has improved. For many people,
depression comes and goes, and someone who has had several episodes is at
high risk of having more. Preventing relapse is therefore a large part of
treatment. It is also where meditation has its most solid result.

The method terms (randomized trial, active control, meta-analysis, effect
size, publication bias) are the ones explained in
chapter {{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}. The question that keeps coming
back is always the same: *compared with what?*

## Compared With What: The Overall Picture

**Short answer:** against doing nothing, mindfulness helps, usually a little
to moderately. Against another activity, the advantage shrinks. Against
other treatments that work, it ties. **Strength of evidence: moderate.**

The best source for this picture is a review of reviews published in 2022 by
Simon Goldberg and colleagues, with Richard Davidson among the authors. It
gathered dozens of meta-analyses and sorted the results by type of
comparison. It is the broadest synthesis in the field, and it was written
from inside it: the authors work at a meditation research center.

![What mindfulness programs showed in each type of comparison, in the review of reviews by Goldberg and colleagues (2022). Against proven treatment there was one exception at the end of treatment (quitting smoking) and, at follow-up, a small advantage for MBCT in preventing relapse, both covered below.](../illustrations/figures/fig-comparado-com-o-que-en.svg)

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Goldberg and colleagues (2022) gathered 44 meta-analyses of
randomized trials, together covering 336 trials and 30,483 participants.
They sorted the results into three types of comparison: passive controls
(waitlist, no treatment), active controls and treatments with established
evidence.

**What it found.** Against passive controls, mindfulness programs were
superior for most of the problems studied (effect sizes from 0.10 to 0.89).
Against active controls, effects were typically smaller and less often
statistically significant. Against established treatments, there was no
difference at the end of treatment, with one exception: quitting smoking.

**Strength of evidence:** moderate. The authors point out the limits:
adverse effects reported inconsistently and, in comparisons with active
controls, low statistical power, the chance of detecting an effect that is
there (0.52 on average, against 0.78 with passive controls).
:::

That last limit changes how to read the tie. Many meta-analyses that
compared mindfulness with another activity were too small to notice a small
difference. "They found no difference" does not always mean "there is no
difference."

A 2014 review led by Madhav Goyal reached the same point by another route.
It accepted only trials in which meditation was compared with an active
control, one capable of producing a placebo effect. It found a **small-to-moderate**
improvement in anxiety, depression and pain (moderate evidence). For stress,
the evidence was low. And it found no evidence that meditation programs
were better than any active treatment: drugs, exercise, other therapies.

Some of the press summed up that review as "meditation works as well as
antidepressants." It does not say that. It says the effects are small to moderate and
do not beat those of other active treatments.

### For People Who Aren't Ill

The pattern is the same in the general population, people without a
diagnosis. A 2021 meta-analysis led by Julieta Galante gathered 136 trials
outside clinical settings. Against doing nothing, the programs on average
improved anxiety, depression, psychological distress and well-being.
Against other active practices, there was no evidence of superiority.

The review itself carries two caveats that matter. The result holds "in
most but not all scenarios." And confidence in the evidence ranged from
moderate to very low, mainly because the studies disagreed with one another
and many were at risk of bias.

In 2023, the same team asked the question again using the individual data
from 13 higher-quality trials. Against passive groups, group
courses led by a teacher reduced psychological distress with a small to
moderate effect, consistently. The limit is in the design: comparing with
"nothing" does not separate meditation from the effect of joining a group,
having a teacher and expecting to get better.

## The Toughest Comparison: Against Other Treatments

**Short answer:** mindfulness works about as well as other active
treatments. It does not work better. **Strength of evidence: moderate; for
depression, against CBT, moderate to strong.**

For someone deciding what to do, this is the question that matters. Not "is
it better than nothing?" but "is it better than the alternatives?" In the
meta-analysis by Goldberg and colleagues (2018), seen in
chapter {{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}, the effect falls from moderate,
against no treatment, to practically zero, against treatments with
established evidence.

The 2022 review of reviews confirmed the picture, with three details:

- **Quitting smoking** was the only exception to the tie at the end of
  treatment. The advantage is contested, as we will see below.
- **Relapse prevention:** at follow-up, MBCT had a small advantage over the
  established treatments.
- **Anxiety:** the established treatments came out slightly ahead, but the
  difference was uncertain and could be zero.

**CBT** (cognitive behavioral therapy) is the psychotherapy that trains a
person to identify and change the thoughts and behaviors that keep a
problem going. It is recommended as the first-line treatment for
depression. That is why the most direct test of the tie is against it.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Sverre and colleagues (2023) gathered the 30 randomized
trials that compared mindfulness-based therapies directly with CBT for
depression, with 2,750 participants. They applied an equivalence test,
which does not ask whether there is a difference, but whether the
difference is small enough to be negligible.

**What it found.** The two were statistically equivalent at the end of
treatment (g = −0.009) and at follow-up (g = −0.033). The authors say it is
not known whether the tie comes from different mechanisms or from factors
common to all psychotherapy.

**Strength of evidence:** moderate to strong. Many head-to-head trials, with
a test designed to measure a tie.
:::

There is also an uncomfortable detail, seen in
chapter {{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}. In some comparisons,
mindfulness seemed to have a small advantage over other real therapies. A
reanalysis by Goldberg and Tucker (2020) showed that the advantage tracked
**researcher allegiance**: the stated preference of the people running the
study. When that preference was absent or balanced, the advantage
disappeared.

Whether meditation falls a little short of established treatments for
anxiety is not yet known: the difference found is small and uncertain.

## Recurrent Depression: The Most Solid Result

**Short answer:** for people who have had several depressions, MBCT lowers
the chance that depression comes back. And it works about as well as
staying on the antidepressant. **Strength of evidence: moderate to
strong.**

In 2016, Willem Kuyken and colleagues pooled the individual data from nine
trials of MBCT with people with recurrent depression who had improved.
Within a little over a year of follow-up, 38% of those who did MBCT
relapsed, against 49% of those who did not. The risk was also lower when
MBCT was compared only with active treatments. The effect seemed larger in
people who still had more symptoms after improving.

The most demanding test came earlier, in the United Kingdom:

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** PREVENT, Kuyken and colleagues, 2015. The participants were
424 adults who had already had three or more episodes of depression and
were taking a maintenance antidepressant, recruited from 95 primary care
practices. Half were randomly assigned to stay on the antidepressant. The
other half did MBCT, with support to taper or stop the medication.
Follow-up lasted 24 months; the assessors did not know which group each
person was in.

**What it found.** 94 of 212 people in the MBCT group relapsed (44%), and
100 of 212 in the antidepressant group (47%). The difference was not
statistically significant (hazard ratio 0.89; p = 0.43). The authors
conclude that **there is no evidence that MBCT is superior** to maintenance
antidepressants, and that both groups did well.

**Strength of evidence:** moderate for "MBCT is an alternative of similar
efficacy for this group of patients." It is one large, well-conducted
trial, and a later pooled analysis of four trials repeated the result. It
does not support "MBCT is better than medication," which is how the study
sometimes appears in the press.
:::

That pooled analysis, from 2021, compared two paths. In one, people tapered
the antidepressant with the support of a psychological therapy (MBCT or
preventive cognitive therapy). In the other, they stayed on the medication
alone. There was no difference in time to relapse. The authors include
researchers who developed or tested these therapies, and the tapering was
always done under medical supervision.

This body of evidence has reached the guidelines. The 2022 depression
guideline from NICE, the British body that issues clinical
recommendations, does two things:

- it lists group meditation, using a program such as MBCT, among the
  options for less severe depression;
- for people who got better on an antidepressant but are at high risk of
  relapse, it says to consider MBCT or group CBT if the person does not
  wish to continue on the medication.

The result is specific. It applies to people with *recurrent* depression,
not to depression in general, and not to an acute episode. How long the
protection lasts is not yet known. In a 2023 meta-analysis that compared
several therapies, MBCT beat placebo at the 3-, 6- and 9-month
measurements; CBT had the longest effect, up to 24 months. The authors call
for trials with long follow-up.

One caveat that cannot be left out: in PREVENT, the antidepressant was
tapered under medical supervision. **No one should stop an antidepressant on
their own in order to meditate.** Meditation is not a substitute for
treatment. In these studies, it was a treatment, delivered by
professionals, within a health system.

## Anxiety: MBSR and Escitalopram

**Short answer:** in one large trial, in-person MBSR was almost as good as a
first-line medication for anxiety disorders, with fewer side effects. It is
a single trial, and the video version did not repeat the result.
**Strength of evidence: moderate, for in-person MBSR.**

The study used a design of its own. **Noninferiority** is a type of trial
that does not ask whether one treatment is better than another. It asks
whether it is *not worse* than the other beyond a margin fixed before the
study. It is the right design when the new treatment has other advantages
(cost, side effects, preference) and it is enough to show that it does not
lose much in efficacy.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Elizabeth Hoge and colleagues (2023) randomly assigned 276
adults with an anxiety disorder, at three American medical centers. Each
person had eight weeks of MBSR or of escitalopram, a first-line
antidepressant for anxiety. The main analysis included the 208 people who completed the study.
Interviewers who did not know each participant's group rated the severity
of their anxiety.

**What it found.** On a clinical severity scale, anxiety fell by 1.35
points with MBSR and 1.43 with escitalopram. The difference stayed within
the agreed margin, and MBSR was declared noninferior. An analysis including
everyone randomized reached the same conclusion. At least one study-related
adverse event occurred in 78.6% of the medication group and in 15.4% of the
MBSR group.

**Strength of evidence:** moderate. A single, well-conducted trial, awaiting
another that repeats the result.
:::

Two later publications from the same trial complete the picture:

- **On the participants' own reports**, the differences between the groups
  were small and stayed that way through 24 weeks. The medication worked
  faster at week 4, but the advantage was no longer significant at week 8.
- **In a second phase, by videoconference**, run during the pandemic, remote
  MBSR was **not** declared noninferior to remote escitalopram. The
  difference between them, however, was not statistically significant.

The limits are clear. It is a single trial. The participants knew what they
were receiving. The remote version did not pass the same test.
"Noninferior" does not mean "better." And 15% of the meditation group
reported some adverse event.

## Where It Didn't Work: Mindfulness in Schools

**Short answer:** a short mindfulness course for *every* student in a
school did not improve adolescents' mental health. **Strength of evidence:
moderate to strong, for the absence of an effect of universal courses with
adolescents.**

A large negative result is worth as much as a positive one, and the field
has one. The MYRIAD trial (Kuyken and colleagues, 2022), in the United
Kingdom, randomly assigned schools to offer either a ten-lesson mindfulness
course or their usual social-emotional teaching. The analysis covered 84 schools and
more than 8,000 students aged 11 to 14. After a year, **there was no
evidence that the course was better** for risk of depression,
social-emotional functioning or well-being. This does not show that
meditation is useless for adolescents. It shows that a short course for
everyone, without selecting those who need it, produces no measurable
benefit.

The study was run by a team sympathetic to mindfulness, with a large,
representative sample. Since then, the null has grown firmer:

- a 2024 reanalysis separated out the adolescents in 22 trials from a large
  earlier meta-analysis: the programs did not improve any outcome;
- two analyses of MYRIAD itself, from 2026, found no effect of the course
  on mental health trajectories; the search for who would benefit found a
  "clinically trivial" difference, in the authors' words.

Whether programs aimed at young people who are already struggling help is
not yet known. A 2022 meta-analysis on children and adolescents in general
rated the evidence as low quality and inconclusive.

## The Brain: The Headline That Didn't Replicate

**Short answer:** the idea that eight weeks of meditation change the
structure of the brain comes from a small study, and the most rigorous test
did not confirm it. **Strength of evidence for structural change from short
programs: contested — the largest trial did not find it.**

In 2011, Britta Hölzel and colleagues published the study the line comes
from: eight weeks of MBSR would increase gray matter density (a term
explained in chapter {{cap:neuroplasticidade-sem-exagero}}) in the
hippocampus and other regions. The study compared 16 MBSR participants with
17 people on a waitlist, with no random assignment between the groups. The
result did not repeat, as the next paragraphs show.

In 2022, a team from Richard Davidson's lab tested the same idea with far
greater rigor (Kral et al. 2022). It randomly assigned 218 people who had
never meditated to three groups: MBSR, a health program with the same
format but no meditation, and a waitlist. Everyone had brain scans before
and after.

The result: **no evidence** that MBSR changed gray matter or the thickness
of the cortex (the brain's outer layer). Neither across the whole brain nor
in the regions singled out by earlier studies. A 2023 meta-analysis that
claimed the opposite was retracted in 2025 for leaving out studies with
null results; the story is in chapter {{cap:neuroplasticidade-sem-exagero}}.

This does not prove that meditation never changes the structure of the
brain. The authors themselves note that much longer or more specific
training might show changes. What it proves is that the "eight weeks"
headline rested on a small study that the more rigorous test did not
confirm.

## Other Outcomes, in One Line

- **Blood pressure:** small trials, mostly of low quality, suggest a
  reduction of a few millimeters of mercury (the most recent review counts the
  creator of one of these programs among its authors). **Preliminary**, and never in
  place of medical treatment; the detail is in
  chapter {{cap:o-corpo-o-sono-e-a-dor}}, along with chronic pain and sleep.
- **Quitting smoking:** the advantage over established treatments comes from
  few studies and appears at the end of treatment, with no demonstrated
  effect on staying smoke-free months later. **Contested**;
  chapter {{cap:atencao-emocao-e-habito}} explains why.

## Adverse Effects Belong in the Count

An assessment that adds up only benefits is incomplete. About the adverse
effects of meditation, three things are documented:

- **They exist**, in clinical trials and in population-based surveys in the
  United States. They range from passing unpleasant experiences to, in a
  minority, impairment at work, in sleep and in relationships.
- **A history of trauma and psychological distress** are linked to higher
  risk.
- **The trials have measured this side poorly**, as the 2022 review of
  reviews showed.

The numbers and the warning signs are in
chapter {{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}}. **Strength of evidence for the
existence of adverse effects: moderate; for their frequency, preliminary.**

## What This Assessment Doesn't License

Three claims circulate widely, and none of them holds up against the data
above:

1. **"Meditation works better than medication."** No. In relapse
   prevention it tied with the antidepressant; in anxiety it was noninferior
   in one trial; in the meta-analyses it ties with established treatments.
2. **"Meditation changes your brain in eight weeks."** The largest and
   best-controlled test found no structural change.
3. **"Meditation is good for everyone."** The average effect is small to moderate, it
   varies between settings, it was zero with adolescents in schools, and
   there are documented adverse effects.

And one that does hold up: a well-run meditation program is a real option,
with a benefit similar to that of other active treatments. For people with
recurrent depression, it is an alternative or a complement with good
evidence. That does not make it a substitute for treatment: anyone with a
diagnosis decides what to do with a health professional.

## What This Chapter Established

- **MBSR** is the eight-week course created by Kabat-Zinn; **MBCT** is its
  adaptation, with cognitive therapy, to prevent **relapse** — the return of
  depression after improvement. Both are secular adaptations of Buddhist
  practices.
- For anxiety, depression and stress, the benefit is **small to moderate**
  (**moderate** evidence).
- Against established treatments, meditation **ties, it does not win**; for
  depression, equivalence with **CBT** has been tested directly.
- The most solid result is relapse prevention in recurrent depression with
  MBCT (38% vs. 49%), with efficacy similar to that of maintenance
  antidepressants (44% vs. 47%); it is in the British depression guideline.
- In a **noninferiority** trial, in-person MBSR was no worse than
  escitalopram for anxiety, with fewer adverse events; the remote version
  did not pass the same test.
- Universal mindfulness in schools had no effect with adolescents.
- The idea that eight weeks of meditation change the structure of the brain
  was not confirmed in the most rigorous test.
- Adverse effects exist and belong in the assessment.
- None of this makes meditation a substitute for treatment.

In one sentence: meditation, in structured programs, is a modest and real
treatment, the size of the other active treatments, not larger, and with
risks that need to be known.

**What is not yet known, and why:**

- **Whether the benefits last** beyond one or two years: few trials follow
  people for longer.
- **Whether more practice produces more benefit:** in a 2017 review, people
  who practiced more at home improved a little more. But that is a
  correlation: people who practice more may differ from those who practice
  less.
- **Who benefits and who is harmed:** in MBCT, people with more symptoms
  seem to benefit more; in schools, the attempt to predict each student's
  response found trivial differences; and there is still no way to know in
  advance who will have adverse effects.
- **Whether apps and remote courses match in-person groups**
  against active treatments: there are few studies of that comparison, and,
  in the trial of MBSR against escitalopram, the remote version did not
  confirm the in-person result.
- **Whether years of intensive practice change the brain** in a way short
  courses do not: studies of long-term practitioners compare people who
  chose to practice. That does not separate the effect of the practice from
  the effect of who chooses to practice.
