This chapter explains the two Buddhist claims that most confuse newcomers:
that everything making up experience is impermanent, and that none of it is a
"self." It walks through the argument of the text where the second idea appears:
what the argument denies, what it doesn't and how the Buddhist schools have
read it. It then presents what philosophy and cognitive science say about the
sense of self. The resemblance between the two is real and interesting. But
it is not enough to say that science has "confirmed" Buddhism.

## The Text: The Second Discourse

The Buddha's first teaching was the one on the Four Noble Truths, covered in
chapter {{cap:as-quatro-nobres-verdades-como-diagnostico}}. According to the
tradition, he gave a second one in the same deer park near Varanasi, to the
same five ascetics. It is the *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta*, "the discourse on the
characteristic of not-self" (*Saṃyutta Nikāya* 22.59). It is short and takes
the form of a dialogue of questions and answers. It is worth following step
by step, because the whole argument is there.

First, two words.

## Anicca: What Changes

**Anicca** means "impermanent": what does not last, what arises and passes.
The word is the negation of *nicca*, "permanent." To say that something is
anicca is not to say that it is bad or illusory. It is to say that it has a
beginning, a duration and an end, and that no part of it is exempt.

One detail matters if we are not to exaggerate. Later, Buddhist schools
developed the doctrine of *momentariness*: the idea that every phenomenon
lasts a single instant and is replaced by another. According to the
philosopher Mark Siderits, this is a later development. As the early suttas
use it, "impermanent" means something simpler and easier to check: that a
thing does not last indefinitely. The body, for example, does not survive
this life.

## The Five Aggregates: An Inventory of a Person

Chapter {{cap:as-quatro-nobres-verdades-como-diagnostico}} mentioned the
**five aggregates** in passing. They are the Buddhist analysis of what makes
up a person: instead of "I," five groups of processes. The Pali word,
*khandha*, works as an umbrella term. Each aggregate gathers every occurrence
of one kind of experience, past, present or future. The order runs from the
coarsest, the body, to the subtlest.

| Aggregate | Pali | In plain words |
|---|---|---|
| Form | *rūpa* | the body and matter; what has weight and takes up space |
| Feeling | *vedanā* | the tone of each experience: pleasant, unpleasant or neutral; the "how" |
| Perception | *saññā* | recognizing and labeling: "this is yellow," "this is a voice"; the "what" |
| Formations | *saṅkhārā* | intentions and volitions: the part of the mind that reacts and has purposes |
| Consciousness | *viññāṇa* | the fact of being aware of something |

Four observations make the table more useful:

- **Vedanā is not emotion.** It is just the tone: the "like" or "don't like"
  that comes with an experience before any story about it. It is the stuff
  of the second arrow in chapter {{cap:as-quatro-nobres-verdades-como-diagnostico}}.
  The first arrow is an unpleasant vedanā; the second is what you do with
  it. Chapter {{cap:observar-sem-agarrar}} comes back to vedanā as a
  practice.
- **Saṅkhārā is the hardest word.** Translations vary: "mental formations,"
  "volitions," *choices* (in Bhikkhu Sujato's version, which this book
  uses). All of them point to
  the active part of the mind, the part that wants and decides.
- **Consciousness is the strongest candidate for "self."** The monk and
  scholar Bhikkhu Anālayo observes that it is the act of being conscious that
  most gives the sense of a cohesive "I" behind experience. That is why the sutta makes a point of
  including it on the list of what is not the self.
- **On the most common reading, the list is complete.** There is no sixth
  item left over called "the one who has the five." That is what the sutta
  goes on to explore.

## The Argument of the Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta

**Anattā** means "not-self": the negation of *attā*, "self." The sutta applies
the word to each of the five aggregates, with two arguments.

**The argument from control.** The text begins like this, for the first
aggregate:

> Mendicants, form is not-self. For if form were self, it wouldn't lead to
> affliction. And you could compel form: "May my form be like this! May it
> not be like that!" But because form is not-self, it leads to affliction.
> And you can't compel form: "May my form be like this! May it not be like
> that!"

And it repeats the same for feeling, perception, formations and
consciousness. The idea behind it is that a "self," in the strong sense,
would be the seat of command. If my body were me, I could decide that it
would not fall ill. I can't. The same goes for the sadness that shows up
uninvited, or the thought that comes back when I wanted it gone.

**The argument from impermanence.** Next the Buddha asks the ascetics, and
they answer:

> "Is form permanent or impermanent?"
> "Impermanent, sir."
> "But if it's impermanent, is it suffering or happiness?"
> "Suffering, sir."
> "But if it's impermanent, suffering, and perishable, is it fit to be
> regarded thus: 'This is mine, I am this, this is my self'?"
> "No, sir."

The structure, as Siderits reconstructs it, is this:

1. if there were a self, it would be permanent;
2. none of the five aggregates is permanent;
3. therefore, none of them is the self.

Notice the threefold formula: "mine," "I am this," "my self." The text does
not just reject the idea of a soul. It rejects three everyday ways of laying
claim to experience: owning it, identifying with it, and taking it as one's
essence.

**The conclusion is practical.** The sutta does not end with a metaphysical
thesis but with an instruction. Each aggregate, "past, future, or present;
internal or external; solid or subtle," is to be seen as "This is not mine,
I am not this, this is not my self." Whoever sees this way, the text says, grows
disillusioned, desire fades away, and they are freed. The sutta ends by
recounting that, while the discourse was being spoken, the minds of the five
ascetics were freed.

A note on context. The "self" the sutta denies is not an invention of the
modern reader. In ancient India, currents of thought linked to the
*Upaniṣads*, ancient texts that Hinduism regards as sacred, held that
suffering comes from ignorance about a person's true identity, the self
(*ātman*). In one version of this idea, the self is pure consciousness, a
witness, identical with the essence of the cosmos.
According to the philosopher Evan Thompson, the self denied in the suttas is
this *ātman*. It is useful to keep that in mind before transplanting the
argument into today's debates.

## What Not-Self Doesn't Say

The doctrine is easy to misread in three directions.

**It does not say that you don't exist.** There is a body, feelings,
perceptions, choices and consciousness, and there is continuity among them.
What the text denies is that, besides all this or behind all this, there is
a fixed and independent owner.

**There is no single reading.** Siderits records a dispute over
interpretation. Some scholars note that the Buddha never denies, in so many
words, the existence of a self that transcends the five aggregates. He only
shows that no aggregate is that self. Against this, all of classical Indian
philosophy, Buddhist and non-Buddhist, understood the Buddha to have denied
the self without qualification. The question is still debated.

**The schools took the idea to different places.** In the Mahāyāna, the
Madhyamaka school, one of the two main schools in India, extended the
argument to everything. Not only the person but all things are **empty** of
a nature of their own: nothing has an essence that exists by itself. This is the doctrine of emptiness mentioned in chapter
{{cap:quem-foi-o-buda-e-quem-ele-nao-foi}}. It is one school's reading, not
the whole of Buddhism's.

There is also a tension the tradition has always known about: classical
Buddhism affirms rebirth. If there is no self, what is reborn? Siderits
observes that the argument from impermanence itself assumes rebirth:
"permanent," there, means lasting beyond a single life. Buddhist answers to
this question fill libraries. This book makes the psychological selection
described in chapter {{cap:quem-foi-o-buda-e-quem-ele-nao-foi}} and does not
try to settle it.

## What Philosophy and Cognitive Science Say

Here the chapter changes register. Up to now, everything has been about what
the texts *claim*. What follows is about what philosophers *argue* and
experiments *show*, and neither was designed to test Buddhism.

### The Self-Model

The German philosopher Thomas Metzinger proposed that the sense of being
someone is the content of a **self-model**: a representation the brain
builds of its own organism. The central point is that this model is
*transparent*. We do not experience it as a model, but as reality itself.
The self, in this theory, is a process, not a thing. Hence Metzinger's
best-known thesis: there is no such thing as a self; there is the model,
which the brain cannot see as a model.

The thesis is philosophical, not a laboratory result. It rests on a fact:
the sense of self emerges from biology and can be manipulated, and even
controlled, in experiments. But the conclusion that "there is no self" is an
interpretation of those data.

### The Construction of the Self

A more modest idea is that of the **construction of the self**. On this
view, the brain continuously assembles our sense of who we are:

- of having a body;
- of being in a place;
- of being the author of our own actions;
- of having a history.

It does this from several sources: vision, touch, signals from inside the
body, memory. And that assembly can be taken apart or fooled under special
conditions. Constructed does not mean
false. A house is constructed too.

The most famous laboratory example is the rubber hand illusion.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** The illusion was described by Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan
Cohen in a one-page letter in *Nature* in 1998. The procedure is simple: the
person's hand is hidden, a rubber hand is in view, and both are stroked with
a brush at the same time. Many participants report feeling the touch on the
rubber hand and feeling that it is "theirs." They also locate their own hand
closer to the fake one than it really is.

**What it found — and the dispute.** In 2020, Peter Lush and colleagues
measured the hypnotic suggestibility of 353 participants and found that it
predicted how strong an illusion they reported. Their conclusion: part of the
effect may come from participants' expectations about what they ought to
feel, and the usual controls do not rule this out. In 2022, a group led by
Henrik Ehrsson replied, in the same journal, that there is no specific
relationship between suggestibility and the illusion. Lush and Anil Seth
responded again.

**Strength of evidence:** moderate for the phenomenon. Many people report
the illusion, and it supports an extensive literature on the sense of body
ownership. Contested as to its interpretation. Does it show how the sense of
"the body that is mine" is assembled, or the power of expectations? The
question is open.
:::

Notice what the illusion shows, at best: that the sense that *this* hand is
*mine* can shift. That is consistent with the idea of a constructed self. It
does not show that there is no self, or that the analysis into five
aggregates is correct.

## Convergence Is Not Confirmation

It is tempting to join the two halves of this chapter in one sentence: "2,500
years ago the Buddha discovered what neuroscience has just proved: the self
is an illusion created by the brain." Thompson criticizes this version, which
he attributes to Buddhist modernism. This book follows the critique on three
points: the first two are his, and the third runs along the same lines.

1. **The concepts are not the same.** The *anattā* of the suttas denies the
   permanent *ātman* of the Upaniṣads, in an argument that assumes rebirth.
   Metzinger's self-model is a hypothesis about representations in the
   brain. Saying that one "confirms" the other treats as identical two ideas
   that were born to answer different questions.
2. **"Not a substance" does not imply "an illusion."** Thompson considers
   Metzinger's notion of the self too restrictive. If only a fixed substance
   could count as a self, then there would be no self. But the self can be a
   *process*. His image is the dance: the dance
   does not exist apart from the dancer and the dancer's movements, and it
   is not an illusion for that.
3. **Finding something in the brain does not settle the question.** That
   the sense of self depends on brain processes is what any scientific
   theory of mind would expect. It does not tell us which conception of the
   self is correct, or what the best way to live with it is. And that is what
   Buddhism cares about.

What remains is a real and limited convergence. Buddhism claims that what we
take to be a fixed self is, looked at closely, a set of changing processes.
Part of philosophy and cognitive science, by its own routes, also treats the
sense of self as something constructed and changeable. The second claim
makes the first worth examining. It does not prove it. Evidence for the
convergence: **preliminary**, and philosophically **contested**.

Two bridges ahead. Chapter {{cap:a-mente-que-divaga}} deals with the
so-called default mode network, a set of brain regions associated with mind
wandering. It shows why it is risky to say that meditating "switches off the
self."

Chapter {{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}} shows the other side. Jared
Lindahl, Willoughby Britton and colleagues (2017) documented difficult
experiences reported by Western meditators. Among them is an entire domain
of changes in the *sense of self*. Across these experiences as a whole, the
impact ranged from very positive to very negative. The distress ranged from
minimal and passing to severe and lasting.
Tampering with the sense of self is not, in every case, a pleasant or
harmless experience.

## What This Chapter Established

- **Anicca** is impermanence: what arises, lasts and passes. The idea that
  everything lasts only an instant is later than the early suttas.
- The **five aggregates** (form, feeling, perception, formations and
  consciousness) are the Buddhist inventory of a person, with no sixth item
  that is "the owner."
- The *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta* argues that no aggregate is the self, because
  none is under our command and because each is impermanent. **Anattā**
  denies a fixed and independent self, not the existence of the person.
- The schools read not-self in different ways; the Madhyamaka extends it to
  all things (emptiness).
- Philosophy and cognitive science speak of a **self-model** and of the
  **construction of the self**. Illusions like the rubber hand show that the
  sense of owning one's body can shift, though the interpretation is
  contested.
- The convergence is real and limited. Science has not "confirmed"
  not-self, and the self can be a process without being an illusion.
