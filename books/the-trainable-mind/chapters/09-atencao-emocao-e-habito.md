This chapter answers a question that sounds simple: when someone meditates,
what exactly are they training? This book's answer is that the training has
three specific targets: attention, the way you handle emotions, and habits.
Each has known mechanisms and evidence of different strength. That is more
useful than saying meditation "changes the brain," which is true of
anything you learn.

## Three Targets, Not "the Brain"

In a 2011 theoretical paper, Britta Hölzel and colleagues proposed that
meditation works through four mechanisms: attention, body awareness,
emotion regulation, and a change in how you see your own self. (This is not
her imaging study from chapter {{cap:neuroplasticidade-sem-exagero}}.) It
is a model, not a result: it organizes hypotheses, and it inherits the
weaknesses of the studies it rests on.

This book uses a simplified version. Body awareness came up in chapter
{{cap:estresse-o-alarme-que-nao-desliga}}, with interoception; perspective
on the self, in chapter {{cap:impermanencia-e-nao-eu}}. That leaves three
targets for this chapter:

| Target | What is trained | The question science asks |
|---|---|---|
| Attention | Holding focus, noticing when it slips, coming back | Does performance on attention tasks improve? Does it improve outside them? |
| Emotion | Relating to what you feel without being swept away | Does emotional reactivity decrease? By what route? |
| Habit | Noticing and interrupting automatic responses | Do automatic behaviors change? How long does it take? |

## Two Styles of Practice

"Meditation" is not one thing. In 2008, Antoine Lutz, Heleen Slagter, John
Dunne and Richard Davidson proposed a distinction that now organizes much
of the research:

- **Focused attention** is directing attention to a chosen object (the
  breath, a sound, a word) and bringing it back every time it leaves. It
  trains three skills: holding the focus, noticing that it has been lost,
  and letting go of the distraction.
- **Open monitoring** is observing, without reacting, whatever arises in
  experience from moment to moment (sounds, sensations, thoughts,
  emotions), without a fixed object. It trains noticing without grabbing
  and without pushing away.

Many methods start with focused attention and move on to open monitoring
later. This book teaches both: attention on the breath in chapter
{{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}} and watching without grabbing in chapter
{{cap:observar-sem-agarrar}}.

The distinction recalls the traditional pair *samatha* and *vipassanā*,
introduced in chapter {{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}}. But
the correspondence is loose. Samatha and vipassanā are categories of a
religious path, with goals defined by the tradition. The other two are
laboratory categories, created to separate what gets measured.

## What Attention Training Has Shown

The short answer has two parts:

- **Months of intensive training improve performance on attention tasks.
  Strength of evidence: moderate.**
- **For ordinary eight-week courses, the evidence is contested.** The gains
  are small, and they shrink or disappear when the comparison is another
  equally engaging activity.

### The Attentional Blink

The **attentional blink** is a well-known limit of human attention. When
two targets appear in a rapid sequence of images, very close together in
time, the second one often goes unnoticed. It is as if attention "blinked"
after capturing the first. Because it is stable and easy to measure, it
became a good test of mental training.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Slagter and colleagues, 2007. Seventeen people were tested
at the beginning and end of a three-month vipassanā retreat, with ten to
twelve hours of meditation a day. The comparison was 23 people interested
in meditation, who had a one-hour class and meditated twenty minutes a day
in the week before each test. The brain's electrical activity (EEG) was
also recorded.

**What it found.** After the retreat, the attentional blink shrank: the
practitioners missed the second target less often. The electrical
recordings suggested that they came to spend fewer resources on the first
target. In general, those who reduced that spending the most improved the
most.

**Strength of evidence:** preliminary. The sample is small, and the groups
were not randomized: people who go on a three-month retreat differ from
people who don't. And the dose, hundreds of hours in three months, says
nothing about ten minutes a day.
:::

### Sustained Attention

The strongest study in this line is the *Shamatha Project* (MacLean and
colleagues, 2010). Participants were randomly assigned: half did a
three-month retreat, with about five hours a day of focused attention, for
example on the breath; the other half waited their turn. The trained group
improved in vigilance, the ability to keep attention on a monotonous
task without performance dropping.

**Strength of evidence: moderate.** There was randomization, but the
control was a waitlist, not an active control. And once again the dose is
that of an intensive retreat.

The same participants were retested up to seven years later. They had kept
their gains *in part*, and those who went on practicing more lost less
attention with age. That is a correlation: people who keep meditating for
years chose to. **Preliminary.**

### What About Small Doses?

With small doses, the studies are weaker. **Preliminary.** The most cited
example is from 2007: Yi-Yuan Tang and colleagues gave Chinese
undergraduates five days of twenty minutes of a body-mind training, drawn
from traditional Chinese medicine and with elements of meditation. Compared
with a randomized group that did relaxation, they improved on an attention
test, in mood, and in cortisol, the stress hormone. But the study is small
and the training very short.

### Ordinary Courses: What the Meta-Analyses Say

For ordinary courses, the best evidence is the meta-analyses, which pool
the controlled studies. They agree that the effect on tests of attention
and memory is small to moderate at most. They disagree on what matters most: whether it holds
up against an active control.

- A 2022 meta-analysis found an advantage over doing nothing, but **no
  advantage over active controls**. Only a minority of the studies it
  pooled were at low risk of bias.
- The largest, from 2024, with more than a hundred trials, found small to
  moderate effects even against active controls. But the effects were
  larger when only the participants who finished the study were counted, a
  sign that the effect may be inflated.

That difference settles the question. Without an active control, you
cannot separate the effect of meditation from the effect of doing something
new, with attention and the expectation of improving. **On balance:**
contested. It is not yet known whether the small gain comes from meditation
or from the conditions that come with it.

## The Transfer Problem

Getting better at a lab task is not the same as paying more attention in
life. In psychology this is called **transfer**: the carrying over of a
gain trained on one task to other tasks.

The best lesson comes from commercial "brain training" games. An extensive
2016 review concluded that they improve performance *on the trained tasks*,
and that there is little evidence of gains in everyday life. Later reviews
went further: transfer to distant tasks is null.

Meditation is not a computer game, but the question applies to it: does
someone who gets better at the attentional-blink test become more attentive
in a difficult conversation? The studies cited here don't say. Meditation
may train something more general, like noticing where attention has gone,
but that has not been tested in real life.

**Strength of evidence for transfer: preliminary.** This is not yet known,
and the history of brain training advises skepticism until someone shows
it.

## Emotion Regulation

**Emotion regulation** is the set of processes by which a person influences
which emotions they have, when they have them, how intensely, and how they
express them. Everyone does this all the time, in different ways:

- **Suppressing:** holding back the expression: swallowing anger, not
  crying. It reduces what shows, but not what is felt, and it impairs
  memory.
- **Reappraising:** changing the interpretation of the situation: "He
  didn't ignore me; he probably didn't see me." It usually works better
  than suppressing.
- **Observing and allowing:** noticing the emotion as an event in the body
  and the mind, without feeding it or pushing it away, until it passes on
  its own.

The third mode is what open-monitoring practices train. It is also the
closest to the Buddhist parable of the second arrow: the emotion is the
first arrow; the fight with it is the second. That is what the text
proposes.

What experiments show is more modest. **Observing with acceptance helps
regulate emotions in experiments, with a small effect. Strength of
evidence: moderate.** A 2025 meta-analysis of more than a hundred studies
found this small effect, and it held up after correction for publication
bias. What counts is *acceptance*: monitoring alone, without accepting,
worked less well than the other strategies.

Mindfulness programs also reduce symptoms of anxiety and depression on
average, with small to moderate effects (the overall picture is in chapter
{{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}). **By what route this
happens is not yet known. Strength of evidence for the mechanisms:
preliminary.** Hölzel's model proposes two routes. One is reappraisal. The
other resembles the exposure used in therapy: staying with a difficult
sensation, without running from it, until it loses its force. The imaging
studies on this are mostly small and subject to the reverse inference
explained in chapter {{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}.

One of the best-designed trials (Goldin and colleagues, 2021) randomly
assigned adults with social anxiety to three groups: MBSR (the mindfulness
course introduced in chapter
{{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}), group
cognitive-behavioral therapy, or a waitlist. Both treatments reduced
negative emotion, with no difference between them, and changed the same
brain responses. Meditation, there, seemed to move the same things as the
therapy.

And a health caveat: anyone with an anxiety disorder or depression should
be treated by a professional. Meditation can be part of care; it is not a
substitute for treatment.

## Habit and Reinforcement Learning

A **habit** is a behavior that fires on its own in response to a context,
without a conscious decision: reaching for your phone as you sit down on
the bus, lighting a cigarette with your coffee. The psychologist Wendy
Wood, one of the leading researchers on the subject, sums up what is known:
habits are associations between a context and a response, formed by
repetition. That is why changing the environment tends to work better than
relying on willpower.

Behind this is **reinforcement learning**: a behavior followed by a reward
tends to be repeated, and one followed by something bad tends to be
avoided. That much is solid.

What is debated is the exact role of dopamine, one of the brain's chemical
messengers. In the 1990s, Wolfram Schultz and colleagues showed that
dopamine neurons in primates seem to signal the **prediction error**: not
the reward itself, but the difference between the expected reward and the
one received. That reading dominated the field for 25 years. In 2022, a
study in animals concluded that dopamine conveys causal associations, not
prediction error. **Strength of evidence for dopamine as a prediction-error
signal: moderate.**

The psychiatrist Judson Brewer has proposed reading the Buddhist notion of
thirst, *taṇhā*, in terms of this loop: a trigger, a behavior, a reward
that reinforces the behavior. Meditation would come in as the ability to
pay attention to the *actual* reward: to notice, for example, that the
cigarette isn't as good as the expectation promised. That would weaken the
reinforcement from the inside.

It is a suggestive reading, with two caveats. First, it is an
*interpretation* of the Buddhist concept, not what the text says. Second,
the book in which Brewer presents it (*The Craving Mind*, 2017) is popular
science, and the author sells apps based on the method, which is a conflict
of interest. Whether the method works is another question, and it now has
an answer.

## What Happened to Mindfulness for Quitting Smoking

**The short answer: a small first trial was encouraging, and the larger
studies did not confirm it. Strength of evidence: contested.**

Brewer's first trial, in 2011, randomly assigned fewer than a hundred
smokers to mindfulness or to a standard program. (It is not the same
group's imaging study, cited in chapter {{cap:a-mente-que-divaga}}.)
Seventeen weeks later, about 3 in 10 people in the mindfulness group were
still not smoking, against fewer than 1 in 10 in the standard program. The
larger studies did not repeat the result:

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Two later evaluations. The first is a Cochrane review (from
an international network known for the rigor of its reviews): Jackson and
colleagues, 2022, with 21 randomized trials and 8,186 people. It counted
only people who were off cigarettes six months or more later. The second is
the *Craving to Quit* trial (Garrison and colleagues, 2020), from Brewer's
own group. It randomly assigned smokers to the mindfulness app or to an app
that only recorded craving and smoking through the day (143 and 182 people
analyzed).

**What it found.** In the review, mindfulness did neither better nor worse
than treatments of equal intensity (risk ratio 0.99, confidence interval
0.67 to 1.46). The authors conclude that they did not detect a clear
benefit. In the app trial, 9.8% were off cigarettes at six months with
mindfulness, and 12.1% in the control, with no difference (p = .51).

**Strength of evidence:** contested. The initial result did not repeat in
the larger trials, nor in the method's developer's own trial. Cochrane
rates the certainty as low or very low, so the picture may still change.
:::

A broad 2022 review, by researchers from inside the field, records a small
advantage of mindfulness for smoking. There is no contradiction: that
advantage comes from a few studies and was measured at the end of
treatment. On staying off cigarettes months later, there is no
demonstrated effect.

For alcohol and drugs, another Cochrane review, from 2021, judged the
evidence uncertain. The best result is that of a mixed program, MORE,
which combines mindfulness, reappraisal and savoring good experiences. In a
trial of people with chronic pain who were misusing opioids, nearly twice
as many stopped misusing, compared with supportive therapy. But the first
author is the program's developer and declares income from it; more than a
third left the study; and MORE is not meditation alone. **Preliminary.**

Anyone with an addiction should be treated by a professional. Meditation is
not a substitute for treatment.

## How Long It Takes: The 21-Day Myth

"It takes 21 days to form a habit" is one of the most repeated lines about
behavior change. There is no basis for it. According to a 2012 paper by
researchers from the same group as the study below, the number seems to
have come from accounts of plastic surgery patients. They tended to get
used to their new appearance in about 21 days, which has nothing to do with
forming a habit.

What has been measured is something else:

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Lally and colleagues, 2010. Ninety-six volunteers chose a
simple behavior (eating, drinking or doing something) to repeat every day
in the same context ("after breakfast") for twelve weeks. Each day, they
rated how automatic it felt. Eighty-two provided enough data. The
mathematical curve fit the data of 62 people, and fit well for 39.

**What it found.** The time to reach 95% of maximum automaticity ranged
from **18 to 254 days**. Missing one opportunity to perform the behavior
did not meaningfully disrupt the process. Automaticity rises quickly at
first and then levels off.

**Strength of evidence:** preliminary for any specific number (small
sample, self-rated automaticity, good fit in only 39 people); moderate for
the general conclusion that forming a habit takes months and varies widely
across people and behaviors.
:::

The 2012 paper summarizes the study by saying that automaticity leveled off
after an average of about 66 days. That number has already become a new
slogan ("66 days to a habit"), and it shouldn't have. The study's message
is the *range*, not the average.

**More recent data confirm this reading. Strength of evidence: moderate.**
Forming a habit takes about two months, often longer, and depends on the
person and the behavior. A 2024 review found the same center and an
individual range from a few days to nearly a year, but most of the studies
it pooled were at high risk of bias. With objective records rather than
self-report, a 2023 study found that the gym habit took months, and the
hospital handwashing habit, weeks.

For anyone who wants to build a meditation practice, this has three
consequences, taken up again in chapter {{cap:uma-pratica-para-a-vida}}:

- the same time and the same place help, because a habit is an association
  with a context;
- missing a day doesn't reset anything;
- the sense of effort can last for months before it eases, without that
  meaning something has gone wrong.

Effort is one thing; suffering that grows with practice is another. The
adverse effects of meditation, and when to stop, are in chapter
{{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}}.

## What This Chapter Established

- Meditation trains specific targets: **attention**, **emotion
  regulation** and **habits**. That is more precise than saying it "changes
  the brain."
- **Focused attention** (holding focus on an object) and **open
  monitoring** (observing whatever arises without reacting) are the two
  basic styles. They recall samatha and vipassanā, but they are not the
  same thing.
- Months of intensive training improve performance on attention tasks,
  such as the **attentional blink** and vigilance (moderate). The gains last
  in part for those who keep practicing (preliminary).
- In ordinary eight-week programs, the gains on cognitive tests are small
  and disappear or shrink against an active control (contested).
  **Transfer** to daily life has not been shown (preliminary).
- In **emotion regulation**, observing with acceptance works in
  experiments, with a small effect (moderate). By what route meditation
  changes emotional life is not yet known (preliminary).
- **Habits** are associations between context and response, formed by
  **reinforcement learning**. Whether dopamine signals **prediction error**
  is debated (moderate). Reading taṇhā as a reinforcement loop is an
  interpretation, not a finding.
- At keeping people off cigarettes, mindfulness has done no better than
  standard treatments (contested).
- A habit doesn't form in 21 days, nor in exactly 66: it usually takes
  about two months, with a range from days to nearly a year, and missing a
  day doesn't undo the process (moderate).
- Open questions: whether any cognitive effect is specific to meditation;
  whether lab gains reach real life; which mechanism (attention,
  acceptance, exposure, reappraisal) carries the emotional benefits; and
  what signal dopamine actually carries.
