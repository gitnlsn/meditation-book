This chapter answers a question that sounds simple: when someone meditates,
what exactly are they training? This book's answer is that the training has
specific targets (attention, the way you handle emotions, and habits) and
that each of them has known mechanisms and evidence of different strength.
That is more useful than saying meditation "changes the brain," which is
true of anything you learn. By the end, you should know what *focused
attention* and *open monitoring* mean, what the attention studies showed
and what they didn't, and why a habit doesn't form in 21 days.

## Three Targets, Not "the Brain"

In 2011, the researcher Britta Hölzel and colleagues, in a theoretical
paper (not her imaging study cited in chapter
{{cap:neuroplasticidade-sem-exagero}}), proposed a scheme that became a
reference in the field: mindfulness meditation would work through
mechanisms that interact with one another, namely attention regulation,
body awareness, emotion regulation, and change in perspective on the self.
It is a theoretical model, not a result: it organizes hypotheses, and the
claims about the brain it cites inherit the weaknesses of the studies they
come from.

This book uses a simplified version of the scheme. Body awareness came up
in chapter {{cap:estresse-o-alarme-que-nao-desliga}}, with interoception;
perspective on the self, in chapter {{cap:impermanencia-e-nao-eu}}. That
leaves three targets for this chapter:

| Target | What is trained | The question science asks |
|---|---|---|
| Attention | Holding focus, noticing when it slips, coming back | Does performance on attention tasks improve? Does it improve outside them? |
| Emotion | Relating to what you feel without being swept away | Does emotional reactivity decrease? By what route? |
| Habit | Noticing and interrupting automatic responses | Do automatic behaviors change? How long does it take? |

## Two Styles of Practice

Before looking at the results, you need to know that "meditation" is not
one thing. In 2008, Antoine Lutz, Heleen Slagter, John Dunne and Richard
Davidson proposed a distinction that now organizes much of the research:

- **Focused attention** is deliberately directing attention to a chosen
  object (the breath, a sound, a word) and bringing it back every time it
  leaves. The training lies in three skills: holding the focus, noticing
  that it has been lost, and letting go of the distraction.
- **Open monitoring** is observing, without reacting, whatever arises in
  experience from moment to moment (sounds, sensations, thoughts, emotions)
  without choosing a fixed object. The training lies in noticing without
  grabbing and without pushing away.

In practice, many methods start with focused attention and move on to open
monitoring later. This book teaches both: attention on the breath in
chapter {{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}} and watching without grabbing in
chapter {{cap:observar-sem-agarrar}}.

The distinction recalls the traditional pair *samatha* and *vipassanā*,
introduced in chapter {{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}}, and
is sometimes described as the scientific version of it. The correspondence
is loose. Samatha and vipassanā are categories of a religious path, with
goals defined by the tradition; focused attention and open monitoring are
laboratory categories, created to separate what gets measured. One helps
you think about the other, but they are not the same thing.

## What Attention Training Has Shown

### The Attentional Blink

The **attentional blink** is a well-known limit of human attention: when
two targets appear in a rapid sequence of images, very close together in
time, the second one often goes unnoticed. It is as if attention "blinked"
after capturing the first. The phenomenon is stable and easy to measure,
which made it a good test of mental training.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Slagter and colleagues, University of Wisconsin, published
in *PLoS Biology* in 2007. Seventeen people were tested at the beginning
and end of a three-month vipassanā retreat, with ten to twelve hours of
meditation a day. The comparison group was 23 people interested in
meditation, who had a one-hour class and meditated twenty minutes a day in
the week before each test. Besides performance, the researchers recorded
the brain's electrical activity (EEG).

**What it found.** After the retreat, the attentional blink shrank: the
practitioners missed the second target less often. The electrical
recordings suggested that they came to spend fewer resources on the first
target, and those who reduced that spending the most were, in general, the
ones who improved the most.

**Strength of evidence:** preliminary. The sample is small, the groups were
not randomized (people who go on a three-month retreat differ from people
who don't), and the dose, hundreds of hours of practice in three months,
says nothing about ten minutes a day.
:::

### Sustained Attention

The most robust study in this line is the *Shamatha Project*. Katherine
MacLean and colleagues (2010) randomly assigned 60 people to two groups of
30: one did the retreat first, the other waited and served as a control.
The training was about five hours a day for three months, with
focused-attention practice, for example on the breath. The trained group
improved in visual discrimination and in vigilance, the ability to keep
attention on a monotonous task for a long time without performance
dropping. **Strength of evidence: moderate**: there was randomization, but
the control was a waitlist, not an active control, and once again the dose
is that of an intensive retreat.

### What About Small Doses?

There are studies with much smaller doses, and they are weaker. Yi-Yuan
Tang and colleagues (2007) gave 40 Chinese university students five days of
twenty minutes of a meditation method, and compared them with a group,
chosen the same way, that did relaxation training. The meditation group
improved more on a test of executive attention (the ability to resolve
conflicts between stimuli), in mood, and in stress-related cortisol. It is
an interesting result, but too small and too short to support much.
**Preliminary.**

In a study of US military personnel before a deployment, Amishi Jha and
colleagues (2010) saw working memory (the ability to hold and manipulate
information for a few seconds) decline under stress in the group without
training. In the group that took an eight-week course, it declined in those
who practiced little and rose in those who practiced a lot. The idea that
dose matters is plausible, but the study did not randomize the groups, and
the link with practice time is a correlation: people who practice more may
differ from people who practice less for other reasons. **Preliminary.**

**On balance:** intensive meditation training improves performance on
attention tasks. **Strength of evidence: moderate.** For short doses, like
those of an app, the evidence is **preliminary**.

## The Transfer Problem

Getting better at a lab task is not the same as paying more attention in
life. Psychology has a name for this distinction: **transfer**, the carrying
over of a gain trained on one task to other tasks.

The best lesson comes from outside meditation. In 2016, an extensive review
of commercial "brain training" programs (Simons and colleagues) concluded
that there is extensive evidence that they improve performance *on the
trained tasks themselves*, less evidence of improvement on similar tasks,
and little evidence of improvement on different tasks or in everyday
cognitive performance. The review also found design or analysis flaws in
many of the studies the companies themselves cite.

Meditation is not a computer game, and the conclusion can't be carried
over automatically. But the question applies to it too: when a
practitioner gets better at the attentional-blink test, does that make them
more attentive in a difficult conversation? The studies cited here don't
say. The most reasonable hypothesis is that meditation trains something
more general than a game (the habit of noticing where attention has gone),
but that still needs to be shown with real-life measures. **Strength of
evidence for transfer: preliminary.**

## Emotion Regulation

**Emotion regulation** is the set of processes by which a person influences
which emotions they have, when they have them, how intensely, and how they
express them. Everyone regulates emotions all the time, in different ways:

- **Suppressing:** holding back the expression: swallowing anger, not
  crying. It works on the outside; on the inside, the emotion goes on.
- **Reappraising:** changing the interpretation of the situation: "He
  didn't ignore me; he probably didn't see me."
- **Observing and allowing:** noticing the emotion as an event in the body
  and the mind, without feeding it or pushing it away, until it passes on
  its own.

The third mode is what open-monitoring practices train, and it is the
closest to what Buddhism describes in the parable of the second arrow: the
emotion is the first arrow; the fight with it is the second. Hölzel and
colleagues' model proposes that meditation works both through reappraisal
and through something like the exposure used in therapy: staying with a
difficult sensation without running from it, until it loses its force.

Here it is necessary to be frank about the state of the evidence. There are
good trials showing that mindfulness programs reduce symptoms of anxiety
and depression on average, with small to moderate effects; the overall
picture is in chapter {{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}.
What is much less established is *by what route* this happens. Brain-imaging
studies of emotion regulation in meditators are mostly small and subject to
the reverse inference explained in chapter
{{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}: seeing a region "light up" does not, by
itself, tell you the person was regulating an emotion. **Strength of
evidence for the mechanisms: preliminary.**

And a health caveat: anyone with an anxiety disorder or depression should
be treated by a professional. Meditation can be part of care; it is not a
substitute for treatment.

## Habit and Reinforcement Learning

A **habit** is a behavior that fires on its own in response to a context,
without a conscious decision: reaching for your phone as you sit down on the
bus, lighting a cigarette with your coffee. The psychologist Wendy Wood, one
of the leading researchers on the subject, sums up what science knows:
habits are associations between a context and a response, formed by
repetition, and that is why changing the environment tends to work better
than relying on willpower (Wood 2019).

Behind this is **reinforcement learning**: the process by which a behavior
followed by a reward tends to be repeated, and a behavior followed by
something bad tends to be avoided. In the 1990s, Wolfram Schultz and
colleagues showed that dopamine neurons in primates seem to signal not the
reward itself but the *prediction error*: the difference between the
expected reward and the one received (Schultz, Dayan & Montague 1997). That
is why reinforcement is stronger when there is surprise, and it is one of
the most solid foundations of the neuroscience of learning.

The psychiatrist Judson Brewer has proposed reading the Buddhist notion of
thirst, *taṇhā*, in terms of this loop: a trigger, a behavior, a reward
that reinforces the behavior. Meditation would come in as the ability to
pay attention to the *actual* reward (to notice, for example, that the
cigarette isn't as good as the expectation promised), which would weaken the
reinforcement from the inside. It is a suggestive reading, but three
caveats come with it. First, it is an *interpretation* of the Buddhist
concept, not what the text says. Second, the book in which Brewer presents
it (*The Craving Mind*, 2017) is popular science, and the author sells apps
based on the method, which is a conflict of interest. Third, the
experimental base is small: in a trial with 88 smokers, mindfulness
training had 36% abstinence at the end of treatment against 15% for the
standard program (a difference that did not reach statistical
significance) and 31% against 6% seventeen weeks later (this one
significant) (Brewer et al. 2011, a different study from the same group,
not the imaging study cited in chapter {{cap:a-mente-que-divaga}}). It is a
promising result from a single small study. **Preliminary.**

## How Long It Takes: The 21-Day Myth

"It takes 21 days to form a habit" is one of the most repeated lines about
behavior change. There is no basis for it. According to Benjamin Gardner,
Phillippa Lally and Jane Wardle (2012), the number seems to have come from
accounts of plastic surgery patients, who tended to get used to their new
appearance in about 21 days, something that has nothing to do with forming
a habit.

What has been measured is something else:

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Lally and colleagues, published in the *European Journal of
Social Psychology* in 2010. Ninety-six volunteers chose a simple behavior
(eating, drinking or doing something) to repeat every day in the same
context ("after breakfast") for twelve weeks, rating each day how automatic
it felt. Eighty-two provided enough data; the mathematical model fit the
data of 62, and fit well for 39.

**What it found.** The time to reach 95% of maximum automaticity ranged
from **18 to 254 days**. Missing one opportunity to perform the behavior
did not meaningfully disrupt the process. Automaticity rises quickly at
first and then levels off.

**Strength of evidence:** preliminary for any specific number (small
sample, self-rated automaticity, good fit in only 39 people); moderate for
the general conclusion that forming a habit takes months and varies widely
across people and behaviors.
:::

Gardner, Lally and Wardle summarize the study by saying that automaticity
leveled off after an average of about 66 days. That number has already
become a new slogan ("66 days to a habit"), and it shouldn't have: the
study's message is the *range*, not the average.

For anyone who wants to build a meditation practice, this has three
practical consequences, taken up again in chapter
{{cap:uma-pratica-para-a-vida}}: the same time and the same place help,
because a habit is an association with a context; missing a day doesn't
reset anything; and the sense of effort can last for months before it eases,
without that meaning something has gone wrong.

## What This Chapter Established

- Meditation trains specific targets (**attention**, **emotion
  regulation** and **habits**), and talking about these targets is more
  precise than saying it "changes the brain."
- **Focused attention** (holding focus on an object and returning to it)
  and **open monitoring** (observing whatever arises without reacting) are
  the two basic styles. They recall samatha and vipassanā, but they are not
  the same thing.
- Intensive training improves performance on attention tasks, such as the
  **attentional blink** and vigilance (moderate). For short doses, the
  evidence is preliminary, and transfer to daily life has not yet been
  well shown.
- **Emotion regulation** can be suppression, reappraisal or observation.
  The programs reduce symptoms on average; the mechanisms are preliminary.
- **Habits** are associations between context and response, formed by
  **reinforcement learning**. Reading taṇhā as a reinforcement loop is
  suggestive, and its experimental base is small.
- A habit doesn't form in 21 days: in the most cited study, the time ranged
  from 18 to 254 days, and missing a day didn't undo the process.
