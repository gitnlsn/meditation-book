This chapter answers a question that holds up the rest of the book: does the
adult brain change with what we do? Yes, and that is well demonstrated. But
it changes in a way that is more modest, slower and harder to measure than
popular books say. By the end, you should know what neuroplasticity is, what
kind of change has been demonstrated, and why "meditation rewires your brain
in eight weeks" doesn't hold up.

## The Parts: Neurons and Synapses

The brain has many kinds of cells. The ones that matter here are
**neurons**: cells specialized in receiving and transmitting signals. Each
neuron receives messages from others. When the total crosses a certain
threshold, it fires an electrical signal that travels onward.

The point where one neuron passes the message to another is called a
**synapse**. At most synapses the two neurons don't touch: the first releases
chemicals into a tiny gap, and the second picks them up. A synapse can be
strong (the message gets through easily) or weak (it gets through poorly).

That strength can change, and that is what matters. If the strength of
synapses changes, the route signals take changes, and with it what the brain
does. Most learning happens at this scale.

## What "Neuroplasticity" Means

**Neuroplasticity** is the nervous system's capacity to change its structure
and function with use, experience and learning. The word covers very
different scales: a single synapse, new connections, the volume of an entire
region. When someone says "neuroplasticity," it is worth asking: at what
scale?

The central idea comes from the Canadian psychologist Donald Hebb, in 1949:
when one neuron repeatedly helps make another fire, the connection between
the two grows stronger. This is **Hebbian learning**: connections that are
used together are reinforced. The slogan "neurons that fire together wire
together" is a later paraphrase, not Hebb's own sentence, but it sums up the
idea well.

Hebb proposed this as a theory. Later, experiments in animals found
mechanisms at the synapse that work as he predicted. That synapses change
with use, and that this underlies memory and learning, is one of the firmest
findings in neuroscience. Strength of evidence: strong.

Day to day, this means that every time you learn a name, a route or a
recipe, your brain has changed. Neuroplasticity is not a special power
switched on by techniques. It is how the brain normally works. That is why
"meditation causes neuroplasticity" is true and nearly empty: anything you
learn does.

## A Myth to Clear Away: The 10 Percent

The idea that we use only 10% of the brain often comes along with promises
to "unlock your potential." It is false. Strength of evidence: strong
(scientific consensus).

The brain makes up about 3% of the body's weight but uses about 20% of its
energy. And most of it is active almost all the time. There is no dormant
90% waiting for a technique to wake it up.

## What a Scan Shows: Gray Matter

Change at the synapses is microscopic and invisible to any scan done on a
living person. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) measures something much
coarser: the volume or thickness of entire regions.

The most widely used measure is **gray matter**: the tissue where the cell
bodies of neurons and a large share of the synapses sit. When a study says
that "meditation increased gray matter," it means that, in some region, the
analysis software estimated slightly more of this tissue.

The problem is the gap between the two scales. A critical review from 2013
pointed out that the changes training causes in animals are microscopic, and
that MRI's resolution is too low to see them reliably. The authors concluded
that flaws of design, statistics and method may explain many of the effects
reported in humans. The review is itself part of an open debate, but it gives
a measure of the caution needed.

## London's Taxi Drivers

The most famous example of structural change in the adult brain comes from
London's taxi drivers. To get their license, they have to memorize the layout
of thousands of streets, in training that takes years. Strength of evidence:
moderate.

The region in question is the **hippocampus**: a curved structure, one on
each side of the brain, deep inside, behind the temples. It is essential for
forming new memories and for finding your way in space: remembering where
things are and how to get from one place to another.

The research of the neuroscientist Eleanor Maguire came in three steps, and
each one teaches something:

1. **In 2000**, she compared taxi drivers with men who did not drive taxis.
   In the taxi drivers, the back part of the hippocampus was larger and the
   front part smaller. The more years on the job, the stronger the pattern.
   But a snapshot of a single moment does not show the cause: perhaps people
   with a hippocampus like that find it easier to become taxi drivers.
2. **In 2006**, she compared taxi drivers with bus drivers, who have driven
   as long as they have, with similar stress, but always on the same routes.
   The difference showed up again in the taxi drivers. And it came at a cost:
   they were *worse* at learning new visual and spatial information.
   Plasticity is not only gain; it can be redistribution.
3. **In 2011** came the missing study: following the same people over time.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Katherine Woollett and Eleanor Maguire (2011) examined 79 men
at the start of taxi-driver training and 31 men who were not in training.
They repeated the scans three to four years later.

**What it found.** Of the 79 trainees, 39 qualified. Only in them did gray
matter increase in the back part of the hippocampus, along with changes in
memory. In the 20 who did not qualify and came back for the second scan, and
in the controls, there was no change. At the start, the groups did not
differ.

**Strength of evidence:** moderate. It is the best human evidence that
intensive learning changes the structure of the adult brain. But there was no
random assignment (no one can be assigned by lot to become a taxi driver),
the sample is a few dozen people, and the timescale is years of daily
training, not weeks.
:::

## Juggling: What Comes Doesn't Always Go

Shorter training also changes what MRI shows, but the change can recede.
Strength of evidence: preliminary.

In a 2004 study, adults who had never juggled learned to keep three balls in
the air. After a few months, MRI showed more gray matter in areas involved in
the visual perception of motion. Months after they stopped, the increase had
shrunk. The study is small and depends on MRI, with all the limits described
above.

This does not mean that "the brain gives back what it stops using." A
theoretical model from 2017 proposes that volume grows at the start of
learning and then returns, wholly or partly, to where it began. Meanwhile, a
selection process would keep what is useful. If so, volume that recedes is
not a lost skill, and volume says little about what was learned.

## New Neurons? A Controversy That Has Moved

Whether adult humans produce new neurons is still contested. The most recent
evidence leans toward yes, at a low level. And no study has measured the
effect of meditation on it.

The production of new neurons is called **neurogenesis**. In adult rodents
and monkeys, it continues in a small part of the hippocampus. The question is
whether this happens in adult humans, and in what quantity.

In 2018, two studies of human brain tissue gave opposite answers. One found
no young neurons in the adult hippocampus. The other found thousands, at
every age. Part of the fight was about method: the marker used to recognize
new neurons also shows up in mature neurons. The strongest proof, one of the
groups said, would be to catch the cells that give rise to neurons at the
moment they divide.

In 2025, a study reported exactly that in the hippocampus of adults. The
figure shows the path there.

![The debate over new neurons in the adult human hippocampus, from 2018 to 2025, study by study.](../illustrations/figures/fig-neurogenese-en.svg)

What is not yet known is how many new neurons an adult makes, and whether
they play any role in memory or mood. The methods still give different
answers. Strength of evidence: contested. And "meditation grows new neurons"
claims something no one has shown.

## "Rewire Your Brain in Eight Weeks"

This is the promise that circulates most, and it is contested: the most
rigorous test did not confirm it.

The promise grew out of small studies. They found more gray matter in some
regions after MBSR, an eight-week meditation course (chapter
{{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}} explains the program).
The most cited, by Britta Hölzel and colleagues (2011), compared a few dozen
people with a waitlist group, without random assignment. It is the kind of
design that chapter {{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}} showed to be weak.

In 2022, Richard Davidson's lab tested the same idea with far greater rigor.
It was a randomized trial with more than two hundred people, an active
control and a waitlist. No structural change attributable to MBSR appeared,
not even in the regions flagged before. Hölzel's findings did not replicate
(details in chapter {{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}).

The authors themselves suggested that perhaps only much longer training, or
training focused on a single practice, changes structure. A trial from the
same year, Age-Well, tested much longer training: eighteen months of
meditation in older adults. Volume did not change. But meditation beat the
comparison group's English classes on a measure of attention, emotion and
self-knowledge.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** The Age-Well trial, in France (Chételat and colleagues, 2022).
137 adults aged 65 or older, without cognitive impairment, were assigned by
lot to eighteen months of meditation (n = 45), eighteen months of English
classes with the same structure (n = 46), or no intervention (n = 46). Both
trainings had two-hour weekly sessions, daily home practice of 20 minutes or
more, and days of intensive practice. All but one completed the trial. The
main measures were the volume and blood flow of two cortical regions, the
anterior cingulate and the insula.

**What it found.** No difference in volume change in either region (p = 0.36
and p = 0.58). The differences in blood flow did not reach statistical
significance (p = 0.06 and p = 0.09). But meditation beat the English
classes on a combined measure of attention regulation, socioemotional
capacities and self-knowledge (*d* = 0.52).

**Strength of evidence:** moderate, for the conclusion that this program did
not change the volume of these regions. It is a single trial, only in older
adults, that looked at only two regions.
:::

The strongest positive result to set beside this one is still preliminary.
In a nine-month study, the ReSource Project, each training module was
followed by different changes in cortical thickness. Those changes tracked
improvements in attention, compassion and the ability to take another's
perspective. But it is one study, from a single group, that compared the
modules with one another. Strength of evidence: preliminary.

## The Meta-Analysis That Was Withdrawn

The only meta-analysis that said mindfulness trials change brain structure
was withdrawn by its journal. It should not be cited as evidence.

Published in 2023, it stated in its title that the trials "led to brain
structural changes." In 2025 the journal withdrew the article, which is
called a **retraction**. According to the notice, the analysis had left out
four studies that found no effect, with 40% of the participants. It had also
included studies done in very specific populations. So it did not test the
question it claimed to test. The authors disagree with the retraction.

This is the publication bias of chapter {{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}
in its most direct form. And the article still circulates.

## And People Who Have Meditated for Years?

People who have meditated for years have, on average, some brain regions
that differ from those of people who don't. But that does not prove
meditation caused the difference. Strength of evidence for "meditation slows
brain aging": preliminary.

A 2014 meta-analysis pooled studies with about 300 practitioners. It found
differences in eight regions, and an average effect of moderate size. The
authors themselves called publication bias and methodological limits "strong
concerns." And most of these studies compare, at a single moment, people who
meditate with people who don't. They cannot separate the effect of practice
from differences between those who choose to meditate and those who don't.

That is the source of the headline that meditators have brains "7.5 years
younger." A 2016 study used a computer program that estimates, from
anatomical signs, how old a brain looks. It compared 50 long-term meditators
with 50 non-meditators. At age fifty, the meditators' brains looked 7.5
years younger.
Three things call for caution. The sample is small. The people chose to
meditate, and comparisons like this tend to inflate effects (chapter
{{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}). And Age-Well, which assigned by lot who
would meditate, found no gain in volume.

## The Verdict

The claim that eight weeks of MBSR change the structure of the brain is
contested, for a concrete reason. The largest and most rigorous trial found
no change, and the only meta-analysis that said otherwise was retracted.
Eighteen months of meditation in older adults did not change the volume of
the regions studied either.

Whether years of intensive practice change brain structure is not yet known.
No randomized trial has tested it, and studies of long-time practitioners
cannot separate cause from selection.

This does not mean meditation "does nothing to the brain." If it changes
attention or habits, it probably changes synapses, at a scale MRI cannot
reach; that is an inference, not a measurement. Small changes in how regions
work together have been measured after mindfulness training (preliminary;
chapter {{cap:a-mente-que-divaga}}). And what MBSR does for health is a
different question, with a different answer.

## What This Means for People Who Meditate

Neuroplasticity is the reason it makes sense to talk about *training* the
mind. Because the brain changes with use, bringing attention back to the
breath or noticing a reaction before following it can leave a trace.

But a change in the brain is not the benefit. What matters is whether the
person pays more attention, suffers less, sleeps better. Age-Well showed
benefit without any change in volume. And plasticity has no moral direction:
repeated worry also trains the brain, as chapter {{cap:a-mente-que-divaga}}
shows.

## What This Chapter Established

- **Synapses**, the points of contact between **neurons**, change in
  strength with use (strong). This **neuroplasticity** is how the brain
  normally works, not a power of meditation; **Hebbian learning** is its
  central idea. We do not use only 10% of the brain.
- Changes in **gray matter** visible on MRI exist, but they are small and
  fragile to measure (moderate). The best example, the **hippocampus** of
  London's taxi drivers, took years.
- Whether adult humans produce new neurons (**neurogenesis**) is still
  contested; the evidence from 2022 to 2025 leans toward a low level. There
  is no basis for saying meditation creates neurons.
- Structural change from eight weeks of MBSR: contested. The largest trial
  did not find it; eighteen months of meditation in older adults brought
  measured benefits without changing the volume of the regions studied; the
  meta-analysis that said otherwise underwent a **retraction**.
- The "younger brain" of long-term meditators comes from small comparisons
  of people who chose to meditate (preliminary).

In one sentence: meditating is learning, and learning changes the brain, but
that change does not show up reliably on structural scans; the benefits are
measured in behavior and health. Three questions remain open: whether years
of intensive practice change brain structure, whether the small changes in
brain function explain any benefit, and how much adult neurogenesis exists
and what it is for.
