This chapter answers a question that holds up the rest of the book: does the
adult brain change with what we do? The answer is yes, and it is solid. But
the brain changes in a way that is more modest, slower and harder to measure
than popular books and headlines say. By the end, you should know what
neuroplasticity is, what kind of change is well demonstrated and what kind
isn't, and why "meditation rewires your brain in eight weeks" is a claim that
doesn't hold up.

## The Parts: Neurons and Synapses

The brain is made of many kinds of cells. The ones that matter most here are
**neurons**: cells specialized in receiving and transmitting signals. Each
neuron receives messages from others, integrates them and, when the total
crosses a certain threshold, fires an electrical signal that travels onward.

The point where one neuron passes the message to another is called a
**synapse**. At most synapses the two neurons don't touch: the first releases
chemicals into a tiny gap, and the second picks them up. A synapse can be
strong (the message gets through easily) or weak (it gets through poorly),
and that strength can change.

That last detail is the one that matters. If the strength of synapses
changes, the route signals take changes, and with it what the brain does.
That is where, at the scale of synapses, most learning happens.

## What "Neuroplasticity" Means

**Neuroplasticity** is the nervous system's capacity to change its structure
and function in response to use, experience and learning. The word covers
things at very different scales: the strength of a single synapse, the
growth of new connections, the volume of an entire region. When someone says
"neuroplasticity," it is worth asking: at what scale?

The central idea has a precise origin. In 1949, the Canadian psychologist
Donald Hebb proposed, in his book *The Organization of Behavior*, that when
one neuron repeatedly helps make another fire, the connection between the
two grows stronger. This is **Hebbian learning**: connections that are used
together are reinforced. The slogan "neurons that fire together wire
together" is a later paraphrase, not Hebb's own sentence, but it sums up the
idea well.

Hebb proposed this as a theory. In the decades that followed, experiments in
animals found mechanisms at the synapse that work much as he predicted. That
synapses change with use, and that this underlies memory and learning, is one
of the firmest findings in neuroscience. Strength of evidence: strong.

Notice what this means day to day. Every time you learn a name, a route or a
recipe, your brain has changed. Neuroplasticity is not a special power
switched on by techniques: it is how the brain normally works. That is why
the sentence "meditation causes neuroplasticity" is at once true and nearly
empty. Anything you learn does.

## A Myth to Clear Away: The 10 Percent

Before going on, a myth that often shows up alongside promises to "unlock the
brain's potential": the idea that we use only 10% of it. It is false. The
neurologist Barry Gordon, of Johns Hopkins University, told *Scientific
American* (2008) that the myth is "so wrong it is almost laughable." The
brain makes up about 3% of the body's weight and uses about 20% of the body's
energy, and most of it is active almost all the time. There is no dormant 90%
waiting for a technique to wake it up. Strength of evidence: strong
(scientific consensus).

## What a Scan Shows: Gray Matter

Change at the synapses happens on a microscopic scale, invisible to any scan
done on a living person. What magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can measure is
something much coarser: the volume or thickness of entire regions.

The most widely used measure is **gray matter**: the tissue where the cell
bodies of neurons and a large share of the synapses sit, as opposed to the
"white matter," made of the long fibers that link distant regions. When a
study says that "meditation increased gray matter," it means that, in a
certain region, the analysis software estimated slightly more of this
tissue.

The problem, pointed out in a critical review by Cibu Thomas and Chris Baker
(2013), is the gap between the two scales. In animals, the changes caused by
training are microscopic, such as alterations in dendritic spines — small
protrusions on neurons where synapses form. MRI's resolution is far too low
to see this, and it is not clear that it can reliably detect what happens at
that scale. The authors concluded that limitations of design, of statistics
and methodological artifacts may lie behind many of the effects reported in
humans. The result they considered most robust was an increase in
hippocampal volume with physical exercise in older people. The review is
itself part of an open debate, but it gives a measure of the caution needed.

## London's Taxi Drivers

The most famous example of structural change in the adult brain comes from an
unexpected place: London's taxis. To get their license, the city's taxi
drivers have to memorize the layout of thousands of streets, in training that
takes years.

The region in question is the **hippocampus**: a curved structure, one on
each side of the brain, on the inner part of the temporal lobes (behind the
temples). It is essential for forming new memories and for navigating space —
remembering where things are and how to get from one place to another.

The research of the neuroscientist Eleanor Maguire came in three steps, and
each one teaches something:

1. **In 2000**, she compared 16 licensed taxi drivers with 50 men who did not
   drive taxis. In the taxi drivers, the back part of the hippocampus was
   larger and the front part smaller. The more years on the job, the stronger
   the pattern. But this study is a snapshot of a single moment: perhaps
   people with hippocampi like that find it easier to become taxi drivers.
2. **In 2006**, she compared taxi drivers with bus drivers, who have
   comparable driving experience and stress levels but always drive the same
   routes. The hippocampal difference showed up again in the taxi drivers.
   And it came at a cost: the taxi drivers were *worse* than the bus drivers
   at learning new visual and spatial information. Plasticity is not only
   gain; it can be redistribution.
3. **In 2011** came the missing study: following the same people over time.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Katherine Woollett and Eleanor Maguire, University College
London, published in *Current Biology* in 2011. They examined 79 men at the
start of taxi-driver training and 31 men who were not in training, and
repeated the scans three to four years later.

**What it found.** Of the 79 trainees, 39 qualified. Only in them was there an
increase in gray matter in the back part of the hippocampus, along with
changes in their memory profile. In the 20 who quit or failed and came back
for the second scan, and in the controls, there was no structural change. At
the start, the groups did not differ.

**Strength of evidence:** moderate. It is the best human evidence that
intensive learning changes the structure of the adult brain. But there was no
random assignment (no one can be assigned by lot to become a taxi driver),
the sample is a few dozen people, and the timescale is years of daily
training, not weeks.
:::

## Juggling: What Comes Also Goes

A study by Bogdan Draganski and colleagues (2004) tested shorter training:
adults who had never juggled learned to keep three balls in the air. After a
few months of practice, MRI showed more gray matter in areas involved in
perceiving visual motion. Months after they stopped practicing, the increase
had shrunk.

The study is small and depends on MRI, with all of Thomas and Baker's
caveats; the strength of evidence is preliminary. But the picture it offers
is useful: structural change, when it appears, follows use, and recedes when
use stops. The brain does not keep forever what it has stopped practicing.

## New Neurons? An Open Controversy

In mammals such as rodents and monkeys, the adult hippocampus keeps producing
new neurons in a small region called the dentate gyrus. The production of new
neurons is called **neurogenesis**. The question that remained was: does this
also happen in adult humans, and in what quantity?

In 2018, two studies of human brain tissue reached opposite answers a few
weeks apart:

| | Sorrells et al. (2018) | Boldrini et al. (2018) |
|---|---|---|
| Material | Post-mortem brains and tissue removed in epilepsy surgery, from adults aged 18 to 77 | Whole post-mortem hippocampi from healthy people aged 14 to 79 |
| Conclusion | Did not detect young neurons in the adult hippocampus; production drops sharply in childhood | Found thousands of immature neurons at every age |

Critics pointed to problems of method, such as the markers used to identify
a "young" neuron and the time between death and the preservation of the
tissue. Later work, with an improved preservation technique, found immature
neurons in healthy people into old age (Moreno-Jiménez et al., 2019), and a
large group of specialists argued that there is no reason to abandon the idea
(Kempermann et al., 2018). The question is not settled. Strength of
evidence: contested.

The practical consequence is simple: any sentence along the lines of
"meditation grows new neurons" claims something that has not been shown. We
don't even know for sure how many new neurons the adult human produces, let
alone whether meditation changes that.

## "Rewire Your Brain in Eight Weeks"

We come to the promise that circulates most. It grew out of small studies
that found more gray matter in some regions after an eight-week meditation
course, MBSR (chapter {{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}
explains the program). The most cited of them, by Britta Hölzel and
colleagues (2011), compared a few dozen people, without random assignment,
with a waitlist group — exactly the kind of design that chapter
{{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}} showed to be weak.

When the same idea was tested with far greater rigor — a 2022 randomized
trial from Richard Davidson's lab, with more than two hundred people, an
active control and a waitlist (Kral and colleagues) — no structural change
attributable to MBSR appeared, not even in the regions flagged by the
earlier studies. Hölzel's findings did not replicate. The study is described
in chapter {{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}. Strength of
evidence for structural change with eight weeks of practice: contested, and
absent in the largest trial.

Two wrong readings should be avoided. The first is to conclude that
meditation "does nothing to the brain": if it changes attention or habits,
it changes synapses, just at a scale MRI can't reach. The second is to think
the study settles the question for every practice: the authors themselves
note that much longer or more specific training might show changes. What can
be said with confidence is what the headline denies: eight weeks of MBSR
leave no visible mark on the structure of the brain. What that same program
does or doesn't do for health is a different question, with a different
answer.

## What This Means for People Who Meditate

Neuroplasticity is the reason it makes sense to talk about *training* the
mind. If the adult brain did not change with use, no practice would have a
lasting effect. Because it does change, repeating a mental habit — bringing
attention back to the breath, noticing a reaction before following it — can
leave a trace.

But three corrections come with this good news:

- **The trace is small and slow.** The best example of structural change in
  humans took years of intensive, daily training. Weeks of light practice
  produce, at best, changes that current scans cannot see.
- **A change in the brain is not the benefit.** What matters is whether the
  person pays more attention, suffers less, sleeps better. A scan showing a
  larger region proves none of that, and a scan showing no change does not
  prove the opposite. Benefits are measured in behavior and in life.
- **Plasticity has no moral direction.** The same mechanism that strengthens
  a good habit strengthens a bad one. Repeated worry also trains the brain —
  and that is what chapter {{cap:a-mente-que-divaga}} examines.

## What This Chapter Established

- **Neurons** transmit signals; **synapses** are the contact points between
  them, and their strength changes with use. This is the basis of learning
  and is well demonstrated (strong).
- **Neuroplasticity** is the brain's capacity to change with experience. It
  is how the brain normally works, not a special power of meditation.
  **Hebbian learning** — connections used together are reinforced — is its
  central idea.
- We do not use only 10% of our brains.
- Changes in entire regions, measured as **gray matter** on MRI, exist, but
  they are small and fragile to measure (moderate). The best example — the
  **hippocampus** of London's taxi drivers — took years.
- Structural changes follow use and recede when it stops.
- Whether the adult human produces new neurons (**neurogenesis**) is a
  contested question; there is no basis for saying that meditation creates
  them.
- The largest randomized trial found no structural change after eight weeks
  of MBSR. "Rewiring the brain in eight weeks" does not hold up.
