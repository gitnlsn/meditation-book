This chapter gives you the tools to read the rest of the book, and any
headline about meditation. To know whether a practice works, you need to:

1. compare the people who practice with people who do something similar;
2. decide by lot who goes into each group;
3. add up many studies instead of trusting one.

Nearly all the hype about meditation, and about the brain in general, comes
from skipping one of these steps. The chapter also defines the words the book
uses for the size of an effect: small, moderate, large.

## Getting Better Proves Nothing

Start with the most common case. Thirty stressed people take an eight-week
meditation course. At the end, they are, on average, less stressed than at
the start. Did meditation work?

Maybe. But there are at least five other explanations, all of them plausible:

1. **Time passed.** People who sign up for a stress course usually do so at a
   bad moment. Bad moments tend to pass on their own.
2. **Expectation.** People who sign up believe they will get better. That
   belief changes what they feel and how they answer.
3. **Context.** Eight weeks of a welcoming group, an attentive instructor and
   time set aside for yourself would do good with almost any content.
4. **The wish to please.** At the end of a course they liked, people tend to
   mark better answers on the questionnaire.
5. **Who dropped out.** If the people it didn't suit left halfway through, the
   average of those who stayed looks better than the course was.

None of these explanations says that meditation *doesn't* work. They only say
that "people got better" is compatible with meditation having done nothing.
The tools that follow separate the effect of the practice from everything
else.

## Compare and Assign by Lot: The Randomized Trial

The first tool is a **control group**: similar people who do *not* receive
the practice being tested and are measured at the same points. It is like
testing a fertilizer by planting two beds on the same day, one with
fertilizer and one without. If both grow the same, the fertilizer made no
difference. Only the difference *between* the groups counts.

The second tool is deciding **by lot** who goes into each group. It looks like
a detail, and it is the most important part. If people chose, the more
motivated or those with more free time would tend to choose meditation. Any
difference at the end could come from that. Assigning by lot spreads those
differences, known and unknown, evenly across the groups.

A study that does both is a **randomized controlled trial**, or RCT
("randomized" means assigned by chance). It is the most reliable design for
answering "Does this cause that?"

Many famous studies of meditation are not randomized trials. They compare
experienced meditators with non-meditators at a single point in time. What
they find is a **correlation**: two things that go together. Correlation is
not causation. People who spend years meditating may have been different from
the start, just as people who go to the gym may already be healthier before
their first class. Chapter {{cap:neuroplasticidade-sem-exagero}} shows how
much this matters in studies of the brain.

## Compared with What: Waitlists, Active Controls and Placebo

Having a control group is not enough. *What* that group does matters. And the
most common control in meditation research is also the weakest.

That control is the **waitlist**: the group does nothing during the study and
gets the course afterward. It is like comparing a drug with "come back in two
months." It does not rule out expectation or context. Worse, it inflates the
result, because people who wait tend to improve *less* than they would with
ordinary care. A 2024 comparison of more than 300 trials of psychotherapy for
depression showed this: against a waitlist, the effect looked about 50%
larger than against usual care. The study is about psychotherapy, but the
design is the same. Strength of evidence: strong.

The way out is an **active control**: the other group does an activity of the
same length, in the same format and with the same human warmth, but without
the ingredient being tested. It is like finding out whether the seasoning is
what makes a dish good: the other group eats the same dish, without the
seasoning. If meditation beats an active control, there is reason to think
meditation itself makes a difference.

Behind this is the idea of a **placebo**. In medicine, a placebo is a
treatment with no active ingredient, such as a sugar pill, that produces real
improvement because the person expects to improve. By extension, the word
covers all the part of a benefit that comes from expectation and context
rather than from the ingredient being tested. With a drug, an identical pill
is enough. With meditation it is harder: everyone knows whether they are
meditating.

When a well-built active control is used, part of meditation's advantage
disappears. A 2012 trial with 63 people compared MBSR with a group health
program: physical activity, posture, music and nutrition. (MBSR is an
eight-week group meditation course, covered in chapter
{{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}.) On questionnaires of
distress and anxiety, both groups improved, and MBSR was "no more effective
than a rigorous active control." On a heat-pain test, MBSR did better, but
only when participants used the mindfulness strategy. The correct reading:
part of what gets credited to meditation comes from the package (the group,
the instructor, time for yourself). Strength of evidence: preliminary,
because it is a single small study.

Active controls are still a minority. The critical review *Mind the Hype*
(2018), written by fifteen researchers, several of them from the field
itself, cites an estimate: fewer than 1 in 10 mindfulness studies had compared
the practice with an active control. A direct count from 2021, in trials
outside clinical settings, found a passive control in about 7 out of 10.
Mindfulness is defined in chapter {{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}}.

## Fake Meditation: The Placebo Test

The verdict comes first: **how much of meditation's benefit is expectation is
not yet known**. Almost no trial measures expectation, and the tests with sham
meditation are few and small. What there is suggests that there is something
beyond placebo, at least for laboratory pain. Strength of evidence:
preliminary.

The problem is that even an active control does not settle everything. It
only neutralizes the placebo if both groups *expect* to improve to the same
degree. If one group thinks it got the "real" treatment and the other thinks
it got the consolation prize, expectations remain unequal (Boot et al.,
2013). This is still rarely measured: a 2024 review of mindfulness and pain
found fewer than 1 in 10 studies dealing with expectation or placebo.

The most direct answer is **sham meditation**: a control presented as
meditation, which creates the same expectation, but without the instruction
being tested. It works as meditation's sugar pill. If real meditation beats
the sham, the effect is not just expectation.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Fadel Zeidan and colleagues (2015) randomly assigned 75
healthy volunteers to four days of mindfulness meditation, placebo cream,
sham meditation or listening to a book. Then they measured heat-induced pain.
A 2025 reanalysis, combining two trials from the same lab and 115
participants, repeated the comparison (Riegner et al., 2025).

**What it found.** The placebo and the sham meditation also relieved pain.
Real meditation relieved it more than either, in both papers.

**Strength of evidence:** preliminary. Laboratory pain, healthy people, small
groups, a single lab. It shows that the effect on acute pain is not *only*
placebo. It does not say how much of it is expectation in patients.
:::

Outside the lab, the arithmetic gets harder. A 2025 trial randomly assigned
164 adults with knee osteoarthritis to four groups: Reiki (a laying-on of
hands), sham Reiki, mindfulness or a waitlist. Mindfulness beat sham Reiki.
But Reiki tied with mindfulness: more than half the people in both groups
improved meaningfully. It is a single trial, and the senior author has income
linked to a mindfulness program. The correct reading: part of the benefit may
come from what the two therapies have in common. Strength of evidence:
preliminary. Meditation is not a substitute for treatment; chronic pain is
covered in chapter {{cap:o-corpo-o-sono-e-a-dor}}.

Another trial, with anxious or depressed older adults, measured expectation
and did not find that it explained the effect of MBSR. It too is a single
trial, and it does not settle the question.

## One Study Is Not Enough: Replication

The verdict: **about half of published positive results in the behavioral
sciences hold up, and with a smaller effect.** Strength of evidence: strong.
This is why this book puts a strength label on every finding.

**Replication** is another group repeating the study, preferably with more
people, to see whether the result holds. It is like a recipe: if it only
works in the kitchen of the person who invented it, maybe the secret is not
the recipe. A finding that has appeared once is a lead, not a fact.

A result is **statistically significant** when it would be unlikely if there
were no effect at all, like a coin that comes up heads many times in a row.
It does not mean the effect is large or important. It only means it is
unlikely to be pure chance.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** In 2015, the Open Science Collaboration, in the best-known
test, repeated 100 psychology studies. In 2026, the SCORE project repeated
274 positive results from 164 papers in the social and behavioral sciences.
The replications had large samples and plans reviewed in advance (Tyner et
al., 2026).

**What it found.** In 2015, 97% of the originals and 36% of the replications
had statistically significant results. In SCORE, the result held in 55.1% of
cases (49.3% counting by paper). The typical effect fell from 0.25 to 0.10,
on a correlation scale where 0 means no relationship.

**Strength of evidence:** strong. Some of the drop is expected, the SCORE
authors note: only positive results were repeated, and those include the
lucky ones.
:::

Meditation has its textbook case. The much-cited changes in brain structure
after eight weeks of MBSR did not appear in the largest randomized trial with
an active control. Chapter {{cap:neuroplasticidade-sem-exagero}} tells the
story.

Why do so many results shrink? One central reason is that many studies have
few people. In neuroscience, average **statistical power**, the chance of
detecting an effect that exists, is very low (Button et al., 2013). A small
study works like a net with a wide mesh. It lets the small fish through, and
the ones it catches are the biggest. That is why a small study with a
spectacular result is the kind that tends not to replicate.

## Adding Up Studies: Meta-Analysis and Effect Size

If one study is not enough, you put them all together. A **meta-analysis** is
a study of studies. The researcher gathers the research on a question,
following rules set in advance, and combines the results into a single
number. It is like reading a hundred reviews of a restaurant instead of one.
A good meta-analysis carries more weight than any single study, because the
errors of each one tend to cancel out.

The number a meta-analysis combines is the **effect size**: how large the
difference between the groups is, not just whether it exists. The most common
one is called *d* (a variant, *g*, is read the same way). It compares the
difference between the groups with how much people already vary naturally.
Two centimeters of difference in height between two groups of adults is
little, because adults vary a lot.

An intuitive way to read *d*: draw one person at random from the group that
practiced and one from the control group, and ask which one is doing better.
If the practice makes no difference (*d* = 0), the meditation person wins 50
out of every 100 draws, like a coin toss. The larger the *d*, the more often
they win.

**How this book puts effect sizes into words.** In the text, the book
replaces the numbers for *d* and *g* with words, using this scale:

| Effect size (*d* or *g*) | The book says | Out of 100 draws, the person who practiced wins about |
|---|---|---|
| indistinguishable from 0 | no detectable effect | 50 |
| about 0.2 | small | 56 |
| about 0.5 | moderate | 64 |
| 0.8 or more | large | 71 or more |

A value between two bands takes both words: 0.35 is "small to moderate."
Below 0.2, it is still small, and very small near 0.1. The last column is a
conversion made for this book, not a figure from the studies. Correlations
use a different scale, explained where they appear.

The scale is a convention, like the bands on a fever thermometer. Three
caveats always apply:

- **"No detectable effect" does not prove the effect is zero.** There may be
  a small effect the study was not large enough to see.
- **Small does not mean useless.** A small effect can be worth having if the
  practice is cheap and safe. And a large effect in a small study may shrink
  on replication.
- **Significant does not mean large.** An effect can be real and small: the
  coin is slightly loaded, but it does not always land on the same side.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Simon Goldberg and colleagues (2018) pooled trials of
mindfulness-based interventions for people with psychiatric disorders: 142
samples and 12,005 participants. What was new was sorting the studies by the
type of control group.

**What it found.** Right after treatment, the effect size fell as the
control became more demanding:

| Compared with | *d* | In words | Out of 100 draws, mindfulness wins about |
|---|---|---|---|
| No treatment | 0.55 | moderate | 65 |
| Minimal treatment | 0.37 | small to moderate | 60 |
| Nonspecific active control | 0.35 | small to moderate | 60 |
| Specific active control | 0.23 | small | 56 |
| Treatment with proven efficacy | −0.004 | no difference | 50 |

The last two columns are readings made for this book. The most consistent
results were for depression, pain, smoking and addictions. A 2022 review by
the same group pooled 44 meta-analyses (336 trials, 30,483 participants) and
found the same pattern (Goldberg et al., 2022).

**Strength of evidence:** moderate. These are large syntheses, but they
inherit the weaknesses of the studies they pool, and one of the authors
directs a meditation research center.
:::

The table sums up the whole chapter. Against "nothing," mindfulness has a
moderate effect. Against something similar, it shrinks until it is small.
Against a treatment that already works, it ties.

Tying with an effective treatment is a real and useful result. It means
mindfulness can be an alternative for some people, in a choice made with
whoever is overseeing their treatment. But it is very different from the
headline "Meditation beats therapy." Meditation is not a substitute for
treatment.

## What Never Gets Published: Publication Bias

The verdict: **in mindfulness, publication bias exists, but it probably does
not erase the effect.** The published success rate is too high to be true.
Strength of evidence: moderate.

**Publication bias** is the tendency to publish the studies that worked more
often than the ones that didn't. Journals prefer novelty, and researchers
leave the study that "came to nothing" in a drawer. It is like judging a
course by the testimonials on its website: only the satisfied customers
appear. The published literature looks more optimistic than reality, and a
meta-analysis built on it inherits that optimism.

In mindfulness, the signal shows up in two ways. Nearly 9 out of 10 published
trials reported some positive result, far more than expected even if
mindfulness were as effective as psychotherapy for depression. And of the
registered trials, more than half were still unpublished two and a half years
after they ended (Coronado-Montoya et al., 2016).

On the other side, the 2022 review by Goldberg and colleagues, written by
researchers from inside the field, found that the effects generally survived tests for publication bias. But it found other
important sources of bias.

A similar bias comes from the people doing the research. Goldberg and Tucker
(2020) reanalyzed 60 comparisons between mindfulness and other established
treatments. Mindfulness had a small edge overall. That edge appeared when the
researchers were sympathetic to mindfulness, and vanished when that sympathy
was absent or balanced. It is an association across studies, not an
experiment. And no one has to cheat for this to happen: people who believe in
a treatment tend to deliver it better and to read ambiguous results in its
favor. Strength of evidence: moderate.

The main defense is **preregistration**: publishing the study's plan before
collecting the data. How many people, what control, what the main outcome is,
how the analysis will be done. It is like sealing your prediction in an
envelope before the game: afterward, you cannot change your bet or make the
envelope disappear.

A good example is a preregistered 2024 trial with more than 2,000 people at
dozens of sites. A single 15-minute mindfulness exercise reduced
self-reported stress right afterward more than listening to an audio excerpt
of fiction did. The authors point out the limits: a small effect, one session
only, almost all students. Strength of evidence: moderate, for immediate
relief of self-reported stress, and nothing beyond that.

## Brain Images: What fMRI Measures

The verdict: **brain images are useful, but fragile.** They depend on
analysis choices, vary within the same person and, to compare people, need a
great many of them. Strength of evidence: strong.

Headlines with colored blobs on a brain nearly always come from **fMRI**,
functional magnetic resonance imaging. It is the hospital MRI scanner,
programmed to record changes in blood oxygenation at each small point in the
brain. When a region works harder, the flow of oxygenated blood to it changes
(Raichle et al., 2001). It is like telling which rooms of a house are in use
by looking at each room's electricity use: fMRI does not see the cells at
work, it sees a signal that tracks their activity.

That signal is weak and noisy. A typical scan divides the brain into about
130,000 small cubes and runs a statistical test on each one. With so many
tests, some will come out "positive" by pure chance, as in a raffle with
thousands of tickets: some ticket always wins. The analysis has to correct
the calculation for this.

A 2010 experiment demonstrated the problem in a way no one has forgotten.
Researchers put a dead salmon in the scanner, showed it photos of people in
social situations and "asked" it to say what emotion each person must have
been feeling. Without the statistical correction, the analysis found "active"
points in the fish's brain. With the correction, none (Bennett et al., 2010).
The work won the 2012 Ig Nobel Prize in neuroscience.

The salmon is a serious joke, and it has to be read correctly. It does *not*
show that fMRI is worthless. It shows that an analysis without correction
produces false positives, and that the correction works. But correcting is
not enough. Three more recent findings weigh more:

1. **Same data, different conclusions.** In 2020, 70 teams received the same
   scans and the same hypotheses. No two analyzed them the same way, and the
   answers varied considerably.
2. **Same person, different measurements.** Two scans of the same person on
   the same task agree poorly: on average, about 0.4 on a scale where 1 is
   perfect repetition. That is too poor to compare one person with another.
3. **Comparing brains takes thousands.** An analysis of about 50,000 scans
   showed that with the typical sample, of about 25 people, links between the
   brain and psychological traits come out exaggerated and fail to replicate
   (Marek et al., 2022). Studies that test an intervention need fewer people,
   the authors note. The target is the study that compares the brains of
   meditators and non-meditators.

Used well, fMRI remains valuable. The right question to ask of a brain image
is not "Is fMRI reliable?" but "Was this analysis planned in advance, done
properly, and with how many people?"

## "The Region Lit Up, So…": Reverse Inference

There is a subtler error, and it shows up even in well-analyzed studies. It is
called **reverse inference**: deducing a mental state from the activation of
a brain region. "The reward region lit up, so meditation is pleasurable."

The classic example is the **amygdala**, a small brain region involved in
detecting threat and emotional relevance. Studies show that fear tends to
activate it. That does not mean every activation of the amygdala is fear. The
same region takes part in many other things: novelty, relevance, surprise.

So the reasoning runs in the wrong direction. It is not logically valid, but
it can provide *some* information: more so the more selective the region is,
that is, the fewer different things activate it (Poldrack, 2006). For most of
the regions the headlines talk about, selectivity is low.

An analogy helps. Knowing that people with the flu tend to sneeze does not
let you conclude that someone sneezing has the flu: dust, pepper and
allergies also make people sneeze. The brain has very few regions that only
"sneeze" for one reason.

Chapter {{cap:a-mente-que-divaga}} gives a concrete example: a network of
regions that is less active in meditators, and the temptation to conclude
from this that they "think less about themselves."

## Six Questions for Any Headline

The chapter fits in a list. When you see "Study proves that meditation…," ask:

1. **Was there a control group, and were people assigned by lot?** If not,
   the study shows a correlation, not a cause.
2. **What did the control group do?** Nothing, a waitlist, or something
   active and similar? The weaker the control, the more the result includes
   expectation and context.
3. **How many people?** Studies with a few dozen participants are leads.
   Large results in small samples tend to shrink.
4. **Was the plan registered in advance, with a main outcome?** If not,
   there is no way to know how many other measures were tried.
5. **Has it been repeated, or pooled in a meta-analysis?** And is the effect
   small, moderate or large?
6. **If there is a brain image, what exactly was measured?** A region that
   "lights up" does not, on its own, reveal what the person felt.

These questions are not meant to make you distrust everything, but to give
each thing its proper weight. Some claims about meditation pass all six well;
many don't pass the first.

## What This Chapter Established

- "People got better" proves nothing, and **correlation** is not causation.
  Only a **randomized controlled trial**, with a **control group** assigned
  by lot, lets you talk about cause.
- A **waitlist** inflates the effect (strong). An **active control**
  separates the ingredient from the **placebo** only if expectations are
  similar.
- Against **sham meditation**, mindfulness relieved laboratory pain more
  (preliminary). How much of the benefit is expectation is not yet known.
- About half of positive results hold up, with smaller effects (strong).
  **Statistically significant** means "unlikely to be chance," not "large."
- In this book, an **effect size** is small (about 0.2), moderate (about 0.5)
  or large (0.8 or more). In mindfulness, it shrinks from moderate to small
  as the control becomes more demanding, and ties with effective treatments
  (moderate).
- **Publication bias** and researchers' sympathies inflate the literature;
  **preregistration** is the main defense (moderate).
- The dead salmon shows the danger of an uncorrected **fMRI** analysis.
  Today analysis choices, low reliability and small samples weigh more
  (strong). **Reverse inference** is weak unless the region is selective.

**This book's position.** A claim about meditation counts as established
only when it comes from randomized trials, registered in advance, adequately
sized, against an active control, and repeated or pooled. By that standard,
the honest picture is this: real and small benefits for some outcomes, not
an exceptionally powerful treatment. Two questions remain open: how much of
that benefit is expectation, and how many brain-imaging findings would
survive samples of thousands of people.
