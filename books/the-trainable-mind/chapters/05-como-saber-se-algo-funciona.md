This chapter gives you the tools to read the rest of the book, and any
headline about meditation. The thesis is simple: to know whether a practice
works, you have to compare the people who practice with people who do
something similar, decide by lot who goes into each group, and add up many
studies instead of trusting one. Nearly all the hype about meditation — and
about the brain in general — comes from skipping one of these three steps. By
the end, you should know what a randomized controlled trial, an active
control, a meta-analysis and an effect size are, and why "region X lit up on
the scan" almost never means what the headline says.

## Getting Better Proves Nothing

Start with the most common case. Thirty stressed people take an eight-week
meditation course. At the end, they fill out a questionnaire and are, on
average, less stressed than at the start. Did meditation work?

Maybe. But there are at least five other explanations, and all of them are
plausible:

1. **Time passed.** People who sign up for a stress course usually do so at a
   bad moment. Bad moments tend to pass on their own.
2. **Expectation.** People who sign up believe they will get better, and
   that belief changes both what they feel and how they answer.
3. **Context.** Eight weeks of a welcoming group, an attentive instructor and
   time set aside to look after yourself would do good with almost any
   content.
4. **The wish to please.** At the end of a course they liked, people tend to
   mark better answers on the questionnaire.
5. **Who dropped out.** If the people it didn't suit left the course halfway
   through, the average of those who stayed looks better than the course
   really was.

None of these explanations says that meditation *doesn't* work. They only say
that "people got better" is compatible with meditation having done nothing.
To separate the effect of the practice from everything else, science has
developed a small set of tools. They are the subject of this chapter.

## Compare and Assign by Lot: The Randomized Trial

The first tool is a **control group**: a group of similar people who do *not*
receive the practice being tested and are measured at the same points. If
both groups improve equally, the improvement came from time, expectation or
chance, not from the practice. Only the difference *between* the groups
counts.

The second tool is deciding **by lot** who goes into each group. This looks
like a detail, and it is the most important part. If people chose their
group, the more motivated, the healthier or those with more free time would
tend to choose meditation, and any difference at the end could come from
that. Assigning by lot spreads those differences, known and unknown, evenly
across the groups.

A study that does both — has a control group and assigns people to each
group by lot — is a **randomized controlled trial**, or RCT ("randomized"
means assigned by chance). It is the most reliable design for answering "Does
this cause that?" Many famous studies of meditation are not randomized
trials: they compare experienced meditators with non-meditators at a single
point in time. Those studies show that the groups are different, not that
meditation caused the difference — people who spend years meditating may
have been different from the start. Chapter
{{cap:neuroplasticidade-sem-exagero}} shows how much this matters in studies
of the brain.

## Compared with What: Active Controls and Placebo

Having a control group is not enough. *What* the control group does matters
a great deal.

The weakest control, and the most common in meditation research, is the
**waitlist**: the control group does nothing during the study and gets the
course afterward. It rules out the "time passed" explanation, but it does not
rule out expectation, the welcoming group, the attentive instructor.

To rule those out, researchers use an **active control**: the control group
does another activity of the same length, in the same format and with the
same human warmth, but without the ingredient being tested. If meditation
beats an active control, there is reason to think the specific ingredient —
meditation itself — makes a difference.

Behind this is the idea of a **placebo**. In medicine, a placebo is a
treatment with no active ingredient, such as a sugar pill, that produces real
improvement because the person expects to improve. By extension, the word has
come to mean all the part of a benefit that comes from expectation and
context rather than from the ingredient being tested. With a drug, this is
controlled with identical pills, so that no one knows who took which. With
meditation it is impossible: everyone knows whether they are meditating or
not.

An example shows why this matters. Researchers at the University of
Wisconsin built an active control for MBSR, an eight-week group meditation
program born in a medical outpatient clinic (chapter
{{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}} covers it). The control
included physical activity, posture and balance exercises, music and
nutrition, in a group and with instructors. In a randomized trial with 63
people, both groups improved over time in psychological distress and
anxiety, and, in the authors' words, MBSR was "no more effective than a
rigorous active control" on these questionnaires (MacCoon et al., 2012). On a
heat-pain test, MBSR did better. The correct reading: part of what gets
credited to meditation comes from the package — the group, the instructor,
time for yourself — and only an active control lets you see which part
belongs to meditation. Strength of evidence: preliminary to moderate (one
study, small sample).

Even an active control has a limit, pointed out by Walter Boot and colleagues
(2013): it only neutralizes the placebo if the people in both groups
*expect* to improve to the same degree. If the meditation group thinks it is
getting the "real" treatment and the control group thinks it is getting the
consolation prize, expectations remain unequal. Almost no study measures
this.

How common are active controls in meditation research? The critical review
*Mind the Hype*, written in 2018 by fifteen researchers — several of them
meditation scientists — cites an estimate according to which only 9% of
research on mindfulness interventions had reached the stage of comparing the
practice with an active control (Van Dam et al., 2018). Mindfulness is
defined in chapter {{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}}.

## One Study Is Not Enough: Replication

A single study, even a well-designed one, can go wrong by chance. That is why
science depends on **replication**: another group of researchers repeats the
study, preferably with more people, and sees whether the result holds. A
finding that has appeared only once is a lead, not a fact.

In 2015, a consortium of researchers, the Open Science Collaboration, ran
the test on a large scale. They repeated 100 studies published in three
major psychology journals, using the original materials whenever possible. Of
the original studies, 97% had statistically significant results — that is,
results unlikely to be due to chance. Of the replications, 36%. And the
effects found in the replications were, on average, half the size of the
originals. Strength of evidence: strong. This is why this book puts a
strength label on every finding.

Why do so many results shrink? One central reason is sample size. Katherine
Button and colleagues (2013) showed that studies in neuroscience have, on
average, very low statistical power — the ability to detect an effect that
exists — almost always because they have few participants. This causes two
problems at once: a small study misses real effects, and, when it does find
one, it tends to exaggerate its size. A small study with a spectacular
result is exactly the kind that tends not to replicate.

## Adding Up Studies: Meta-Analysis and Effect Size

If one study is not enough, you put them all together. A **meta-analysis** is
a study of studies: the researcher gathers all the research on a question,
following rules set in advance, and combines the results into a single
number. A good meta-analysis carries more weight than any single study,
because the errors of each study tend to cancel out.

The number a meta-analysis combines is the **effect size**: how large the
difference between the groups is, not just whether it exists. The most
common one is called *d*. It measures the difference between the averages of
the two groups in "units of natural variation" — that is, compared with how
much people already differ from one another anyway.

An intuitive way to read *d*: draw one person at random from the group that
practiced and one from the control group, and ask which one is doing better.
If the practice makes no difference at all (*d* = 0), the meditation person
wins 50 out of every 100 draws, like a coin toss. With *d* = 0.35, they win
about 60. With *d* = 0.8, about 71. A "statistically significant" effect can
be real and still small: it means the coin is slightly loaded, not that it
always lands on the same side.

**The study.** Simon Goldberg and colleagues (2018) pooled trials of

type of control group.

control became more demanding:

| Compared with | *d* | Out of 100 draws, the mindfulness group wins about |

directs a meditation research center.

The table sums up the whole chapter. Against "nothing," mindfulness looks
good. Against something similar, the effect shrinks. Against a treatment that
already works, it ties. Tying with an effective treatment is a real and useful
result — it means mindfulness can be an alternative for some people, in a
choice made with whoever is overseeing their treatment — but it is very
different from the headline "Meditation beats therapy."

## What Never Gets Published: Publication Bias

A meta-analysis can only add up the studies it finds. And studies that find a
positive result are more likely to be published than those that find
nothing: journals prefer novelty, and researchers leave the study that
"didn't work" in a drawer. This is called **publication bias**. The
consequence is that the published literature looks more optimistic than
reality, and a meta-analysis built on it inherits that optimism. There are
statistical methods for estimating how much is missing, but none of them
recovers the study that never left the drawer.

A similar bias comes from the people doing the research. In a new look at
Goldberg's data, Goldberg and Tucker (2020) examined 60 comparisons between
mindfulness and other established treatments. Mindfulness had a small edge
overall, but the edge appeared when the researchers were sympathetic to
mindfulness and vanished when that sympathy was absent or balanced. No one
has to be cheating for this to happen: people who believe in a treatment
tend to deliver it better, to choose favorable measures and to interpret
ambiguous results in its favor. Strength of evidence: moderate.

## Brain Images: What fMRI Measures

Half the headlines about meditation come with a picture of a brain with
colored blobs. Nearly all of them come from **fMRI**, functional magnetic
resonance imaging. It is an MRI scanner — the same kind used in hospitals —
programmed to record changes in blood oxygenation at each small point in the
brain. When a region works harder, the flow of oxygenated blood to it
changes, and the scanner detects that. In other words, fMRI does not see
neurons (the cells that transmit signals, introduced in chapter
{{cap:neuroplasticidade-sem-exagero}}) at work: it sees a blood signal that
tracks the activity of neurons (Raichle et al., 2001).

That signal is weak and noisy, and the analysis requires careful statistics.
A typical scan divides the brain into about 130,000 small cubes, called
voxels, and runs a statistical test on each one. With so many tests, some
will come out "positive" by pure chance, unless the researcher corrects the
calculation for it.

Craig Bennett and colleagues (2010) demonstrated the problem in a way no one
has forgotten. They put a dead salmon in the scanner, showed it photos of
people in social situations and "asked" it to say what emotion each person
must have been feeling. Without the statistical correction, the analysis
found a cluster of "active" voxels in the fish's brain cavity. With the
correction, none. The work won an Ig Nobel Prize.

The salmon is a serious joke, and it has to be read correctly. It does *not*
show that fMRI is worthless. It shows that an analysis done without the
proper correction produces false positives, and that the correction works.
Used well, fMRI is one of the most valuable tools in neuroscience. The right
question to ask of a brain image is not "Is fMRI reliable?" but "Was this
analysis done properly, and with how many people?"

## "The Region Lit Up, So…": Reverse Inference

There is a subtler error, and it shows up even in well-analyzed studies. It
has a name: **reverse inference**. It means deducing a mental state from the
activation of a brain region: "the amygdala (a small region involved in
detecting threat and emotional relevance) lit up, so the person felt fear";
"the reward region lit up, so meditation is pleasurable."

The reasoning feels natural, but it runs in the wrong direction. Studies show
that fear tends to activate the amygdala. That does not mean every
activation of the amygdala is fear, because the same region takes part in
many other things: novelty, relevance, surprise. The neuroscientist Russell
Poldrack (2006) showed that this kind of deduction is not logically valid,
but that it can provide *some* information — more so the more selective the
region is, that is, the fewer different things activate it. For most of the
regions the headlines talk about, selectivity is low.

An analogy helps. Knowing that people with the flu tend to sneeze does not
let you conclude that someone sneezing has the flu: dust, pepper and
allergies also make people sneeze. The brain has very few regions that only
"sneeze" for one reason.

Chapter {{cap:a-mente-que-divaga}} gives a concrete example: a network of
regions that is less active in meditators, and the temptation to conclude
from this that they "think less about themselves."

## Five Questions for Any Headline

The chapter fits in a list. When you see "Study proves that meditation…," ask:

1. **Was there a control group, and were people assigned by lot?** If not,
   the study shows a difference, not a cause.
2. **What did the control group do?** Nothing, a waitlist, or something
   active and similar? The weaker the control, the more the result includes
   expectation and context.
3. **How many people?** Studies with a few dozen participants are leads.
   Large results in small samples tend to shrink.
4. **Has it been repeated, or pooled in a meta-analysis?** And how big is the
   effect — not just "does it exist," but how much?
5. **If there is a brain image, what exactly was measured?** A region that
   "lights up" does not, on its own, reveal what the person felt.

These questions are not meant to make you distrust everything. They are
meant to give each thing its proper weight. Some claims about meditation
pass all five well; many don't pass the first.

## What This Chapter Established

- "People got better" does not prove that the practice worked: time,
  expectation, context and dropouts also produce improvement.
- A **randomized controlled trial** compares a group that practices with a
  **control group**, deciding by lot who goes into each. It is the design
  that lets you talk about cause.
- The type of control matters. A waitlist is weak; an **active control**
  separates the specific ingredient from the **placebo** — the benefit that
  comes from expectation and context — and even it only works if
  expectations are similar.
- **Replication** is the test of a finding. In psychology, only a minority of
  the results tested held up, and with smaller effects.
- A **meta-analysis** adds up studies; the **effect size** tells you how
  much, not just whether. In mindfulness, the effect shrinks as the control
  becomes more demanding, and ties with treatments that are already proven.
- **Publication bias** and researchers' sympathies inflate the published
  literature.
- **fMRI** measures a blood signal that tracks brain activity. The dead
  salmon shows the danger of an uncorrected analysis, not that the method is
  worthless. **Reverse inference** — "the region lit up, so the person
  felt" — is weak unless the region is selective.
