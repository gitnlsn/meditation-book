This chapter explains why it is so hard to stay in the present. The short
answer is that not staying in the present is the mind's normal state: it
spends a large share of its time thinking about something else, a set of
brain regions is associated with that way of working, and it has both costs
and benefits. By the end, you should know what mind wandering is, what
rumination is, what the default mode network is, and why the skill
meditation trains is not to stop wandering but to *notice* that you have
wandered.

## What Wandering Is

**Mind wandering** is when your attention leaves what you are doing and
drifts to thoughts and feelings unrelated to the task. You are reading, and
at the bottom of the page you realize you don't know what you just read,
because you were thinking about yesterday's conversation. You are washing
the dishes and, without deciding to, you are planning the week.

The psychologists Jonathan Smallwood and Jonathan Schooler (2015), in a
review of the field, describe three features of this state. The mind
*decouples* from what the senses are taking in: your eyes stay on the page,
but the information doesn't get in. The content comes from memory and
emotion: recollections, plans, worries. And keeping mind wandering in check
depends on executive functions, the same ones we use to concentrate on
purpose.

Two details matter. First, mind wandering is not choosing to think about
something else: most of the time, people only notice they have wandered
once they are already far away. Second, wandering is not necessarily bad,
as we will see.

## How Much Time the Mind Spends Elsewhere

Before 2010, mind wandering had been studied mostly in the lab, with
artificial tasks; measurements in daily life were rare and involved few
people. The question "how much do people's minds wander in real life, and
does it make them more or less happy?" required asking a lot of people, at
random moments of the day, what they were doing and thinking at that
instant. In 2010, a smartphone app made that possible.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert, published in
*Science* in 2010. An iPhone app contacted participants at random moments
of the day and asked: How are you feeling right now (from 0 to 100)? What
are you doing? Are you thinking about something other than what you are
doing? The analysis used responses from 2,250 adults.

**What it found.** Minds were wandering in 46.9% of the samples, and in at
least 30% of the samples during almost every activity. People were less
happy when their minds were wandering than when they were focused on what
they were doing. Wandering to pleasant topics (42.5% of mind-wandering
samples) made no one happier than being in the present; wandering to
neutral or unpleasant topics made people less happy. What people were
thinking predicted their happiness better than what they were doing.
Time-lag analyses suggested, according to the authors, that mind wandering
tended to come before unhappiness, and not only after it.

**Strength of evidence:** moderate for how often minds wander (large
sample, measured in daily life); preliminary for "mind wandering causes
unhappiness." The participants were iPhone users who volunteered, not a
representative sample; and the study is correlational: it shows that the
two things go together, not that one produces the other.
:::

The paper's title, "A Wandering Mind Is an Unhappy Mind," became a catch
phrase, and that is exactly why it deserves a careful reading.

The first part of the finding is solid and easy to confirm for yourself:
the mind wanders a lot. Almost half the times someone was asked, they were
thinking about something else.

The second part is shakier. The authors themselves point out that bad
moods were already known to make the mind wander. To separate the two
directions, they looked at the order of events over the day and concluded
that mind wandering came before unhappiness more often than the reverse.
That kind of analysis is better than nothing, but it is not an experiment:
no one was randomly assigned to wander more or less, and a third factor
(fatigue, stress, an unresolved problem) can produce both at once.
Correlation is not causation, as chapter {{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}} showed.

It is also worth noticing what the study does *not* say: that thinking
about the past or the future is a mistake. It measures mood in the moment,
not how useful the thinking was.

## Wandering Is Also Good for Something

Smallwood and Schooler's review insists on balance. Mind wandering has
costs: it is associated with errors in tasks that demand attention, from
reading to driving. But it also has benefits: it is linked to creativity
and to planning for the future. A mind unable to leave the present could
not rehearse a difficult conversation, imagine a solution, or remember to
pick up bread on the way home.

So the problem is not wandering. It is wandering without noticing, for too
long, and to the wrong places.

## When Wandering Spins Its Wheels: Rumination

One of those wrong places has a name. **Rumination** is repetitive, passive
thinking about your own distress, its causes and its consequences: "Why do
I feel this way?", "Why does this always happen to me?", "What does this
say about me?" The word comes from cattle chewing the same food over again.
Rumination doesn't solve the problem it circles; it keeps it on the agenda.

In 1991, the psychologist Susan Nolen-Hoeksema proposed a theory of the
role this pattern plays in depression. In a 2008 review, she and her
colleagues gathered evidence that rumination worsens depression, intensifies
negative thinking, impairs problem solving and erodes social support.
Rumination also predicts the onset of depressive episodes and is associated
with anxiety, binge eating, binge drinking and self-harm. Strength of
evidence: moderate; the review is broad, but much of the data shows
association, not cause.

The bridge to Part I is direct. Rumination is a textbook case of what the
*Sallatha Sutta* calls the second arrow (chapter
{{cap:as-quatro-nobres-verdades-como-diagnostico}} explains the image): the initial pain is the first arrow; chewing on it for
hours is the second. But the bridge has limits. The sutta describes a
universal human reaction; the research on rumination describes a pattern
measured with questionnaires and associated with disorders. These are
claims in different registers, and one does not prove the other.

A safety note: if rumination takes up much of your days, or comes with
persistent sadness, loss of interest, or thoughts of hurting yourself, it
may be a sign of depression or anxiety, which can be treated. Meditation is
not a substitute for that treatment.

## The Default Mode Network

In 2001, the neuroscientist Marcus Raichle and colleagues published a paper
whose title went on to have a long career: "A default mode of brain
function." They started from a curious observation in brain imaging. When
a person stops resting and starts a task that requires attention to the
outside world (solving a problem, pressing a button when a light appears),
some regions become *more* active, as you would expect. But a consistent
set of other regions becomes *less* active, whatever the task. Raichle
proposed that these regions form a "default mode": a baseline way of
working, switched on at rest and suspended when attention turns to an
external task.

Today this set is called the **default mode network**: a group of regions,
among them parts of the front and middle of the brain's inner surface, such
as the medial prefrontal cortex and the posterior cingulate cortex, that
tend to activate together when a person is not focused on an external task.
That this network exists and behaves this way is one of the best-replicated
findings in brain-imaging neuroscience.

![A schematic of the brain cut down the middle and seen from the inside, front to the left, with the regions this book mentions. Positions are approximate, and the drawing does not say what each region does.](../illustrations/figures/fig-cerebro-en.svg)

What it *does* is another question. Later research associated it with
thinking about oneself, and Killingsworth and Gilbert open their paper by
observing that mind wandering "appears to be the brain's default mode of
operation," citing Raichle. Hence the popular link: "the default mode
network is the mind-wandering network," or, worse, "the ego network." This
is where the warning about reverse inference from chapter
{{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}} comes in. The network tends to be active
during mind wandering; that doesn't mean that every time it activates the
person is wandering, or that it is "the self" in the brain. And, like mind
wandering, it also takes part in useful functions, such as planning and
imagining. Strength of evidence: moderate for the association with mind
wandering; the more ambitious interpretations are contested.

## What Changes in People Who Meditate

In 2011, Judson Brewer and colleagues used fMRI to scan experienced
meditators and people with no meditation experience while they practiced
several kinds of meditation. In the meditators, two core regions of the
default mode network, the medial prefrontal cortex and the posterior
cingulate, were relatively less active across every type of practice. And,
both at rest and while meditating, the posterior cingulate appeared more
strongly connected to regions associated with self-monitoring and attention
control. The authors concluded that the differences are "consistent with"
less mind wandering.

That phrase deserves attention. The study didn't ask participants how much
their minds wandered; it inferred less wandering from the activity of a
network, which is reverse inference. It was also a small study comparing
groups at a single point in time: the meditators may differ for reasons
other than their practice. Strength of evidence: preliminary. It is an
interesting lead, not a demonstration.

## Noticing You've Wandered: Meta-awareness

A study by Wendy Hasenkamp and colleagues (2012) looked at the humblest
moment of practice, and perhaps the most important one. Fourteen meditation
practitioners did breath-focused meditation inside the fMRI scanner. Each
time they realized their mind had left the breath, they pressed a button and
returned to it.

Using those button presses, the researchers divided the practice into a
cycle of four phases:

1. **Wandering**: the mind leaves the breath, without the person noticing.
2. **Noticing**: the person realizes they have wandered.
3. **Redirecting**: attention is brought back.
4. **Sustaining**: attention stays on the breath, until the next departure.

![The four-phase cycle of breath-focused meditation, as Hasenkamp and colleagues (2012) divided it.](../illustrations/figures/fig-ciclo-divagacao-en.svg)

During the wandering phase, activity showed up in regions of the default
mode network. At the moment of noticing, it appeared in regions of another
network, associated with detecting what is relevant. During redirecting and
sustaining, it appeared in regions associated with executive control of
attention. Activity in these phases varied with accumulated meditation
experience. Strength of evidence: preliminary (fourteen people, no control
group, and the same reverse-inference caution).

The study's value is not in the colored blobs. It is in the cycle. It gives
shape to something practitioners report and the tradition describes:
meditation is not a state in which the mind stays still, but the repetition
of a movement: leave, notice, come back.

Step 2 has a name. **Meta-awareness** is the capacity to notice what your
own mind is doing while it does it: not just thinking, but knowing you are
thinking; not just wandering, but noticing that you have wandered.
Smallwood and Schooler point out that mind wandering's relation to this
awareness (whether or not the person notices they are wandering) helps
determine whether it gets in the way or serves a purpose. Noticed mind
wandering can be interrupted, put to use, or let go. Unnoticed mind
wandering simply happens.

## Three Registers, Again

To close, it helps to separate the three voices this book keeps distinct.

- **What the texts claim.** Buddhist discourses on attention, such as the
  satipaṭṭhāna (defined in chapter
  {{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}}), ask the practitioner to
  know what the mind is doing: for example, whether it is distracted or
  concentrated. This is a claim about how to train, made within a religious
  path with its own goals.
- **What practitioners report.** That the mind keeps leaving, even in people
  who have practiced for years. Hasenkamp's study depended on exactly that:
  on practitioners' reports, made with a button, each time they noticed they
  had drifted off.
- **What experiments show.** That mind wandering is frequent in daily life
  (moderate); that it goes together with worse mood (preliminary as to
  cause); that there is a brain network associated with it (moderate); and
  that meditators show differences in that network (preliminary). Whether
  practice increases meta-awareness in daily life, off the cushion, is still
  an open question.

Chapter {{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}} turns the four-phase cycle into a practice.

## What This Chapter Established

- **Mind wandering** is attention leaving the task for thoughts unrelated
  to it. In daily life, it takes up close to half the waking time of the
  people studied (moderate).
- Mind wandering goes together with being less happy, but the study is
  correlational; the idea that mind wandering *causes* unhappiness is
  preliminary.
- Wandering has costs (errors) and benefits (planning, creativity). The
  problem is wandering without noticing.
- **Rumination** is repetitive thinking about your own distress; it is
  associated with depression and anxiety, and it is a good modern example of
  the second arrow. Meditation is not a substitute for treatment.
- The **default mode network** is a set of regions active when attention is
  not on an external task. Linking it to mind wandering is reasonable;
  treating it as "the ego network" is reverse inference.
- Differences in this network in meditators are a preliminary lead.
- **Meta-awareness** is noticing what the mind is doing. The cycle of
  wandering, noticing, coming back and sustaining is the basic movement
  meditation repeats.
