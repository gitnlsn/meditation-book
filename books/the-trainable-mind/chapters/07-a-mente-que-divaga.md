This chapter explains why it is so hard to stay in the present. The short
answer: not staying in the present is the mind's normal state. It spends
somewhere between a third and half of its time thinking about something
else. Brain networks are associated with that way of working, and it has
both costs and benefits. That is why the skill meditation trains is not to
stop wandering. It is to *notice* that you have wandered.

## What Wandering Is

**Mind wandering** is when your attention leaves what you are doing and
drifts to thoughts and feelings unrelated to the task. You read a page and,
at the bottom, realize you don't know what you just read, because you were
thinking about yesterday's conversation. You wash the dishes and, without
deciding to, you are planning the week.

A major review of the field describes three features of this state:

- **The mind decouples from the senses.** Your eyes stay on the page, but
  the information doesn't get in.
- **The content comes from inside**: memories, plans, worries, emotions.
- **Keeping it in check uses the same capacities** we use to concentrate on
  purpose.

Two details matter. First, mind wandering is not always involuntary.
Sometimes we let the mind drift on purpose, and a 2016 review showed that
the two kinds behave differently. Meditation works on unintentional
wandering, the kind you only notice afterward. Second, wandering is not
necessarily bad, as we will see.

## How Much Time the Mind Spends Elsewhere

**Verdict: strong.** The mind leaves the present in somewhere between a
third and half of waking moments. The exact figure depends on how you ask.

The study that made this widely known used a smartphone app. Until 2010,
studies of mind wandering and happiness in daily life were rare and small.
The app made it possible to ask thousands of people, at random moments,
what they were doing and thinking at that instant.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert, 2010. An iPhone
app contacted participants at random moments of the day and asked three
questions. How are you feeling right now, from 0 to 100? What are you
doing? Are you thinking about something other than what you are doing? The
analysis used responses from 2,250 adults.

**What it found.**

- Minds were wandering in 46.9% of the samples, and in at least 30% of the
  samples during almost every activity.
- People were less happy when their minds were wandering than when they
  were focused on what they were doing.
- Wandering to pleasant topics (42.5% of mind-wandering samples) made no
  one happier than being in the present. Wandering to neutral or unpleasant
  topics made people less happy.
- What people were thinking predicted their happiness better than what they
  were doing.
- According to the authors, the order of events strongly suggested that
  mind wandering tended to come before unhappiness, and not only after it.

**Strength of evidence:** moderate for how often minds wander: the sample
is large and the measurement was made in daily life. For "mind wandering
causes unhappiness," later research has made the conclusion contested (see
below). The participants were iPhone users who volunteered, not a
representative sample, and the study is correlational.
:::

A 2023 meta-analysis pooled 17 studies of the same kind and arrived at
about a third of the day. It also showed that how you ask changes the
answer: using the person's own phone and asking often both shift the
reports.

## Does Wandering Make You Unhappy?

**Verdict.** That mind wandering goes with being a little less happy is
**moderate**. The relationship is real, but small, and it depends more on
*where* the mind goes than on the fact that it goes. That wandering
*causes* unhappiness is **contested**.

The 2010 paper's title, "A Wandering Mind Is an Unhappy Mind," became a
catch phrase. Its first half holds up; the second is the weaker one. The
authors tried to separate cause and effect by looking at the order of events
over the day. That is not an experiment: no one was randomly assigned to
wander more or less.

Without random assignment, other explanations remain. The authors
themselves point out that bad moods were already known to make the mind
wander. And a third factor, such as fatigue, stress or an unresolved
problem, can produce both. Correlation is not causation, as chapter
{{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}} showed.

Those who repeated this kind of analysis did not find the same thing. Two
studies show why:

- **The order may run the other way.** A 2013 study, also using phones,
  found sadness coming *before* mind wandering. And wandering predicted a
  worse mood only when its content was negative.
- **What matters is the tone of the thought.** A 2025 study of more than
  300 people asked about the tone of thoughts in present-focused moments
  too. The 2010 study had not done that. The mood difference was largely
  explained by that tone.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Kam and colleagues, systematic review with meta-analysis,
2024. It gathered 76 studies on mind wandering and emotional well-being in
adults; 64, with 23,168 participants, went into the calculations.

**What it found.** On average, mind wandering goes with worse well-being,
but only slightly: a correlation of −0.18, on a scale where 0 is no
relationship and −1 a perfect negative one. The size and even the direction
of the relationship changed with the content of the thoughts, intention,
and the method of measurement.

**Strength of evidence:** moderate for a small association. The studies are
correlational and do not settle what causes what.
:::

Settling the question for good is hard. It would take an experiment in
daily life, and there is no way to assign people to wander more for weeks.

## Wandering Is Also Good for Something

**Verdict.** The cost in tasks is **moderate**. The role in planning is
plausible but **preliminary**, because it has rarely been tested directly.
The benefit for creativity is **contested**.

First, the cost. Mind wandering is associated with errors in tasks that
demand attention, from reading to driving. And it increases the longer a
task goes on: a 2025 meta-analysis of more than 10,000 people confirmed it.

Then, the use. The review mentioned at the start links mind wandering to
planning for the future and to creativity. A mind unable to leave the
present could not rehearse a difficult conversation.

Creativity is the most cited benefit and the least solid. In one study, a
break with an easy task, which lets the mind wander, improved creative
problem solving. In another series of three studies, wandering during the
break had no relationship with creative performance.

The problem, then, is not wandering. It is wandering without noticing, for
too long, and to the wrong places.

## When Wandering Spins Its Wheels: Rumination

One of those wrong places has a name. **Rumination** is repetitive, passive
thinking about your own distress, its causes and its consequences: "Why do
I feel this way?", "Why does this always happen to me?", "What does this
say about me?". The word comes from cattle chewing the same cud over and
over. Rumination doesn't solve the problem it circles around; it keeps it
on the agenda.

Rumination is a risk factor for depression and other disorders
(**moderate**: the evidence is broad, but mostly association). The
psychologist Susan Nolen-Hoeksema proposed, in 1991, a theory about the
role of this pattern in depression. In a 2008 review, she and her
colleagues gathered evidence that rumination:

- worsens depression and intensifies negative thinking;
- gets in the way of problem solving and erodes social support;
- predicts the onset of depressive episodes;
- is also associated with anxiety, binge eating, binge drinking and
  self-harm.

Mindfulness-based programs reduce rumination more than usual care
(**moderate**), but nothing suggests they beat other treatments. A 2017
meta-analysis of eleven studies of patients with depression found a
moderate reduction compared with usual care. Against medication or
psychotherapy there was no difference; only three studies made that
comparison. These programs, which combine mindfulness and acceptance, are
led by professionals. They are not meditation done on your own.

The bridge to Part I is direct. Rumination is a textbook case of what the
*Sallatha Sutta* calls the second arrow (chapter
{{cap:as-quatro-nobres-verdades-como-diagnostico}} tells the image): the
initial pain is the first arrow; chewing on it for hours is the second. But
the bridge has limits. The sutta describes a universal human reaction. The
research on rumination describes a pattern measured in questionnaires and
associated with disorders. These are claims in different registers, and
one does not prove the other.

A safety note: if rumination takes up a good part of your days, or comes
with persistent sadness, loss of interest, or thoughts of hurting yourself,
it may be a sign of depression or anxiety, which can be treated. Meditation
is not a substitute for treatment.

## The Default Mode Network

**Verdict.** That the network exists and behaves as described is
**strong**. That it takes part in mind wandering is **moderate**: the link
is consistent but not exclusive. "The mind-wandering network" and "the ego
network" are oversimplifications the research does not support.
"Depression is an overactive default mode network" is **contested**.

The discovery dates from 2001. The neuroscientist Marcus Raichle and
colleagues published a paper whose title made a career: "A default mode of
brain function." When a person starts a task that demands outward
attention, some regions become *more* active, as you would expect. But a
consistent set of others becomes *less* active, whatever the task. Raichle
proposed that these regions form a "default mode": a baseline way of
working, on during rest and suspended when attention turns outward.

Today that set is called the **default mode network**: a group of regions
that tend to activate together when a person is not focused on an external
task. They include the medial prefrontal cortex and the posterior cingulate
cortex, on the brain's inner surface. It is one of the best-replicated
findings in brain imaging. Seen up close, in individual people, it appears
to be several interwoven networks rather than one.

![Diagram of the brain cut down the middle, seen from the inside, with the front on the left and the regions mentioned in this book. The positions are approximate, and the drawing does not say what each region does.](../illustrations/figures/fig-cerebro-en.svg)

What it *does* is another question. Later research linked it to
remembering, imagining the future, and thinking about yourself and others.
A 2023 review proposes that it weaves memory, language and meaning into an
internal narrative tied to the sense of self. And Killingsworth and Gilbert
called mind wandering the brain's "default mode," citing Raichle. Hence the
popular link: "the default mode network is the mind-wandering network," or,
worse, "the ego network."

This is where the warning about reverse inference from chapter
{{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}} comes in. The network tends to be
active when the mind wanders. That does not mean that whenever it activates
the person is wandering. Still less that it is "the self" in the brain.

And the link is not exclusive. A 2015 meta-analysis found that mind
wandering just as consistently activates regions outside the network,
including control networks. And the pattern varies from person to person.

The same oversimplification shows up in a common idea, including in
scientific papers: that depression is an overactive default mode network. A
consortium of research groups in China tested this with scans of more than
800 patients with depression. They measured how much the network's regions
work in sync, that is, how much the activity of one rises and falls with
that of another. Earlier studies had often reported more synchrony. The
consortium found *less*, and only in recurrent depression.

The brain basis of rumination is not yet known.

## What Changes in People Who Meditate

**Verdict: preliminary.** People who meditate show differences in this
network, and training appears to increase its synchrony with attention
networks slightly. Whether that means less mind wandering in daily life is
not yet known: these studies measure brain activity, not reported mind
wandering.

In 2011, Judson Brewer and colleagues used fMRI to scan experienced
meditators and people with no meditation experience while they meditated.
In the meditators, two central regions of the network, the medial
prefrontal cortex and the posterior cingulate, were relatively less active.
And the posterior cingulate appeared more in sync with regions associated
with self-monitoring and attention control. The authors concluded that the
differences are "consistent with decreased mind-wandering."

That phrase deserves attention, for three reasons:

- **It is reverse inference.** The study didn't ask participants how much
  their minds wandered. It inferred less wandering from the activity of a
  network.
- **It is a snapshot.** The study, a small one, compared groups only once.
  The meditators may differ for reasons other than their practice.
- **The repetition came from the same group.** The same lab repeated the
  finding in 2015 with more participants. A repetition by the same group
  does not count as much as an independent one.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Rahrig and colleagues, meta-analysis, 2022. Twelve studies,
not all randomized, compared people trained in mindfulness (226) with
control groups (204). They measured synchrony between brain regions at
rest.

**What it found.** After training, the posterior cingulate appeared more in
sync with a region of the salience network, a set associated with detecting
what is relevant. The effect was small (*g* = 0.234). The test for
publication bias showed no sign of a problem.

**Strength of evidence:** preliminary. There are few studies and the effect
is small. The reading that this reflects more control over inward-directed
attention is the authors', not something measured.
:::

## Noticing You've Wandered: Meta-awareness

The humblest moment of practice may be the most important: noticing that
the mind has left. A study by Wendy Hasenkamp and colleagues (2012) looked
at that moment. Fourteen practitioners did breath-focused meditation inside
the fMRI scanner. Each time they realized their mind had left the breath,
they pressed a button and returned to it.

Using those button presses, the researchers divided the practice into a
cycle of four phases:

1. **Wandering**: the mind leaves the breath, without the person noticing.
2. **Noticing**: the person realizes they have wandered.
3. **Redirecting**: attention is brought back.
4. **Sustaining**: attention stays on the breath, until the next departure.

![The four-phase cycle of breath-focused meditation, as Hasenkamp and colleagues (2012) divided it.](../illustrations/figures/fig-ciclo-divagacao-en.svg)

Each phase came with activity in different networks:

- **while wandering**, in regions of the default mode network;
- **while noticing**, in regions of the salience network;
- **while redirecting and sustaining**, in regions associated with control
  of attention.

Activity in these phases varied with accumulated meditation experience.

The strength of evidence is **preliminary**. There were fourteen people, no
control group, and the same reverse-inference caution applies. A 2022
meta-analysis found the same three networks in meditation focused on a
single object. But the phase-by-phase mapping comes from a single study,
which the research for this book did not find repeated by another group.

The study's value is not in the colored blobs. It is in the cycle. It gives
shape to something practitioners report and the tradition describes:
meditation is not a state in which the mind stays still. It is the
repetition of a movement: leave, notice, come back.

Step 2 has a name. **Meta-awareness** is the capacity to notice what your
own mind is doing while it does it: not just thinking, but knowing you are
thinking; not just wandering, but noticing that you have wandered.

The review mentioned at the start points out that whether or not a person
notices the wandering helps determine whether it gets in the way or serves
a purpose. Noticed mind wandering can be interrupted, put to use, or let
go. Unnoticed mind wandering simply happens. The idea is plausible, but
**preliminary**.

## Does Training Reduce Mind Wandering?

**Verdict: preliminary.** Perhaps a little, during tasks, on weak evidence.

- **Before-and-after studies say yes.** A 2021 review gathered 24 studies.
  Most of those that measured before and after, with at least two weeks of
  practice, found less mind wandering. But the studies differed widely, and
  the authors call for replication.
- **Randomized trials, so far, don't confirm it.** A 2025 meta-analysis
  looked only at randomized trials in university students and judged the
  quality of the evidence generally low. The only significant gains were in
  one part of attention and in episodic memory. Mind wandering was not
  among them.

This is not yet known, because the studies are few, small, different from
one another, and measure mind wandering mostly in the lab. The research for
this book found no meta-analysis devoted to this question alone. Whether
the effect lasts and reaches daily life is open.

## Three Registers, Again

To close, it helps to separate the three voices this book keeps distinct.

- **What the texts claim.** Buddhist discourses on attention, such as the
  satipaṭṭhāna (defined in chapter
  {{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}}), ask the practitioner to
  know what the mind is doing: for example, whether it is distracted or
  concentrated. This is a claim about how to train, made within a religious
  path with its own goals.
- **What practitioners report.** That the mind keeps leaving, even in people
  who have practiced for years. Hasenkamp's study depended on exactly that:
  on practitioners' reports, made with a button, each time they noticed they
  had drifted off.
- **What experiments show.** That mind wandering is frequent (strong); that
  it goes with a slightly worse mood (moderate), but not that it causes it
  (contested); that brain networks are associated with it (moderate); that
  meditators show differences in those networks, and that training may
  reduce mind wandering (preliminary).

Chapter {{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}} turns the four-phase cycle into a
practice; chapter {{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}} covers meditation's
documented adverse effects.

## What This Chapter Established

- **Mind wandering** is attention leaving the task for thoughts unrelated
  to it, with or without intention. It takes up between a third and half of
  waking moments (strong) and costs errors in tasks (moderate).
- Mind wandering goes with a slightly worse mood (moderate), especially
  when the content is negative. That wandering *causes* unhappiness is
  contested, and so is the benefit for creativity.
- **Rumination** is repetitive thinking about your own distress: a risk
  factor for depression and other disorders (moderate) and a good modern
  example of the second arrow. Mindfulness-based programs reduce it more
  than usual care, not more than other treatments (moderate). Meditation is
  not a substitute for treatment.
- The **default mode network** exists (strong), is several networks up
  close, and takes part in mind wandering but not exclusively (moderate).
  "Ego network" is reverse inference; "depression is an overactive default
  mode network" is contested.
- Differences in this network in meditators, and a small increase in its
  synchrony with attention networks after training, are preliminary. So is the claim that training reduces mind wandering.
- **Meta-awareness** is noticing what the mind is doing. The cycle of
  wandering, noticing, coming back and sustaining is the basic movement
  meditation repeats.
- **Open questions:** whether practice increases meta-awareness off the
  cushion, how long effects last, whether intentional and unintentional
  mind wandering respond differently, and what brain changes, if any,
  explain the effects.
