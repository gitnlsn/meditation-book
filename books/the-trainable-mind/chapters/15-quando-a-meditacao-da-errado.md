This chapter answers a question most books on meditation avoid: can it do
harm? It can. There are documented adverse effects. Some pass, others last.
And they also show up in people with no history of mental health problems.
The chapter lays out what is known about these effects, how often they seem
to happen and when they become more likely. And it shows how to recognize
when it is time to adjust your practice, to stop, and to seek help. The
intended tone is that of a well-written medication leaflet: neither an alarm
nor a brochure that lists only benefits.

## Why This Chapter Exists

Research on meditation has mostly measured benefits. One example: in a review
of 38 trials on chronic pain, only seven reported safety data. Britton and
colleagues (2021) describe the research on adverse effects in mindfulness
programs as scarce and imprecise in method. The result was a public image in
which meditating is, at worst, useless.

That image does not come from the tradition. Lindahl and colleagues (2017)
point out that the Buddhist textual sources themselves, from which modern
programs draw the benefits, also recognize other effects of practice beyond
those related to health. In the move to wellness, the knowledge that practice
can destabilize was not lost. What was lost was the habit of talking about
it.

It is worth keeping the three registers apart, as throughout the book:

- **What the texts claim:** that serious practice can pass through difficult
  states.
- **What practitioners report:** experiences of that kind, sometimes severe.
- **What the studies show:** that negative effects also occur in short
  clinical programs, and roughly how often.

This chapter stays mostly in the third register.

## What Counts as an Adverse Effect

An **adverse effect**, in health care, is any unwanted effect associated
with a treatment or practice. It ranges from a headache caused by a drug to
a symptom getting worse during therapy. For meditation, the definition needs
two refinements.

The first is to separate an adverse effect from **expected discomfort**.
Boredom, restlessness, a sore knee, sleepiness, the irritating discovery
that the mind doesn't stop: all of this is part of learning, the way muscle
soreness is part of taking up running. Running into emotions that were there
all along, and were being avoided, is also part of the practice. None of
this, by itself, is a sign that something has gone wrong.

The second refinement is to ask about more than one thing. Willoughby
Britton's team created an interview that, for each experience, looks into
five points:

1. whether it occurred;
2. whether it is linked to meditation practice;
3. how long it lasted;
4. whether it felt good or bad;
5. whether it impaired the person's functioning: at work, in sleep, in
   relationships.

An unpleasant experience that passes in minutes and one that keeps someone
from working for weeks are not the same thing. Counting them together
misleads in both directions.

## The Map of Difficult Experiences

The study that organized the field was *The Varieties of Contemplative
Experience*, by Jared Lindahl, Willoughby Britton and colleagues (2017).
They interviewed Western practitioners from Theravāda, Zen and Tibetan
traditions, along with meditation teachers and clinicians. From what they
heard, they built a classification of **59 types of experience, in 7
domains**: thinking, perception, emotion, the body, motivation and will,
sense of self, and social life.

Some examples, using the study's own names:

| Domain | Example categories |
|---|---|
| Perceptual | perceptual hypersensitivity; distortions in time or space; derealization (the world seems unreal) |
| Affective | fear, anxiety, panic or paranoia; depression, dysphoria or grief; re-experiencing of traumatic memories; affective flattening or emotional detachment |
| Somatic | sleep changes; involuntary movements; cardiac changes; pain; fatigue or weakness |
| Sense of self | loss of sense of agency; change in sense of embodiment; changes in self-other or self-world boundaries |

Two observations prevent a misreading of this table.

**First, the study does not measure frequency.** The participants were
chosen *because* they had had difficult experiences. The researchers
deliberately sought out the extreme cases, through referrals from teachers.
The study shows that these experiences exist and what they are like, not how
many practitioners have them.

**Second, the same experience can be lived in opposite ways.** Even when
descriptions were similar, interpretations and responses varied a great deal
across participants. For some the experience was very good, for others very
bad. The distress ranged from minimal and transient to severe and enduring.
A change in the sense of self can be read by one practitioner as progress
and by another as collapse.

One term from this family needs defining, because it comes up in personal
accounts and in clinics. **Depersonalization** is a persistent sense of
estrangement from yourself. It is like watching your own life from the
outside, as if your thoughts, emotions or body were not yours. When the
estrangement falls on the world around you, it is called derealization.

A feeling like this for a few moments during practice is not, by itself, a
sign of a problem. The warning sign is when it persists after the session,
frightens you, and gets in the way of your life.

## How Often

The short answer: **unpleasant experiences are common; impaired functioning
affects a minority, close to one person in ten in the surveys; lasting
impairment is rarer.** For frequency, the evidence is **preliminary**: the
numbers vary a great deal from one study to another. For the existence of
the effects, the evidence is **moderate**: several groups, using different
methods, reach the same conclusion.

The spread in the numbers has one main explanation: the questions are
different. A 2025 survey, with a sample of American adults resembling the
population, showed this directly. It measured the same thing four ways, in
the same people. The result ran from about one in ten to nearly eight in
ten, depending on the question.

![A 2025 survey of 886 American adults: the percentage who reported adverse effects of meditation, depending on the question asked. The four bars are the same people.](../illustrations/figures/fig-perguntas-adversos-en.svg)

The type of study matters too:

- **Trials that don't actively ask** about negative effects tend to record
  only what participants report on their own.
- **Surveys of practitioners** ask directly and cover years of practice,
  retreats included.
- **A single question** about an "unpleasant experience" captures mild and
  severe things together.

A review of 83 studies, by Farias and colleagues (2020), shows the weight of
the type of study. In the experimental studies, fewer than 4% of
participants had adverse events. In the observational ones, about a third.
The review also found events in people with no history of mental health
problems.

In surveys of practitioners, the numbers are higher. In Schlosser and
colleagues' survey (2019) of regular meditators, about a quarter said they
had had particularly unpleasant experiences they attributed to meditation.

Lasting impairment is the rarest finding. In a 2022 survey of a sample of
the American population, about one in ten people who had meditated reported
some impairment of functioning. Impairment that lasted a month or more
showed up in just over one in a hundred. And people who reported adverse
effects were as glad to have practiced meditation as those who did not.

The study that measured most carefully followed people through an
eight-week clinical program:

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Britton and colleagues, published in 2021. 96 people took one
of three variants of an eight-week MBCT program (mindfulness-based cognitive
therapy, defined in chapter {{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}).
An independent interviewer administered a 44-item interview on meditation
effects. For each item, it asked about occurrence, link to practice,
duration, whether it was good or bad, and impact on functioning.

**What it found.** 83% reported at least one meditation-related effect (good
or bad). Effects felt as bad appeared in 58%; effects that impaired
functioning, in 37%. **Lasting bad effects occurred in 6% to 14%** of the
sample. They came with signs of dysregulated arousal: hyperarousal (a state
of excessive alertness) and dissociation. The authors conclude that
transient distress and negative impacts occurred at rates similar to those
of other psychological treatments.

**Strength of evidence:** moderate. The measurement was careful and
independent, but the sample is modest and from a single program.
:::

Britton's study shows the effect of asking carefully. The same sample
produces very different numbers depending on whether you count any negative
experience or only lasting ones. None of these numbers is "the" rate.

## When the Risk Goes Up

The short answer: **some signals recur across studies, but the evidence is
preliminary.** These are studies of association: they show what goes
together, not what causes what. The signals:

- **Intensity and amount.** An **intensive retreat** is a period of several
  days, sometimes weeks or months, given over almost entirely to formal
  practice. It means many hours a day, usually in silence. In Schlosser's
  survey, having attended a retreat at some point was associated with more
  unpleasant experiences. That does not prove the retreat caused them:
  people who go on retreats practice more, and differently. But it is
  consistent with what teachers and practitioners report.
- **Type of practice.** In the same survey, people who did only
  "deconstructive" practices reported more unpleasant experiences. These
  are the practices that investigate the nature of experience and of the
  self, such as vipassanā (defined in
  chapter {{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}}).
- **Repetitive negative thinking.** There too, people more prone to
  rumination reported more unpleasant experiences.
- **Dysregulated arousal.** In Britton's study, lasting effects came with
  hyperarousal and dissociation.
- **Childhood adversity.** In the 2022 survey, it was associated with a
  higher risk of adverse effects.
- **Psychological distress and unusual experiences.** In the 2025 survey,
  four factors were associated with more adverse effects: psychological
  distress, psychoticism, unusual beliefs and having attended retreats.
- **Trauma.** In a 2025 trial of mindfulness programs for active depression,
  childhood trauma predicted lasting adverse effects. So did subclinical
  post-traumatic stress symptoms. The trial is small, but it points the same
  way as the surveys.

Some factors showed up as *protective* in Schlosser's survey: women and
religious participants reported fewer unpleasant experiences. The study
does not allow us to say why.

One point has to be clear. Farias and colleagues found adverse effects in
people with no history of mental health problems, too. There is no way to
know in advance who will have difficulty.

But anyone who already has a history of trauma, psychosis, bipolar
disorder, dissociation or severe depression has good reasons for two things:
to practice with qualified guidance, and to talk first with whoever is
treating them. For trauma, those reasons now have support in the studies
above. For the other groups, they are reasons of prudence, because the
studies do not yet measure the risk in them well.

## Neither Alarm nor Minimizing

There are two symmetrical mistakes you can make with these numbers.

**The alarm mistake** is to conclude that meditating is dangerous. Britton's
numbers need to be read alongside the authors' own conclusion: the rates
were similar to those of other psychological treatments. Psychological
treatments in general cause some transient distress in some people. That is
not an argument against them. It is an argument for doing them with someone
who knows what to do when something gets worse.

In the experimental studies pooled by Farias, the rate of adverse events was
below 4%. Most people have no problem. Short practices like the ones in this
book are low risk: low, not zero.

**The minimizing mistake** is to treat any difficulty as "part of the
process" and tell the person to practice more. It is a dangerous mistake,
because it turns a signal into an obstacle to be overcome. Many
practitioners and teachers read difficult states as a sign of progress, and
sometimes they can be. Lindahl and colleagues recorded exactly this
diversity of readings. But a teacher who has only that reading available is
no use to someone who is going under.

## Adjust, Stop, Seek Help

What follows is practical guidance based on prudence, not a clinical
protocol. It sorts the signs into three levels.

**Adjust your practice** when moderate discomfort comes up that passes after
the session: restlessness, strong emotion, strange sensations in the body.

1. Shorten your sessions. Five minutes done well are worth more than thirty
   endured.
2. Open your eyes and shift your anchor to something external or stable:
   sounds, your feet on the floor, the weight of your body in the chair.
3. Swap sitting practice for a moving practice, such as mindful walking, or
   for a kindness practice (the chapter
   {{cap:bondade-e-compaixao-como-treino}}).
4. Do things that "ground" you: exercise, eating, talking with people,
   practical tasks with your hands.

**Stop and talk to a teacher or a professional** when:

- the effects last beyond the session for days;
- your sleep gets persistently worse;
- panic attacks, depersonalization that won't go away, or intrusive
  traumatic memories appear;
- you notice that your work and relationships are being affected.

Stopping for a while is not failure; the practice will be there later.

**Seek help immediately** if you have:

- thoughts of hurting yourself or of ending your life;
- lost touch with reality: hearing or seeing things others don't perceive,
  strange certainties that won't let themselves be questioned;
- suffering you feel you cannot bear.

Wherever you are, call your local emergency number or a crisis line. In
Brazil:

- **CVV — Centro de Valorização da Vida: call 188.** Free, confidential
  emotional support, 24 hours a day, nationwide; also by chat on the CVV
  website.
- **SAMU (Brazil's emergency medical service): call 192** in an urgent
  situation, including psychiatric emergencies and suicide attempts. The
  service is free and runs 24 hours a day.
- **CAPS — Centros de Atenção Psicossocial (psychosocial care centers):**
  public mental health services of the SUS, Brazil's public health system,
  open to the community, that take in people in intense psychological
  distress. UPA 24h (24-hour urgent care units) and hospital emergency rooms
  also receive mental health crises.

When you see a professional, tell them that you meditate, how much and in
what way. Not every clinician knows about the effects of meditation, and
that information changes how your situation is read.

## Not a Substitute for Treatment

This is also the place to state, in so many words, a rule the book repeats
in every clinical chapter: **meditation is not a substitute for treatment**
for depression, anxiety, trauma, pain, insomnia or any other diagnosis.

The strongest evidence in the field is preventing depressive relapse with
MBCT. It comes from a structured program, led by a trained instructor, for a
specific group of patients. It does not come from solitary practice with an
app. And the best clinical results for meditation put it on a par with other
active treatments, not above them (the data are in
chapter {{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}).

If you are in treatment, don't stop it in order to meditate. Bring
meditation into the conversation with whoever is treating you.

## What This Chapter Established

- **Adverse effects of meditation exist** and have been documented by
  several research groups (moderate evidence). They also appear in people
  with no history of mental health problems and in eight-week clinical
  programs.
- Lindahl and colleagues mapped **59 types of experience in 7 domains**,
  from fear and panic to changes in the sense of self. The study shows that
  they exist, not how many people have them.
- **Frequency varies widely** depending on the question and the type of
  study (preliminary). Unpleasant experiences are common. Impaired
  functioning affects close to one person in ten in the surveys. Lasting
  impairment is rarer.
- The risk seems higher with intensive practice and retreats, exclusively
  deconstructive practices, rumination, dysregulated arousal, trauma and
  psychological distress (preliminary).
- Neither alarm nor minimizing: the rates resemble those of other
  psychological treatments. Persistent difficulty is a signal to adjust,
  stop or seek help, not to practice more.
- In a crisis, in Brazil: **CVV 188**, **SAMU 192**, CAPS, UPA and the
  emergency room. Meditation is not a substitute for treatment.
