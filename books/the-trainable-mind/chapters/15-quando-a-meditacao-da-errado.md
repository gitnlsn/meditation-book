This chapter answers a question most books on meditation avoid: can it do
harm? It can. There are documented adverse effects, some passing and some
lasting, and they also show up in people with no history of mental health
problems. The chapter lays out what is known about these effects, how often
they seem to happen, under what conditions they become more likely, and how
to recognize when it is time to adjust your practice, to stop, and to seek
help. The intended tone is that of a well-written medication leaflet:
neither an alarm nor a brochure that lists only benefits.

## Why This Chapter Exists

Research on meditation has mostly measured benefits. One example: of the 38
randomized trials on chronic pain pooled by Hilton and colleagues (2017),
only seven reported safety data. Britton and colleagues (2021) describe the
research on adverse effects in mindfulness programs as scarce and hampered
by methodological imprecision. The result was a public image in which
meditating is, at worst, useless.

That image does not come from the tradition. Lindahl and colleagues (2017)
point out that the Buddhist textual sources themselves, from which modern
programs draw the benefits, also recognize a range of other effects of
practice beyond those related to health. What got lost in the move to
wellness was not the knowledge that practice can destabilize; it was the
habit of talking about it.

It is worth keeping the three registers apart, as throughout the book.
**What the texts claim:** that serious practice can pass through difficult
states. **What practitioners report:** experiences of that kind, sometimes
severe. **What the studies show:** that negative effects also occur in
short clinical programs, and roughly how often. This chapter stays mostly in
the third register.

## What Counts as an Adverse Effect

An **adverse effect**, in health care, is any unwanted effect associated
with a treatment or practice — from a headache caused by a drug to a
symptom getting worse during therapy. For meditation, the definition needs
two refinements.

The first is to separate an adverse effect from **expected discomfort**.
Boredom, restlessness, a sore knee, sleepiness, the irritating discovery
that the mind doesn't stop — all of this is part of learning, the way
muscle soreness is part of taking up running. Running into emotions that
were there all along, and were being avoided, is also part of the practice.
None of this, by itself, is a sign that something has gone wrong.

The second refinement is to ask about more than one thing. Willoughby
Britton's team developed an interview that, for each experience, looks into
five aspects: whether it occurred, whether it is linked to meditation
practice, how long it lasted, whether it felt good or bad, and whether it
impaired the person's functioning — at work, in sleep, in relationships. An
unpleasant experience that passes in minutes and one that keeps someone from
working for weeks are not the same thing, and counting them together
misleads in both directions.

## The Map of Difficult Experiences

The study that organized the field was *The Varieties of Contemplative
Experience*, by Jared Lindahl, Willoughby Britton and colleagues (2017).
They interviewed 60 Western practitioners from Theravāda, Zen and Tibetan
traditions and 32 meditation teachers and clinicians, and sorted what they
heard into a taxonomy of **59 types of experience, in 7 domains**:
cognitive, perceptual, affective, somatic (of the body), conative (of
motivation and will), sense of self, and social.

Some examples, using the study's own names:

| Domain | Example categories |
|---|---|
| Perceptual | perceptual hypersensitivity; distortions in time or space; derealization (the world seems unreal) |
| Affective | fear, anxiety, panic or paranoia; depression, dysphoria or grief; re-experiencing of traumatic memories; affective flattening or emotional detachment |
| Somatic | sleep changes; involuntary movements; cardiac changes; pain; fatigue or weakness |
| Sense of self | loss of sense of agency; change in sense of embodiment; changes in self-other or self-world boundaries |

Two observations prevent a misreading of this table.

**First, the study does not measure frequency.** The participants were
chosen *because* they had had difficult experiences — the researchers
deliberately sought out the extreme cases, through referrals from teachers.
The study shows that these experiences exist and what they are like, not how
many practitioners have them.

**Second, the same experience can be lived in opposite ways.** The authors
report that even when descriptions were similar across participants,
interpretations and responses varied considerably, and the emotional tone
ranged from very positive to very negative; the level of distress ranged
from minimal and transient to severe and enduring. A change in the sense of
self can be read by one practitioner as progress and by another as
collapse.

One term from this family needs defining, because it comes up in personal
accounts and in clinics. **Depersonalization** is a persistent sense of
estrangement from yourself: as if you were watching your own life from the
outside, as if your thoughts, emotions or body were not yours. When the
estrangement falls on the world around you, it is called derealization. A
feeling like this for a few moments during practice is not, by itself, a
sign of a problem. The warning sign is when it persists after the session,
frightens you, and gets in the way of your life.

## How Often

Here the numbers vary a great deal, and the variation is part of the
information.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Britton and colleagues, published in 2021. 96 people took one
of three variants of an eight-week MBCT program (mindfulness-based cognitive
therapy, defined in chapter {{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}). An
independent interviewer administered a 44-item interview on meditation
effects, asking about each one's occurrence, link to practice, duration,
valence and impact on functioning.

**What it found.** 83% reported at least one meditation-related effect (good
or bad). Effects with a negative valence appeared in 58%; effects with a
negative impact on functioning, in 37%. **Lasting bad effects occurred in 6%
to 14%** of the sample, associated with signs of dysregulated arousal —
hyperarousal (a state of excessive alertness) and dissociation. The authors
conclude that transient distress and negative impacts occurred at rates
similar to those of other psychological treatments.

**Strength of evidence:** moderate. The measurement was careful and
independent, but the sample is modest and from a single program.
:::

Other estimates:

- **Farias and colleagues (2020)**, in a systematic review of 83 studies with
  6,703 participants, calculated a total prevalence of adverse events of
  **8.3%**. The figure changes with the type of study: 3.7% in experimental
  studies and 33.2% in observational ones. Anxiety, depression and cognitive
  anomalies were the most commonly reported types, and events occurred in
  people with no prior history of mental health problems.
- **Schlosser and colleagues (2019)** asked 1,232 regular meditators, in an
  online survey, whether they had ever had particularly unpleasant
  experiences they attributed to meditation: **25.6%** said yes.

Why anywhere from 3.7% to more than 30%? The most likely explanation is that
the questions are different. A trial that doesn't actively ask about
negative effects tends to record only what participants report on their own;
a survey of practitioners asks directly and covers years of practice,
retreats included; a single question about an "unpleasant experience"
captures mild and severe things together. Britton showed the effect of
asking carefully: the same sample produces very different numbers depending
on whether you count any negative experience or only lasting ones. None of
these numbers is "the" rate. Strength of evidence for frequency:
**preliminary**. For the existence of the effects: **moderate** — several
groups, using different methods, reach the same conclusion.

## When the Risk Goes Up

Research on risk factors is young, but some signals recur.

- **Intensity and amount.** An **intensive retreat** is a period of several
  days, sometimes weeks or months, given over almost entirely to formal
  practice, many hours a day and usually in silence. In Schlosser's survey,
  having attended a retreat at some point was associated with more
  unpleasant experiences. The association does not prove the retreat caused
  them — people who go on retreats practice more, and differently — but it
  is consistent with what teachers and practitioners report.
- **Type of practice.** In the same study, people who did only
  "deconstructive" practices (those that investigate the nature of
  experience and of the self, such as vipassanā, defined in
  chapter {{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}}) reported more unpleasant
  experiences than the others.
- **Repetitive negative thinking.** People more prone to rumination reported
  more unpleasant experiences.
- **Dysregulated arousal.** In Britton (2021), lasting effects were
  associated with hyperarousal and dissociation.

And some factors showed up as *protective* in Schlosser's survey: women and
religious participants reported fewer unpleasant experiences. The study
does not allow us to say why.

One point has to be clear: Farias and colleagues found adverse effects in
people with no history of mental health problems, too. There is no way to
know in advance who will have difficulty. But anyone who already has a
history of trauma, psychosis, bipolar disorder, dissociation or severe
depression has good reasons to practice with qualified guidance and to talk
first with whoever is treating them — reasons of prudence, since the studies
do not yet measure the risk in these groups well.

## Neither Alarm nor Minimizing

There are two symmetrical mistakes you can make with these numbers.

**The alarm mistake** is to conclude that meditating is dangerous. Britton's
numbers need to be read alongside the authors' own conclusion: the rates of
distress and negative impact were similar to those of other psychological
treatments. In other words: psychological treatments in general produce some
transient distress in some people, and that is not an argument against
them; it is an argument for doing them with someone who knows what to do
when something gets worse. In the experimental studies pooled by Farias, the
rate of adverse events was 3.7%. Most people have no problem, and short
practices like the ones in this book are low risk — low, not zero.

**The minimizing mistake** is to treat any difficulty as "part of the
process" and tell the person to practice more. It is a dangerous mistake,
because it turns a signal into an obstacle to be overcome. In the view of
many practitioners and teachers, difficult states are a sign of progress,
and sometimes they can be — Lindahl and colleagues recorded exactly this
diversity of interpretations. But a teacher who has only that reading
available is no use to someone who is going under.

## Adjust, Stop, Seek Help

What follows is practical guidance based on prudence, not a clinical
protocol. It sorts the signs into three levels.

**Adjust your practice** when moderate discomfort comes up that passes after
the session — restlessness, strong emotion, strange sensations in the body:

1. Shorten your sessions. Five minutes done well are worth more than thirty
   endured.
2. Open your eyes and shift your anchor to something external or stable:
   sounds, your feet on the floor, the weight of your body in the chair.
3. Swap sitting practice for a moving practice, such as mindful walking, or
   for a kindness practice (the chapter
   {{cap:bondade-e-compaixao-como-treino}}).
4. Do things that "ground" you: exercise, eating, talking with people,
   practical tasks with your hands.

**Stop and talk to a teacher or a professional** when the effects last
beyond the session for days, when your sleep gets persistently worse, when
panic attacks, depersonalization that won't go away, or intrusive traumatic
memories appear, or when you notice that your work and relationships are
being affected. Stopping for a while is not failure; the practice will be
there later.

**Seek help immediately** if you have thoughts of hurting yourself or of
ending your life, if you lose touch with reality (hearing or seeing things
others don't perceive, strange certainties that won't let themselves be
questioned), or if you are in suffering you feel you cannot bear. Wherever
you are, call your local emergency number or a crisis line. In Brazil:

- **CVV — Centro de Valorização da Vida: call 188.** Free, confidential
  emotional support, 24 hours a day, nationwide; also by chat on the CVV
  website.
- **SAMU (Brazil's emergency medical service): call 192** in an urgent
  situation, including psychiatric emergencies and suicide attempts. The
  service is free and runs 24 hours a day.
- **CAPS — Centros de Atenção Psicossocial (psychosocial care centers):**
  public mental health services of the SUS, Brazil's public health system,
  open to the community, that take in people in intense psychological
  distress. UPA 24h (24-hour urgent care units) and hospital emergency rooms
  also receive mental health crises.

When you see a professional, tell them that you meditate, how much and in
what way. Not every clinician knows about the effects of meditation, and
that information changes how your situation is read.

## Not a Substitute for Treatment

This is also the place to state, in so many words, a rule the book repeats
in every clinical chapter: **meditation is not a substitute for treatment**
for depression, anxiety, trauma, pain, insomnia or any other diagnosis. The
strongest evidence in the field — preventing depressive relapse with MBCT —
comes from a structured program, led by a trained instructor, for a
specific group of patients, not from solitary practice with an app. And the
best clinical results for meditation put it on a par with other active
treatments, not above them (the data are in
chapter {{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}). If you are in
treatment, don't stop it in order to meditate; bring meditation into the
conversation with whoever is treating you.

## What This Chapter Established

- **Adverse effects of meditation exist** and have been documented by
  several research groups (moderate evidence). They also appear in people
  with no history of mental health problems and in eight-week clinical
  programs.
- Lindahl and colleagues mapped **59 types of experience in 7 domains**,
  from fear and panic to changes in the sense of self; the study shows that
  they exist, not how many people have them.
- **Frequency varies widely** depending on the method: 8.3% overall in the
  Farias review (3.7% in experimental studies, 33.2% in observational ones);
  in MBCT, 6% to 14% with lasting bad effects (Britton). Preliminary.
- The risk seems higher with intensive practice and retreats, exclusively
  deconstructive practices, rumination and dysregulated arousal
  (preliminary).
- Neither alarm nor minimizing: the rates resemble those of other
  psychological treatments, and persistent difficulty is a signal to adjust,
  stop or seek help — not to practice more.
- In a crisis, in Brazil: **CVV 188**, **SAMU 192**, CAPS, UPA and the
  emergency room. Meditation is not a substitute for treatment.
