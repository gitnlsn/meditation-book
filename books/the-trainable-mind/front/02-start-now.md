This book leaves the practice for Part III, after the foundations. You don't
have to wait until then to practice. This page gives you a minimal practice,
five minutes long, to do from today while you read Parts I and II. It is the
simplest form of the practice taught in chapter
{{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}}, without the history and the science, which
come later.

## The Practice

Five minutes a day. Sit in a chair with your feet on the floor, your back
upright but not rigid, and your hands in your lap; your eyes can be closed or
half open, resting on the floor a little in front of you.

1. **Set an alarm** for five minutes. That way you don't have to check the
   clock.
2. **Find the breath** where it is clearest — at the nostrils, in the chest
   or in the belly — and stay with that spot for the session. It is your
   anchor: the place your attention will come back to.
3. **Don't change the breath.** Just notice how it is: long or short, deep or
   shallow. If it changes on its own as you watch it, that's fine.
4. **Your attention will drift off** — to a plan, a memory, a sound, a
   comment about the practice itself. That is not a failure. It is what the
   mind does all the time, and chapter {{cap:a-mente-que-divaga}} explains
   why.
5. **When you notice it has drifted, come back.** Note the fact without
   comment and bring your attention back to the breath. No scolding, no
   restarting the session, no concluding that you "can't meditate." That
   return is the exercise: each time you notice and come back is a
   repetition, like each lift in strength training. A session with thirty
   returns was not a bad session; it was a session with thirty repetitions.
6. **When the alarm goes off, stop.** It doesn't matter how it went.

## When to Do It

Pick a moment that already exists in your day and tie the practice to it:
after your coffee, when you get home, before you open your computer. Same
time, same place. A fixed trigger holds a practice up better than willpower
does, and chapter {{cap:uma-pratica-para-a-vida}} explains why. If you miss a
day, pick it up the next day, without making up for it.

At first, avoid practicing lying in bed at night. Lying down, sleep comes
easily, and the goal here is to train attention while awake. Using meditation
to fall asleep has its own place, in chapter {{cap:o-corpo-o-sono-e-a-dor}}.

## What Not to Expect

- **An empty mind.** Stopping thought is not the goal, either in the
  Buddhist texts or in practice that is well taught. Thoughts will keep
  showing up; the practice is noticing and coming back.
- **Guaranteed calm.** Some sessions will be peaceful; others restless,
  boring or sleepy. Noticing boredom, sleepiness or irritation is practice
  too.
- **Results within days.** What research shows about the benefits of
  meditation, and how strongly, is in chapter
  {{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}. That a few minutes a
  day are enough for a measurable effect is, for now, **preliminary**
  evidence (chapter {{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}}). Five minutes is a start,
  not a promise.

## If This Is Hard

For some people — especially those who have panic attacks, asthma or a
history of trauma — paying attention to the breath raises anxiety instead of
calming it. If that happens, switch anchors: the sounds around you, the soles
of your feet on the floor, the weight of your body in the chair. Open your
eyes. Cut the session to two or three minutes, or stop. If distress that
doesn't pass after the session, a persistent sense of unreality or intrusive
traumatic memories show up, stop and seek guidance; chapter
{{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}} describes these signs. If you have
thoughts of harming yourself, get help right away: wherever you are, call
your local emergency number or a crisis line; in Brazil, CVV (188, a free
emotional support line) or SAMU (192, the emergency medical service).
Meditation is not a substitute for treatment, and if you are in treatment,
you stay in it.

## From Here On

From chapter {{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}} on, Part III deepens this
practice — where it comes from, counting as a support, ten-minute sessions —
and adds others: noting what shows up, cultivating kindness, moving through
the body with attention. Until then, five minutes and the return are enough.
