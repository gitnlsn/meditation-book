These are the questions that tend to come up after the first days of
practice, when the novelty has worn off and the difficulties have stayed.
The answers are short and practical, and each one points to the chapter where
the subject is treated in depth. Where an answer rests on research, it says
how strong the evidence is. Where the sources of this book have no research
on the question, it says that too.

## Posture and Body

**Do I have to sit on the floor, cross-legged?**

No. The *Ānāpānasati Sutta* talks about sitting cross-legged, but a chair
works just as well (chapter {{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}}). Posture has a
job: to keep the body stable and alert without strain. Any position that does
that works: a chair with your feet on the floor, a cushion, a meditation
bench, standing. Lying down is possible too, at the cost of making you
sleepier. The body scan in chapter {{cap:o-corpo-o-sono-e-a-dor}} is done
that way.

**My back or my knees hurt. Am I doing something wrong?**

Probably not. Knee pain is among the expected discomforts of beginning.
Chapter {{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}} compares them to the muscle
soreness of someone who has just started running. But there is no merit in
enduring pain. Adjust: a higher cushion or a chair takes the weight off your
knees, and a backrest supports your lower back. If you need to shift position in the
middle of a session, move slowly, noticing the movement, and come back to the
anchor. New pain, pain that changes its pattern or pain that comes with other
symptoms needs a medical evaluation, not another cushion (chapter
{{cap:o-corpo-o-sono-e-a-dor}}).

**Eyes open or closed?**

Either works. Chapter {{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}} suggests closed or half
open, resting on the floor about a meter away. Half open helps
if you get sleepy. Open is the recommendation when practice stirs up anxiety
or strange sensations: opening your eyes is one of the first adjustments in
chapter {{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}}. None of this book's sources
compares the options in an experiment. Pick the one that keeps you alert and
at ease.

## How Much and When

**How long per session, and how often?**

To begin, five to ten minutes a day, every day. That is the dose of the
practice in chapter {{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}}. It is also the start of
the eight-week plan in chapter {{cap:uma-pratica-para-a-vida}}, which builds
up gradually to fifteen or twenty minutes. The most useful rule from that
chapter: start with the length you can manage on a bad day, not the one you
would like to manage on a good day. A short daily practice builds the habit;
a long, occasional one does not.

On the effect of short doses, the evidence is **preliminary**. The study of
soldiers described in chapter {{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}} offers a clue.
Participants were supposed to practice thirty minutes a day. They logged, on
average, about 178 minutes in total over the eight weeks. Even so, they kept
their attention more stable than the other groups. It is a small study. It
suggests modest doses count, but it does not establish a minimum dose.

**I missed a few days. Do I have to start over?**

No. In Lally and colleagues' study of habit formation, missing a day did not
materially affect the process (**preliminary** evidence, chapter
{{cap:uma-pratica-para-a-vida}}). Pick it up the next day, at your usual
length, without trying to make up for it.

**Morning or evening?**

None of this book's sources compares the two, so the answer is practical,
not scientific. The best time is the one you can repeat, tied to something
that already happens every day: after your coffee, when you get home
(chapter {{cap:uma-pratica-para-a-vida}}). The one difference that matters:
at night, in bed, practice tends to turn into sleep. That is good if the goal
is to sleep and bad if it is to train attention. For anyone who wants both,
chapter {{cap:o-corpo-o-sono-e-a-dor}} suggests a seated session at another
time of day.

## During the Session

**I fall asleep when I meditate.**

It happens, and the tradition even has a name for it: dullness and
drowsiness are one of the five hindrances to practice that the *Satipaṭṭhāna
Sutta* tells you to recognize (chapter {{cap:observar-sem-agarrar}}). What
tends to help:

- sitting instead of lying down;
- straightening your back;
- keeping your eyes half open;
- practicing at a time when you aren't exhausted;
- shortening the session.

If you fall asleep in every session, at any hour, you may simply not be
sleeping enough. Persistently poor sleep belongs to chapter
{{cap:o-corpo-o-sono-e-a-dor}}, not to more meditation. And if it is chronic
insomnia, it calls for its own treatment.

**I can't stop thinking. I don't think I'm cut out for this.**

Nobody stops thinking, and stopping is not the goal. The mind spends a large
share of its time away from what it is doing (chapter
{{cap:a-mente-que-divaga}} shows the data and their limits). Even
experienced practitioners wander during meditation (chapter
{{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}}). The practice is not keeping attention still.
It is noticing that it has left and bringing it back. A session in which you
noticed and came back fifty times had fifty repetitions. If you are too
scattered, use the counting from one to ten in chapter
{{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}}, which exists for exactly this. The sign that
you are doing it the hard way is not thinking a lot; it is fighting every
thought.

**I get bored and restless and want to get up.**

That is part of it too, and it has a name too: restlessness and remorse make
up another of the five hindrances (chapter {{cap:observar-sem-agarrar}}).
Boredom and restlessness are experiences like any others. You can note them
with a word ("bored," "restless"), notice where they show up in the body, and
come back to the anchor. That is the noting practice in chapter
{{cap:observar-sem-agarrar}}. If the restlessness is strong, shorten the
session rather than abandon it. Or switch to a practice in motion, such as
mindful walking (chapter {{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}}). A short
session you finish builds more habit than a long one you break off.

**Strong emotions came up: crying, anger, sadness. Is that normal?**

It can be. Meeting emotions that were there and were being avoided is part of
practice, and chapter {{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}} counts them among
the expected discomforts. Faced with a strong emotion that passes after the
session, the advice is to adjust:

- notice the emotion without pushing it away or feeding it, the "watching
  without grabbing" of chapter {{cap:observar-sem-agarrar}};
- open your eyes;
- swap the breath for an external anchor, such as sounds or your feet on
  the floor;
- shorten the session.

The picture changes in these cases:

- the effect lasts beyond the session for days;
- sleep gets persistently worse;
- panic attacks, a persistent sense of unreality or intrusive traumatic
  memories appear;
- work and relationships start to be affected.

Then the advice is to stop and talk to a teacher or a health professional.
Adverse effects of meditation are real and documented, including in
eight-week programs (**moderate** evidence that they exist). Chapter
{{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}} describes the signs.
If you have thoughts of harming yourself, or suffering you feel you cannot
bear, get help right away: wherever you are, call your local emergency number
or a crisis line. In Brazil: **CVV, 188** (free, confidential emotional
support), and **SAMU, 192** (the emergency medical service).

## Tools and Company

**Do meditation apps work?**

They help a little, in the short term, and that is as far as the research
lets you conclude.

::: {.relatorio}
**The study.** Jake Linardon and colleagues, published in 2024. A
meta-analysis of 45 randomized trials of mindfulness apps, measuring symptoms
of depression (5,852 participants) and anxiety (6,082). One of the authors is
Simon Goldberg, whose meta-analyses appear in chapter
{{cap:como-saber-se-algo-funciona}}.

**What it found.** Small effects in favor of the apps compared with control
groups: an effect size of 0.24 for depression and 0.28 for anxiety. The
results held when the analysis was limited to the trials with lower risk of
bias and larger samples. Against active treatments there was no significant
difference, but those studies were few. The authors call for higher-quality
studies with longer follow-ups.

**Strength of evidence:** moderate for a small, short-term effect against
controls; preliminary for any comparison with treatments.
:::

The other problem is sticking with it. In a 2023 review, the same Linardon
calculated that about a quarter of the people assigned to use a mindfulness
app dropped out of the study. In the larger studies, the share was higher
still. Data on actual use were poorly reported, but pointed to clear
problems with keeping people engaged over time. In other words, an app does not solve what
chapter {{cap:uma-pratica-para-a-vida}} calls structure.

In practice, an app is a good support for getting started, timing your
session and hearing guided instructions. The strongest clinical evidence in
this book is for preventing relapse of depression with MBCT. It comes from
instructor-led programs, not from apps (chapter
{{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}). And an app that
promises to "change your brain" or cure anxiety is promising more than the
research has measured.

**How do I choose a teacher or a group?**

Chapter {{cap:uma-pratica-para-a-vida}} gives the criteria in detail. In
short, a good teacher:

- states their training clearly, and the lineage or program they learned in;
- talks openly about difficulties and adverse effects, and knows where to
  refer you;
- does not promise a cure, quick enlightenment or guaranteed transformation;
- does not ask you to stop a treatment;
- does not create pressure for money, time or loyalty.

For a secular program, ask whether the instructor has certified training in
MBSR or MBCT, the two programs defined in chapter
{{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}. Remember that the
evidence for MBCT is specific: relapse prevention for people who have already
had recurrent depression, not treatment for an acute crisis. For a Buddhist
center, ask which tradition it belongs to and what assumptions that tradition
brings.

In Brazil, two starting points, checked in October 2026. Naming them is not a
seal of quality. The criteria above apply to them as well.

- **Centro Mente Aberta (Unifesp).** The mindfulness center of the Federal
  University of São Paulo (Unifesp), coordinated by a professor of
  preventive medicine. It offers courses and trains instructors in
  protocols such as MBCT and MBHP. MBHP is an eight-week health-promotion
  program created by the center's coordinator. The center says it follows
  British criteria for assessing mindfulness teaching. Website:
  mindfulness.unifesp.br.
- **The SUS (Brazil's public health system).** Since 2017 (Ministry of
  Health Ordinance No. 849), meditation has been part of the National Policy
  on Integrative and Complementary Practices (PNPIC). That allows the
  public system to offer it. It is worth asking at your local public health
  clinic whether it does. With one caveat: being in a public policy is an
  administrative decision, not proof of effectiveness. The ordinance
  describes meditation as an "instrumento de fortalecimento físico,
  emocional, mental, social e cognitivo" (a tool for physical, emotional,
  mental, social and cognitive strengthening). That goes beyond what the
  evidence in chapter {{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}
  supports.

## Health and Treatment

**Can I meditate while in therapy or on medication?**

Yes, and the rule that comes with the answer is firm: **do not stop or cut
back a treatment on your own in order to meditate**. One trial compared
MBCT with maintenance antidepressants (chapter
{{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}). In it, tapering the
medication was done under medical supervision, within a health system.

Bring meditation into the conversation with whoever is treating you: tell
them that you meditate, how much and in what way (chapter
{{cap:quando-a-meditacao-da-errado}}). Anyone with a history of trauma,
psychosis, bipolar disorder, dissociation or severe depression has good
reason to practice with qualified guidance. And to talk first with whoever
is treating them. Meditation is not a substitute for treatment.

**How do I know if it's working?**

Not by how any one session went. A restless session full of returns is a
training session. A calm session proves nothing. Chapter
{{cap:atencao-na-respiracao}} gives the signs that you are doing it right,
even when it doesn't feel that way: you lost track many times and noticed;
you felt boredom, sleepiness or irritation and noticed.

Over weeks, the most honest instrument is the log from the plan in chapter
{{cap:uma-pratica-para-a-vida}}: an X on the calendar and one sentence about
what you noticed. Reread it after a month. Ask about observable things, such
as whether you notice sooner when you are ruminating, or whether you come
back from irritation a little faster.

And calibrate your expectations by the evidence:

- the average effects of meditation programs on anxiety, depression and
  stress are small to moderate (**moderate** evidence, chapter
  {{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}});
- they vary from person to person and from setting to setting;
- long-term effects in longtime practitioners are **preliminary**, because
  people who have meditated for decades chose to meditate (chapter
  {{cap:uma-pratica-para-a-vida}}).

If nothing has changed after a few weeks, you did nothing wrong. If something
has gotten persistently worse, that is a signal to adjust or stop, not to
push on.

## Buddhism

**Do I have to become a Buddhist?**

Not to do the practices in this book. MBSR and MBCT are secular adaptations
of Buddhist practices. They were deliberately taken out of their religious
context so they could enter hospitals and clinics (chapter
{{cap:o-que-a-ciencia-da-meditacao-mostra-e-o-que}}).

But it would be inaccurate to say meditation is "just a technique."
Buddhism is a living, diverse religious tradition (chapter
{{cap:quem-foi-o-buda-e-quem-ele-nao-foi}}). Within it, meditation is one
of three parts of a training that includes ethics and wisdom. Taking it out
of the whole changes what it is (chapter
{{cap:o-caminho-e-onde-a-meditacao-entra-nele}}). Part I exists so that you
know what you are taking and what you are leaving behind when you practice
outside the tradition. Neither choice is the true version for everyone:
neither the secular program nor the path within a tradition. It is honest, though,
to know which of the two you chose.
