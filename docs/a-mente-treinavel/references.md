# Referências

O que é **real** neste livro, e onde foi conferido. Toda afirmação empírica do
manuscrito — sobre o cérebro, o corpo, a saúde, a história do budismo, o que um
texto canônico diz — remonta a uma entrada daqui, ou fica marcada
`[[?fato: …]]` na prosa, e `make marcadores` não deixa o capítulo passar de
`draft`.

Nunca reconstruir de memória um número, um tamanho de efeito, um tamanho de
amostra ou uma citação. Citação direta e número exato só depois de conferidos
contra a fonte primária em `sources/` e registrados em `sources/VERIFIED.md`.

---

## Como usar

Cada entrada diz o que a obra **dá ao livro**, não do que a obra trata. Onde um
livro de divulgação se apoia numa fonte primária, as duas entram: cita-se a
pesquisa, recomenda-se a divulgação.

Três cautelas embutidas na lista:

- **[core]** marca as obras com que o livro argumenta — estas se leem direito.
- **[care]** marca achados que enfraqueceram, foram contestados, não
  replicaram ou foram retratados depois de publicados. Estão aqui porque são
  famosos, e um livro que cita muito tem de lidar com eles honestamente.
  **Nunca usar um trabalho `[care]` sem a ressalva junto, no corpo do texto.**
- Boa parte da pesquisa sobre meditação tem amostras pequenas, grupos de
  controle fracos (lista de espera em vez de controle ativo), viés de
  publicação e inferência reversa a partir de imagens do cérebro. A crítica de
  Van Dam et al. (2018), *Mind the Hype*, fica atrás de toda afirmação
  empírica do livro.

As entradas abaixo foram conferidas na web em 2026-10-11, uma a uma; cada uma diz onde. Ficam em inglês — docs não se traduzem.

**Legend**
- **[core]**: a spine work the book should engage directly.
- **[care]**: a finding or work that is contested, weakened, overstated in popular use, or not peer reviewed. The caveat is stated in the entry.
- **Evidence labels**: *strong* (large, replicated, preregistered or meta-analytic with good quality); *moderate* (RCTs or meta-analyses with real limits); *preliminary* (small, single, uncontrolled or cross-sectional); *contested* (active, unresolved disagreement in the field). For primary texts, histories and philosophy, the label is *n/a (text/argument)*.

## 1. Buddhism: primary texts, introductions, and critical scholarship

**Thanissaro Bhikkhu (trans.). Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta: Setting the Wheel of Dhamma in Motion (SN 56.11).** Access to Insight. https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn56/sn56.011.than.html. [core]
Gives the book the Buddha's first discourse: the Four Noble Truths and the Middle Way, in a reliable free translation readers can open. Evidence: n/a (text).
Verified via https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn56/sn56.011.than.html (fetched; title, translator and content confirmed).

**Ñāṇamoli, Bhikkhu (trans.) & Bodhi, Bhikkhu (ed./rev.) (1995). The Middle Length Discourses of the Buddha: A New Translation of the Majjhima Nikāya.** Wisdom Publications / Barre Center for Buddhist Studies. ISBN 0-86171-072-X. [core]
The standard scholarly translation containing MN 10, the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta ("establishings of mindfulness"), which is the canonical source for most modern mindfulness. Free alternate translations of MN 10 are on Access to Insight. Evidence: n/a (text).
Verified via https://humanitieshub.sdsu.edu/omeka/items/show/1052 and https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BA59265713. Note: later editions exist (2001, 2009); cite the edition actually used.

**Ñāṇamoli Thera / Mendis / Thanissaro (trans.). Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta: The Discourse on the Not-self Characteristic (SN 22.59).** Access to Insight. https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn22/sn22.059.nymo.html. [core]
The second discourse: the five aggregates analyzed as impermanent, unsatisfactory and "not-self". This is the textual anchor for the chapter on the sense of self. Evidence: n/a (text).
Verified via https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn22/sn22.059.nymo.html and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anattalakkha%E1%B9%87a_Sutta.

**Thanissaro Bhikkhu (trans.). Kālāma Sutta / "To the Kalamas" (AN 3.65, also called Kesamutti Sutta).** Access to Insight. https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an03/an03.065.than.html. [core] [care]
Often quoted as "the Buddha said believe nothing, test everything". That fits the book's commitment to truth, but the caveat matters too: the translator's note says the text asks for more rigor than "follow your own judgment", and it is not a charter for modern empiricism (see Lopez, Thompson). Evidence: n/a (text).
Verified via https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an03/an03.065.than.html.

**Fronsdal, Gil (trans.) (2005). The Dhammapada: A New Translation of the Buddhist Classic with Annotations.** Shambhala. ISBN 9781590303801.
A clear, annotated translation of the most-quoted verse collection, translated by a Stanford PhD in Buddhist Studies who also teaches meditation. A good source of epigraphs. Evidence: n/a (text).
Verified via https://tricycle.org/?p=8122 and https://www.bookshare.org/browse/book/1372148. Note: some catalogs date it 2006.

**Bodhi, Bhikkhu (ed. & trans.) (2005). In the Buddha's Words: An Anthology of Discourses from the Pāli Canon.** Wisdom Publications (Teachings of the Buddha). ISBN 9780861714919. [core]
A thematic anthology in 10 chapters with introductions. It is the best single doorway into the early texts for lay readers. Evidence: n/a (text).
Verified via https://tricycle.org/magazine/books-brief-winter-2005/ and https://www.eagle.pitt.edu/index.php/bibcite/reference/7754.

**Rahula, Walpola (1959). What the Buddha Taught.** Gordon Fraser (Bedford); 2nd enlarged ed. 1967; Oneworld revised ed. 1997.
A classic Theravāda introduction by a monk-scholar. It is useful, but it is itself a product of Buddhist modernism: it presents Buddhism as rational and compatible with science (read it alongside McMahan). Evidence: n/a (text).
Verified via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_the_Buddha_Taught and https://librarycatalogue.taylors.edu.my/bib/33793. Note: sources disagree on whether a Grove Press edition also dates from 1959.

**Gethin, Rupert (1998). The Foundations of Buddhism.** Oxford University Press (Opus). ISBN 9780192892232. [core]
An academic but readable overview of what Theravāda, Tibetan and East Asian Buddhism share. It is the book's best guard against treating "Buddhism" as one thing. Evidence: n/a (scholarly synthesis).
Verified via https://oeaw.ac.at/resources/Record/993505912904498 and https://www.goodreads.com/work/editions/144039-the-foundations-of-buddhism-opus.

**Lopez, Donald S., Jr. (2008). Buddhism and Science: A Guide for the Perplexed.** University of Chicago Press. ISBN 9780226493121. [core]
A history of the claim that "Buddhism is compatible with science", from the reinterpretation of Mount Meru, through its uses in nineteenth-century race science, to the Dalai Lama and meditation labs. It is essential for the book's hype-literacy stance. Evidence: n/a (history).
Verified via https://www.press.uchicago.edu/Misc/Chicago/493121.html and https://www.lionsroar.com/is-buddhism-really-scientific/.

**Lopez, Donald S., Jr. (2012). The Scientific Buddha: His Short and Happy Life.** Yale University Press (Terry Lectures).
Argues that a "Scientific Buddha" was invented in nineteenth-century Europe and is routinely confused with the historical teacher. Short and pointed. Evidence: n/a (history).
Verified via https://nyjournalofbooks.com/book-review/scientific-buddha-his-short-and-happy-life-terry-lectures-series and https://news.yale.edu/2008/09/26/terry-lectures-will-examine-buddha-and-modern-science.

**Anālayo, Bhikkhu (2003). Satipaṭṭhāna: The Direct Path to Realization.** Windhorse Publications. ISBN 9781899579549. [core]
The standard scholarly-practitioner commentary on MN 10. Shows what the "four establishments of mindfulness" meant in context: much more than "non-judgmental present-moment awareness". A free PDF is hosted by Univ. Hamburg. Evidence: n/a (textual scholarship).
Verified via https://windhorse.com.au/9781899579549-satipatthana.html and https://www.buddhismuskunde.uni-hamburg.de/pdf/5-personen/analayo/direct-path.pdf.

**Anālayo, Bhikkhu (2018). Satipaṭṭhāna Meditation: A Practice Guide.** Windhorse Publications. ISBN 9781911407102.
The practical companion to the 2003 book, with guided audio. Useful for practice boxes in the book. Evidence: n/a (practice manual).
Verified via https://windhorse.com.au/9781911407102-satipatthana-meditation.html.

**Suzuki, Shunryu (1970). Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind.** Weatherhill (ed. Trudy Dixon).
The Sōtō Zen voice. The "beginner's mind" idea fits a beginner's book perfectly, and it shows a non-Theravāda, non-technique view of practice. Evidence: n/a (teaching text).
Verified via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zen_Mind,_Beginner%27s_Mind and https://web.stanford.edu/~funn/ZenMindBeginners.pdf.

**Thich Nhat Hanh (1975; English ed. Beacon Press 1976). The Miracle of Mindfulness.** Beacon Press (trans. Mobi Ho/Warren).
Vietnamese Thiền/Engaged Buddhism. Written as a manual for social workers, it is the warmest everyday-life mindfulness text. Evidence: n/a (teaching text).
Verified via https://plumvillage.org/books/1975-phep-la-cua-su-tinh-thuc-the-miracle-of-mindfulness and https://www.beacon.org/The-Miracle-of-Mindfulness-P1234.aspx. Note: the date of the English edition (1975 vs 1976) and the translator's name are given differently across sources.

**Dalai Lama (Tenzin Gyatso) (2005). The Universe in a Single Atom: The Convergence of Science and Spirituality.** Morgan Road Books.
The Tibetan voice, and the most influential insider argument for Buddhism–science dialogue (it grew out of the Mind & Life dialogues). Pair it with Lopez and Thompson. Evidence: n/a (argument).
Verified via https://physicstoday.aip.org/reviews/the-universe-in-a-single-atom-the-convergence-of-science-and-spirituality.

**Yongey Mingyur Rinpoche (with Eric Swanson) (2007). The Joy of Living: Unlocking the Secret and Science of Happiness.** Harmony Books. [care]
A Tibetan teacher who was also a subject in Davidson's lab, writing for beginners. [care] Its "science of happiness" framing is popular, and its neuroscience should not be cited as evidence; use it as a voice from the tradition. Evidence: n/a (popular).
Verified via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yongey_Mingyur_Rinpoche and https://bemis2.marmot.org/Record/.b59205271.

**Thompson, Evan (2020). Why I Am Not a Buddhist.** Yale University Press. ISBN 9780300226553. [core]
A philosopher-cognitive scientist friendly to Buddhism critiques "Buddhist exceptionalism", the idea that Buddhism is uniquely rational or scientific. Includes a reply to Wright's *Why Buddhism Is True*. This should be the book's main philosophical sparring partner. Evidence: n/a (argument).
Verified via https://readingreligion.org/books/why-i-am-not-buddhist and https://publishersweekly.com/978-0-300-22655-3.

**McMahan, David L. (2008). The Making of Buddhist Modernism.** Oxford University Press. ISBN 9780195183276. [core]
Shows how the meditation-centered, science-friendly, psychologized Buddhism most Western readers meet was shaped by encounters with modernity. It is critical but sympathetic. Evidence: n/a (history).
Verified via https://newbooksnetwork.com/david-mcmahan-the-making-of-buddhist-modernism-oxford-up-2008 and https://tricycle.org/magazine/how-buddhist-modern-buddhism/.

**Sharf, Robert H. (1995). Buddhist Modernism and the Rhetoric of Meditative Experience.** *Numen* 42(3): 228–283. [care]
Argues that "meditative experience" as the core of Buddhism is largely a twentieth-century reform-movement emphasis, and that talk of meditative states often served authority and legitimation. [care] The thesis is influential but disputed: some scholars argue for the centrality of experience in the Pāli canon. Evidence: contested (scholarly debate).
Verified via https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/handle/2027.42/43810?show=full.

**Sharf, Robert H. (2015). Is mindfulness Buddhist? (and why it matters).** *Transcultural Psychiatry* 52(4): 470–484. doi:10.1177/1363461514557561. [core]
Traces "bare attention" mindfulness to early-twentieth-century Burmese reform movements (e.g., the Mahasi lineage), and argues it is arguably at odds with more traditional Theravāda doctrine. Ideal for the chapter on what "mindfulness" actually means. Evidence: n/a (scholarly argument).
Verified via https://www.academia.edu/26441098 and https://www.mcgill.ca/tcpsych/training/advanced/previous/2013-asi.

**Purser, Ronald E. (2019). McMindfulness: How Mindfulness Became the New Capitalist Spirituality.** Repeater Books. ISBN 9781912248315. [care]
The sharpest critique of corporate, school and military mindfulness as self-pacification. [care] It is polemical. A *Times Higher Education* review faults it for not engaging the wider context, so present it as one side. Evidence: n/a (polemic).
Verified via https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/676584 and https://octoberbooks.org/products/9781912248315.

## 2. Neuroplasticity basics (and its limits)

**Hebb, D. O. (1949). The Organization of Behavior: A Neuropsychological Theory.** Wiley (New York) / Chapman & Hall. [core]
The origin of "Hebbian learning", usually paraphrased as "cells that fire together wire together" (that slogan is a later paraphrase, not Hebb's wording). The conceptual root of plasticity. Evidence: n/a (foundational theory; the mechanism is strongly supported at the synaptic level in later work).
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10643472/ and https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/000662046/Cite.

**Maguire, E. A., Gadian, D. G., Johnsrude, I. S., et al. (2000). Navigation-related structural change in the hippocampi of taxi drivers.** *PNAS* 97(8): 4398–4403. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC18253. [core] [care]
16 male London taxi drivers vs 50 controls. Drivers had larger posterior hippocampi and smaller anterior hippocampi, and volume correlated with years driving. [care] The design is cross-sectional, so it cannot by itself show that driving caused the difference (see Woollett & Maguire 2011). The finding was also a redistribution, not simply "bigger brain". Evidence: preliminary alone; moderate with the follow-ups below.
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC18253 and https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/Maguire/Maguire_CORE_2000.pdf.

**Maguire, E. A., Woollett, K., & Spiers, H. J. (2006). London taxi drivers and bus drivers: a structural MRI and neuropsychological analysis.** *Hippocampus* 16: 1091–1101. doi:10.1002/hipo.20233.
18 taxi drivers vs 17 bus drivers, matched for driving and stress. More mid-posterior and less anterior hippocampal grey matter in taxi drivers, and taxi drivers were *worse* at learning new visuo-spatial information. This is a good lesson that plasticity involves trade-offs. Evidence: preliminary to moderate.
Verified via https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Maguire2006.pdf and https://www.themantic-education.com/ibpsych/2019/02/01/key-study-london-taxi-drivers-vs-bus-drivers-maguire-2006/.

**Woollett, K., & Maguire, E. A. (2011). Acquiring "the Knowledge" of London's layout drives structural brain changes.** *Current Biology* 21(24). doi:10.1016/j.cub.2011.11.018. [core]
A longitudinal study of 79 trainees and 31 controls. Only the 39 who qualified showed a posterior hippocampal grey-matter increase after 3–4 years, and baseline brains did not differ. This is the strongest human evidence that intensive learning changes adult brain structure. Note the timescale: years, not weeks. Evidence: moderate.
Verified via https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/12/111208125720.htm and https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/Maguire/WoollettMaguireCBDec2011.pdf.

**Draganski, B., Gaser, C., Busch, V., Schuierer, G., Bogdahn, U., & May, A. (2004). Neuroplasticity: changes in grey matter induced by training.** *Nature* 427: 311–312. doi:10.1038/427311a.
24 non-jugglers: those who learned a three-ball cascade showed a transient grey-matter increase in mid-temporal area hMT/V5 and the posterior intraparietal sulcus, which *receded* three months after they stopped. A good image for "use it or lose it". Evidence: preliminary (small sample, MRI-based; see Thomas & Baker).
Verified via https://www.nature.com/articles/427311a.pdf (search listing) and https://staff.washington.edu/chudler/jugg.html. Note (revisão 2026-10-19): still not verified at source; PubMed has no abstract (`draganski-2004-grey-matter-training-pubmed-abstract-en.txt` holds the citation only), so "three months" stays out of the prose. See also Wenger et al. 2017 (expansion–renormalization).

**Thomas, C., & Baker, C. I. (2013). Teaching an adult brain new tricks: a critical review of evidence for training-dependent structural plasticity in humans.** *NeuroImage* 73: 225–236. [core]
Argues that given MRI's coarse resolution, many reported training-induced structural changes may reflect design limits, statistics and artifacts. The one robust result they identify is anterior hippocampal volume increase with exercise in older adults. Key for tempering "your brain rewires in 8 weeks". Evidence: n/a (critical review); it is itself part of an ongoing debate (see Fields' reply in the same issue).
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22484409/ (search listing with authors and pages).

**Sorrells, S. F., Paredes, M. F., et al. (2018). Human hippocampal neurogenesis drops sharply in children to undetectable levels in adults.** *Nature* 555: 377–381. [core] [care]
Found no young neurons in the dentate gyrus of adults aged 18–77. Critics (Lucassen et al.; Kempermann et al.) point to its reliance on two markers and to post-mortem tissue preservation. One side of a live controversy. Evidence: contested.
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29513649/ and https://snyderlab.com/2018/03/07/wtf-no-neurogenesis-in-humans.

**Boldrini, M., Fulmore, C. A., et al. (2018). Human hippocampal neurogenesis persists throughout aging.** *Cell Stem Cell* 22(4): 589–599.e5. doi:10.1016/j.stem.2018.03.015. [care]
Post-mortem hippocampi from 28 people aged 14–79 who died suddenly. Reports persistent neurogenesis into the eighth decade, despite a smaller progenitor pool and fewer new blood vessels with age. Published weeks after Sorrells and reaching the opposite conclusion. Evidence: contested.
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5957089. Note: n = 28 is from press coverage, not the abstract.

**Moreno-Jiménez, E. P., Flor-García, M., ... Llorens-Martín, M. (2019). Adult hippocampal neurogenesis is abundant in neurologically healthy subjects and drops sharply in patients with Alzheimer's disease.** *Nature Medicine* 25(4): 554–560. doi:10.1038/s41591-019-0375-9. [care]
With improved tissue processing, it found immature neurons into the ninth decade in healthy brains, and reduced numbers in Alzheimer's disease. Supports the "persists" side, but the question is not closed. Its reliance on DCX was challenged by Sorrells et al. 2021. Evidence: contested.
Verified via https://portalcientifico.universidadeuropea.com/documentos/6038db0485382a6b3d7bb934 and https://thesciencebreaker.org/breaks/neurobiology/hunting-new-born-neurons-in-the-human-adult-brain; primary source (revisão 2026-10-19): `moreno-jimenez-2019-neurogenesis-alzheimer-pubmed-abstract-en.txt` (PMID 30911133).

**Kempermann, G., Gage, F. H., et al. (2018). Human adult neurogenesis: evidence and remaining questions.** *Cell Stem Cell* 23(1): 25–30. doi:10.1016/j.stem.2018.04.004.
An 18-author response arguing there is no current reason to abandon the idea of human adult hippocampal neurogenesis, while listing the open questions. Useful for presenting the debate fairly. Evidence: n/a (expert review); topic contested. Note (plain-language pass, 2026-10-19): no chapter cites it now; "Neuroplasticidade sem exagero" tells the debate through Sorrells 2018/2021, Boldrini 2018 and Dumitru 2025.
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29681514/ and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6035081/; file (revisão 2026-10-19): `kempermann-2018-human-adult-neurogenesis-pubmed-abstract-en.txt`.

**Doidge, Norman (2007). The Brain That Changes Itself.** Viking. [care]
The bestseller that popularized "neuroplasticity", using vivid clinical cases such as stroke recovery. [care] It is a popular, case-based book; do not use it as evidence for specific claims. It is useful to show *where* the public idea of neuroplasticity comes from. Evidence: n/a (popular).
Verified via https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/brain-changes-itself.

## 3. Meditation neuroscience

**Raichle, M. E., MacLeod, A. M., Snyder, A. Z., Powers, W. J., Gusnard, D. A., & Shulman, G. L. (2001). A default mode of brain function.** *PNAS* 98(2): 676–682. doi:10.1073/pnas.98.2.676. [core]
The paper that named the "default mode". This is the background for every later meditation–DMN claim. Evidence: strong (the DMN as a network is robustly replicated). Its *functional* interpretations are still debated. Later work describes the DMN as multiple interwoven networks (Buckner & DiNicola 2019), and mind-wandering also recruits non-DMN regions (Fox et al. 2015).
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/14647; title verified (revisão 2026-10-19) from `raichle-2001-default-mode-pubmed-abstract-en.txt` (PMID 11209064): "A default mode of brain function".

**Killingsworth, M. A., & Gilbert, D. T. (2010). A wandering mind is an unhappy mind.** *Science* 330(6006): 932. [core]
Experience sampling via an iPhone app: the analysis used 2,250 adults (the ~250,000 samples from ~5,000 people describe the whole database, not this analysis — corrected 2026-10-11 against the paper). Minds wandered 46.9% of waking time, and people were less happy when wandering. Time-lag analyses "strongly suggested" (the paper's words) that wandering preceded unhappiness. The best opening hook for the book. Frequency later pooled at about 34.5% (Kawashima et al. 2023). The time-lag causal claim is contested: Poerio et al. 2013 found sadness preceded wandering; Gross et al. 2025 found thought valence largely explains the mood effect; the Kam et al. 2024 meta-analysis gives r = −.18, contingent on content and intentionality. Evidence (revisão 2026-10-19): strong that mind-wandering is frequent (the exact share depends on the method); moderate for a small association with worse mood; contested for causation (large sample, but self-selected app users, correlational).
Verified via https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2010/11/wandering-mind-not-a-happy-mind/ and https://www.imm.uzh.ch/news/schatzkammer/Science_2010.330.932.pdf.

**Smallwood, J., & Schooler, J. W. (2015). The science of mind wandering: empirically navigating the stream of consciousness.** *Annual Review of Psychology* 66: 487–518. doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-010814-015331.
Balances Killingsworth. Mind-wandering has costs (errors) but also benefits (creativity, future planning). It prevents the book from treating wandering as simply bad. The creativity benefit is contested (Baird et al. 2012 vs Smeekens & Kane 2016). Evidence: n/a (review); moderate.
Verified via https://labs.psych.ucsb.edu/schooler/jonathan/node/222 and https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/103321.

**Lazar, S. W., Kerr, C. E., Wasserman, R. H., et al. (2005). Meditation experience is associated with increased cortical thickness.** *NeuroReport* 16(17): 1893–1897. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1361002. [care]
20 experienced insight meditators vs 15 controls. Thicker prefrontal cortex and right anterior insula. [care] It is cross-sectional and small. The authors themselves said longitudinal work was needed, so it cannot show meditation *caused* the differences. Evidence: preliminary.
Verified via https://www.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/publications/journal_articles/667 and https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-meditation-alters-brain.

**Hölzel, B. K., Carmody, J., Evans, K. C., et al. (2010). Stress reduction correlates with structural changes in the amygdala.** *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience* 5(1): 11–17. doi:10.1093/scan/nsp034. [care]
26 stressed adults in MBSR. Reductions in perceived stress correlated with decreased right basolateral amygdala grey-matter density. [care] There was no control group, the sample was small, and the effect was not reproduced in Kral et al. 2022. Evidence: preliminary.
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2840837.

**Hölzel, B. K., Carmody, J., Vangel, M., et al. (2011). Mindfulness practice leads to increases in regional brain gray matter density.** *Psychiatry Research: Neuroimaging* 191(1): 36–43. [core] [care]
16 MBSR participants vs 17 waitlist controls. Grey-matter concentration increases in the left hippocampus, posterior cingulate, TPJ and cerebellum. This is the main source of the "meditation grows your brain in 8 weeks" headline. [care] A small, non-randomized, waitlist-controlled study that did **not** replicate in Kral et al. 2022 (n = 218, RCT). Evidence: preliminary, weakened by non-replication.
Verified via https://nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/publications/journal_articles/6277 and https://www.psychiatry.wisc.edu/courses/Nitschke/seminar/Holzel%202011%20Psychiatry%20Res%20Neuroimag.pdf.

**Hölzel, B. K., Lazar, S. W., Gard, T., Schuman-Olivier, Z., Vago, D. R., & Ott, U. (2011). How does mindfulness meditation work? Proposing mechanisms of action from a conceptual and neural perspective.** *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 6(6): 537–559. doi:10.1177/1745691611419671. [core]
Proposes four interacting mechanisms: attention regulation, body awareness, emotion regulation (reappraisal, exposure/extinction), and change in perspective on the self. A great skeleton for a beginner's chapter structure. Evidence: n/a (theoretical framework). The neural claims inherit the weaknesses of the studies it cites.
Verified via https://www.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/publications/journal_articles/396 and https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/10/111031154134.htm.

**Brewer, J. A., Worhunsky, P. D., Gray, J. R., Tang, Y.-Y., Weber, J., & Kober, H. (2011). Meditation experience is associated with differences in default mode network activity and connectivity.** *PNAS* 108(50): 20254–20259. doi:10.1073/pnas.1112029108. [core]
Experienced meditators showed relative deactivation of the medial PFC and posterior cingulate (DMN hubs) across three meditation types, and stronger PCC–dACC–dlPFC coupling. The authors read this as consistent with less mind-wandering. Replicated by the same lab (Garrison et al. 2015), not independently. Meta-analyses: Rahrig et al. 2022 (small salience–DMN connectivity increase after training, g = .234) and Ganesan et al. 2022 (default-mode, salience and executive networks in focused-attention meditation). Evidence: preliminary (small, cross-sectional; "consistent with" is reverse inference; see Poldrack 2006).
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22114193/ and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/3250176.

**Lutz, A., Greischar, L. L., Rawlings, N. B., Ricard, M., & Davidson, R. J. (2004). Long-term meditators self-induce high-amplitude gamma synchrony during mental practice.** *PNAS* 101(46): 16369–16373. doi:10.1073/pnas.0407401101. [core] [care]
Long-term Tibetan practitioners showed high-amplitude EEG gamma synchrony during meditation, elevated even at baseline. This is the "monks' brains" study. [care] It is small and cross-sectional, and practitioners differ from controls in many ways. The paper itself notes muscle artifact above about 42 Hz. Scalp-EEG gamma is vulnerable to eye-movement artifacts (Yuval-Greenberg 2008). It is not evidence that "gamma = enlightenment". Evidence: preliminary.
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC526201.

**Lutz, A., Slagter, H. A., Dunne, J. D., & Davidson, R. J. (2008). Attention regulation and monitoring in meditation.** *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 12(4): 163–169. [core]
Introduces the influential distinction between *focused attention* and *open monitoring* practice. It gives the book a vocabulary for "types" of meditation. Evidence: n/a (conceptual review).
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18329323/ and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2693206/.

**Dahl, C. J., Lutz, A., & Davidson, R. J. (2015). Reconstructing and deconstructing the self: cognitive mechanisms in meditation practice.** *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 19(9): 515–523. doi:10.1016/j.tics.2015.07.001. [core]
Three families of meditation (attentional, constructive, deconstructive), linked to meta-awareness, perspective taking/reappraisal, and self-inquiry. It is the bridge between the self chapter and the practice chapters. Evidence: n/a (conceptual framework).
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26231761/.

**Tang, Y.-Y., Hölzel, B. K., & Posner, M. I. (2015). The neuroscience of mindfulness meditation.** *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 16: 213–225. doi:10.1038/nrn3916. [core]
The most-cited overview. The abstract itself says the mechanisms remain unclear and that more rigorous studies are needed, which is useful to quote. Evidence: n/a (review); underlying literature mostly preliminary to moderate.
Verified via https://www.proquest.com/docview/1667988880 and https://search.asu.edu/profile/3993071.

**Fox, K. C. R., Nijeboer, S., Dixon, M. L., et al. (2014). Is meditation associated with altered brain structure? A systematic review and meta-analysis of morphometric neuroimaging in meditation practitioners.** *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* 43: 48–73. [core] [care]
21 studies, about 300 practitioners. Eight regions are consistently different (e.g., frontopolar cortex, insula/sensory cortices, hippocampus, ACC), with a medium global effect (d = 0.46). [care] The authors themselves flag publication bias and methodological limits as strong concerns. Most included studies are cross-sectional (as Kral et al. 2022 note). Evidence: moderate for "meditators differ"; preliminary for "meditation causes it".
Verified via https://scottbarrykaufman.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/fox2014.pdf (author PDF) and search listing; primary abstract (revisão 2026-10-19): `fox-2014-morphometric-meditation-pubmed-abstract-en.txt` (PMID 24705269).

**Kral, T. R. A., Davis, K., Korponay, C., ... Davidson, R. J. (2022). Absence of structural brain changes from mindfulness-based stress reduction: Two combined randomized controlled trials.** *Science Advances* 8(20): eabk3316. [core]
218 meditation-naive adults were randomized to MBSR (n = 75), an active control (HEP, n = 73), or waitlist (n = 70). There were **no** MBSR-specific changes in grey-matter volume, density or cortical thickness. The authors suggest that "only with much longer duration of training and/or training explicitly focused on a single form of practice" might structural alterations be identified (Discussion, full text). It comes from Davidson's own lab, a mindfulness-research center, which makes it all the more credible. **Most important single correction to the "8 weeks rewires your brain" story.** Evidence (revisão 2026-10-19): the claim that 8 weeks of MBSR changes brain structure is **contested**; the largest and best-controlled trial did not find it. (Previously "strong (for absence…)"; changed to fit the four-label rule and the chapters.)
Verified via https://www.centerhealthyminds.org/news/new-research-shows-no-evidence-of-structural-brain-change-with-short-term-mindfulnesstraining and https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.06.14.21258762.full.pdf (preprint); primary sources (revisão 2026-10-19): `kral-2022-absence-structural-pubmed-abstract-en.txt` (PMID 35594344) and `kral-2022-absence-structural-pmc-en.txt` (PMC9122316, full text).

**Van Dam, N. T., van Vugt, M. K., Vago, D. R., ... Meyer, D. E. (2018). Mind the Hype: A Critical Evaluation and Prescriptive Agenda for Research on Mindfulness and Meditation.** *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 13(1): 36–61. doi:10.1177/1745691617709589. [core]
Fifteen researchers, many of them meditation scientists (Lazar, Britton, Saron), warn that misinformation and weak methods risk misleading the public. The paper covers definition problems, measurement, adverse effects and neuroimaging. This is the book's methodological north star. Evidence: n/a (expert critique). Note (plain-language pass, 2026-10-19): still cited in "Como saber se algo funciona"; no longer cited in "O que a ciência da meditação mostra — e o que não mostra".
Verified via https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/1745691617709589/.

**Goleman, Daniel, & Davidson, Richard J. (2017). Altered Traits: Science Reveals How Meditation Changes Your Mind, Brain, and Body.** Avery. ISBN 9780399184383. (UK title: *The Science of Meditation*.) [core] [care]
Written by two insiders, and more self-critical than most popular books. Its distinction between temporary *states* and lasting *traits* is useful. [care] It is written by a lab director about his own field, and the Kral 2022 non-replication postdates it. Check any specific claim against the primary papers. Evidence: n/a (popular synthesis).
Verified via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altered_Traits and https://www.librarything.com/work/19651922.

## 4. Clinical evidence

**Kabat-Zinn, J. (1982). An outpatient program in behavioral medicine for chronic pain patients based on the practice of mindfulness meditation: theoretical considerations and preliminary results.** *General Hospital Psychiatry* 4(1): 33–47. [core] [care]
The founding MBSR paper: 51 chronic-pain patients, a 10-week program, and large reductions in mood disturbance. [care] It was uncontrolled, and the abstract itself calls the results preliminary. It matters historically, not as proof. Evidence: preliminary.
Verified via https://www.qigonginstitute.org/abstract-print/2954 and https://profiles.umassmed.edu/display/97704.

**Kabat-Zinn, J. (1990; revised ed. 2013). Full Catastrophe Living: Using the Wisdom of Your Body and Mind to Face Stress, Pain, and Illness.** Delacorte (1990); Bantam revised ed. (2013).
The MBSR manual for the public. Use it as a source for how MBSR is actually taught, and for its framing. Evidence: n/a (program manual).
Verified via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_Catastrophe_Living and https://www.abebooks.com/9780345536938/FULL-CATASTROPHE-LIVING-USING-WISDOM-0345536932/plp.

**Kabat-Zinn, J. (2003). Mindfulness-based interventions in context: past, present, and future.** *Clinical Psychology: Science and Practice* 10(2): 144–156. doi:10.1093/clipsy.bpg016.
The founder's own account of what mindfulness is, where it came from, and how it entered medicine. Pair it with Sharf 2015. Evidence: n/a (commentary). Note (plain-language pass, 2026-10-19): still cited in "O caminho, e onde a meditação entra nele"; no longer cited in "O que a ciência da meditação mostra — e o que não mostra".
Verified via https://contextualscience.org/publications/mindfulnessbased_interventions_context_past_present_future_2003. Note: the exact wording of his famous definition was not confirmed from this source; quote it from the PDF at jonkabat-zinn.com.

**Goyal, M., Singh, S., Sibinga, E. M. S., et al. (2014). Meditation programs for psychological stress and well-being: a systematic review and meta-analysis.** *JAMA Internal Medicine* 174(3): 357–368. [core]
47 trials and 3,515 participants, with **active controls only**. Moderate evidence for small improvements in anxiety, depression and pain; low evidence for stress and quality of life; low or insufficient evidence for attention, sleep, weight and substance use. **No evidence that meditation beat active treatments** (drugs, exercise, other therapies). Press coverage inflated it ("as good as antidepressants"). Evidence: moderate.
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24395196/ and https://stafforini.com/works/goyal-2014-meditation-programs-for/.

**Kuyken, W., Hayes, R., Barrett, B., et al. (2015). Effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of mindfulness-based cognitive therapy compared with maintenance antidepressant treatment in the prevention of depressive relapse or recurrence (PREVENT): a randomised controlled trial.** *The Lancet* 386: 63–73. doi:10.1016/S0140-6736(14)62222-4. [core]
424 adults with 3 or more prior depressive episodes. MBCT with support to taper antidepressants vs maintenance antidepressants, over 2 years. Relapse was 44% vs 47%, which is **not** a significant difference (HR 0.89). The correct framing is "an alternative for some people", not "better than pills". Evidence (revisão 2026-10-19): moderate (one large RCT; the null was repeated in the Breedvelt et al. 2021 IPD meta-analysis). Previously "strong"; changed to match the chapter.
Verified via https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/alert/mindfulness-therapy-may-provide-an-alternative-to-continuing-antidepressants-in-preventing-recurrence-of-depression and https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:ccf5d708-91ac-411f-9f63-654aba7b7a6c.

**Kuyken, W., Warren, F. C., Taylor, R. S., et al. (2016). Efficacy of Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy in Prevention of Depressive Relapse: An Individual Patient Data Meta-analysis.** *JAMA Psychiatry* 73(6): 565–574. doi:10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2016.0076. [core]
9 RCTs and 1,258 patients with data (of 1,329). Relapse within 60 weeks was 38% with MBCT vs 49% without, and the benefit was larger with more residual symptoms. This is the strongest clinical claim in the field, and it is specific to people with *recurrent* depression. Evidence (revisão 2026-10-19): moderate to strong (previously "strong"; changed to match the chapter).
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27119968/ and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6640038/.

**Hoge, E. A., Bui, E., Mete, M., et al. (2023). Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction vs Escitalopram for the Treatment of Adults With Anxiety Disorders: A Randomized Clinical Trial.** *JAMA Psychiatry* 80(1): 13–21 (online Nov 2022). [core]
276 randomized, 208 analyzed. MBSR was **non-inferior** to escitalopram on CGI-S, with mean reductions of 1.35 vs 1.43 points. Adverse events: 78.6% (drug) vs 15.4% (MBSR). The authors call for replication. The remote, videoconference phase (Hoge et al. 2025) did not confirm non-inferiority; secondary outcomes through 24 weeks are in Hu et al. 2024. Evidence: moderate (single RCT, unblinded, completers analysis; in-person delivery only).
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36350591/ and https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2023/0600/poems-mindfulness-stress-reduction.html.

**Galante, J., Friedrich, C., Dawson, A. F., et al. (2021). Mindfulness-based programmes for mental health promotion in adults in nonclinical settings: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials.** *PLOS Medicine* 18(1): e1003481. doi:10.1371/journal.pmed.1003481. [core]
136 RCTs and 11,605 participants in 29 countries. Compared with *no intervention*, MBPs improve anxiety, depression, distress and well-being "in most but not all scenarios" (abstract; corrected (revisão 2026-10-19) from "in more than 1 in 20 settings they may not help", which was an interpretation of the prediction intervals). Against nonspecific active controls MBPs still improved depression (SMD −0.46), and against specific active controls there was no statistically significant evidence of superiority (corrected from "Not compared with other active practices", which was inaccurate). The most common control group was passive (no intervention or waitlist), used in 96 trials (71%) (full text, `galante-2021-mbp-nonclinical-plosmed-en.pdf`). Evidence: moderate.
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7799763 and https://cambridgebrc.nihr.ac.uk/2021/01/12/mindfulness-based-programmes-for-mental-health-promotion-in-adults-in-nonclinical-settings-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-randomised-controlled-trials/.

**Galante, J., Friedrich, C., et al. (2023). Systematic review and individual participant data meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials assessing mindfulness-based programs for mental health promotion.** *Nature Mental Health* 1: 462–476. doi:10.1038/s44220-023-00081-5.
13 trials and 2,371 community adults. Teacher-led, in-person MBPs reduced distress vs passive controls (SMD −0.32) at 1–6 months, with no clear moderators. Caveats: self-report, no blinding, passive controls, and under-representation of men, less-educated people and people over 70. The user's brief said "PLOS Med 2023"; the 2023 paper is in *Nature Mental Health*. Evidence: moderate.
Verified via https://www.psypost.org/2023/08/new-meta-analysis-confirms-mindfulness-based-programs-reduce-psychological-distress-167726 and https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/systematic-review-and-individual-participant-data-meta-analysis-o/.

**Kuyken, W., Ball, S., Crane, C., et al. (2022). Effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of universal school-based mindfulness training compared with normal school provision in reducing risk of mental health problems and promoting well-being in adolescence: the MYRIAD cluster randomised controlled trial.** *Evidence-Based Mental Health* 25(3): 99–109. doi:10.1136/ebmental-2021-300396. [core] [care]
84 schools and 8,376 students (ages about 11–14). **No evidence** that school-based mindfulness training beat usual teaching at 1 year on depression risk, social-emotional-behavioural functioning or well-being. A companion paper (Montero-Marin et al. 2022) found small *worse* scores in at-risk students, which were not clinically meaningful. A very large, well-run null result from a pro-mindfulness team. Later: the Galla et al. 2024 reanalysis, Holst et al. 2026 and Webb et al. 2026 confirm the null. Evidence (revisão 2026-10-19): moderate to strong (for the null in universal school delivery; previously "strong", changed to match the chapter).
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9340028/ (fetched) and https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9340034/.

**Creswell, J. D. (2017). Mindfulness Interventions.** *Annual Review of Psychology* 68: 491–516. doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-042716-051139.
A balanced review of RCTs across health, cognition, affect and relationships; settings such as the workplace, military and schools; mechanisms, dosing and risks. A good one-stop academic reference for the clinical chapter. Evidence: n/a (review).
Verified via https://sources.mandala.library.virginia.edu/node/64686 and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Creswell.

**Goldberg, S. B., Tucker, R. P., Greene, P. A., et al. (2018). Mindfulness-based interventions for psychiatric disorders: A systematic review and meta-analysis.** *Clinical Psychology Review* 59: 52–60. [core]
142 samples and 12,005 participants. Per the abstract (corrected 2026-10-11): d = 0.55 vs no treatment, 0.37 vs minimal treatment, 0.35 vs non-specific active controls, 0.23 vs specific active controls, and **−0.004 vs evidence-based treatments** (equivalent). The best support is for depression, pain, smoking and addictions; the smoking advantage is now contested (see the chapter "Atenção, emoção e hábito": the Goldberg et al. 2022 d = 0.42 vs evidence-based treatments is post-treatment, from 4 studies, and the Cochrane review of Jackson et al. 2022 found no clear benefit). (The brief's "Goldberg 2017" is this paper; it was online in 2017 and in print in 2018.) Evidence: moderate.
Verified via https://repository.lsu.edu/psychology_pubs/1041 and https://www.mindandlife.org/media/meditation-for-mental-health-how-does-mindfulness-compare-to-other-treatments/.

**Hilton, L., Hempel, S., Ewing, B. A., et al. (2017). Mindfulness meditation for chronic pain: systematic review and meta-analysis.** *Annals of Behavioral Medicine* 51(2): 199–213. doi:10.1007/s12160-016-9844-2. [care]
38 RCTs. **Low-quality** evidence (GRADE) of a *small* reduction in pain vs controls, with better support for depression and quality of life. Use it to temper pain claims. Evidence: preliminary-to-moderate (low quality for the pain outcome).
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5368208.

**Zeidan, F., Emerson, N. M., Farris, S. R., et al. (2015). Mindfulness meditation-based pain relief employs different neural mechanisms than placebo and sham mindfulness meditation-induced analgesia.** *Journal of Neuroscience* 35(46): 15307–15325. [care]
75 healthy volunteers, with 4 days of training each. Mindfulness reduced experimental heat-pain intensity and unpleasantness more than placebo cream or sham meditation, with a different neural signature. [care] The participants were healthy and the pain was lab-induced. It does not show efficacy for chronic pain (see Hilton 2017). Evidence: preliminary. Used in: "Como saber se algo funciona" (box: 75 volunteers, four days, four arms) and "O corpo, o sono e a dor". Later neural-signature analysis from the same lab: Riegner et al. 2025.
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26586819/ and https://newsroom.wakehealth.edu/News-Releases/2015/11/Mindfulness-Meditation-Trumps-Placebo-in-Pain-Reduction.

**Brook, R. D., Appel, L. J., Rubenfire, M., et al. (2013). Beyond medications and diet: alternative approaches to lowering blood pressure. A scientific statement from the American Heart Association.** *Hypertension* 61: 1360–1383. doi:10.1161/HYP.0b013e318293645f. [care]
Transcendental Meditation: Class IIb, LOE B ("may be considered"; modest BP lowering). *Other* meditation techniques: Class III, LOE C (not recommended due to insufficient evidence). Many trials were flawed. [care] TM-affiliated press releases overstate this statement. Evidence: preliminary-to-moderate (TM); insufficient (other meditation). Note (revisão 2026-10-19): no chapter cites it now; "O corpo, o sono e a dor" uses Schneider et al. 2022 for TM. The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline (Jones et al. 2025, *Hypertension* 82(10): e212–e316, PMID 40811516) "retires and replaces" the 2017 guideline; only its abstract is on file (`jones-2025-aha-bp-guideline-pubmed-abstract-en.txt`), and its stress-reduction recommendations are **not verified**.
Verified via https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/beyond-medications-and-diet-alternative-approaches-to-lowering-blood-pressure\top-things-to-know and https://mdedge.com/content/alternative-therapies-may-help-lower-blood-pressure.

**Black, D. S., & Slavich, G. M. (2016). Mindfulness meditation and the immune system: a systematic review of randomized controlled trials.** *Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences* 1373(1): 13–24. doi:10.1111/nyas.12998. [care]
20 RCTs and 1,602 participants. Tentative signals: lower CRP and NF-κB activity, higher telomerase, and CD4+ counts in HIV. [care] Immune measures were often secondary outcomes, studies were underpowered and heterogeneous, and the authors themselves say the findings are tentative and need replication. Updated by Grasmann et al. 2023 (preregistered, RCTs only: g = −0.11, publication bias) and Ballesio et al. 2023. Evidence: preliminary.
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4940234/ (fetched) and https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26799456/.

**Epel, E., Daubenmier, J., Moskowitz, J. T., Folkman, S., & Blackburn, E. (2009). Can meditation slow rate of cellular aging? Cognitive stress, mindfulness, and telomeres.** *Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences* 1172: 34–53. doi:10.1111/j.1749-6632.2009.04414.x. [care]
A hypothesis-generating review (co-authored by Nobel laureate Blackburn) proposing that meditation might protect telomeres by reducing stress cognition. [care] The title is a question. It is not a trial, and it has been cited popularly as "meditation slows aging". First direct RCT test: Kaliman et al. 2025 (Age-Well, 18 months) found no group effect. Evidence: preliminary (hypothesis); the claim "meditation protects telomeres" is now contested.
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3057175/.

**Conklin, Q. A., Crosswell, A. D., Saron, C. D., & Epel, E. S. (2019). Meditation, stress processes, and telomere biology.** *Current Opinion in Psychology* 28: 92–101. doi:10.1016/j.copsyc.2018.11.009. [care]
An updated review: meditation *may* improve telomere regulation, but mechanisms and boundary conditions remain underspecified. (The brief said *Psychoneuroendocrinology*; the correct journal is *Current Opinion in Psychology*.) First direct RCT test: Kaliman et al. 2025 (Age-Well, 18 months) found no group effect. Evidence: preliminary; the claim "meditation protects telomeres" is now contested. Note (plain-language pass, 2026-10-19): no chapter cites it now; "Estresse" rests the telomere verdict on Epel 2009, Dasanayaka 2021 and Kaliman 2025.
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6526075 and https://prevention.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Conklin-et-al.-2019-Curr-Opin-Psychol.pdf.

**Black, D. S., O'Reilly, G. A., Olmstead, R., Breen, E. C., & Irwin, M. R. (2015). Mindfulness meditation and improvement in sleep quality and daytime impairment among older adults with sleep disturbances: a randomized clinical trial.** *JAMA Internal Medicine* 175(4): 494–501. doi:10.1001/jamainternmed.2014.8081. [care]
49 older adults (mean age about 66) with moderate sleep problems. Six weeks of mindfulness improved PSQI vs sleep-hygiene education (d about 0.89), along with insomnia, depression and fatigue. There was no difference in inflammatory markers. [care] A single small trial. Evidence: preliminary.
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4407465.

**Rusch, H. L., Rosario, M., Levison, L. M., et al. (2019). The effect of mindfulness meditation on sleep quality: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.** *Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences* 1445(1): 5–16. doi:10.1111/nyas.13996. [core]
18 trials and 1,654 participants. Mindfulness improved sleep vs *non-specific* controls (ES about 0.33, moderate evidence), but showed **no** benefit vs *specific* active controls such as evidence-based sleep treatment (ES 0.03). Correct framing: meditation helps sleep somewhat, and it is not better than proper insomnia treatment. Evidence: moderate.
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30575050/ and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6557693. Note: the 0.33 figure is from secondary summaries; check the full text before quoting.

## 5. Adverse effects

**Lindahl, J. R., Fisher, N. E., Cooper, D. J., Rosen, R. K., & Britton, W. B. (2017). The varieties of contemplative experience: A mixed-methods study of meditation-related challenges in Western Buddhists.** *PLOS ONE* 12(5): e0176239. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0176239. [core]
Interviews with about 60 practitioners and about 30 teachers/clinicians from Theravāda, Zen and Tibetan traditions. A taxonomy of **59** meditation-related experiences in 7 domains (cognitive, perceptual, affective, somatic, conative, sense of self, social), some distressing or impairing. Evidence: preliminary (qualitative; not a prevalence estimate).
Verified via https://www.brown.edu/news/2017-05-24/experiences and https://sites.brown.edu/britton/?p=221.

**Britton, W. B., Lindahl, J. R., Cooper, D. J., Canby, N. K., & Palitsky, R. (2021). Defining and measuring meditation-related adverse effects in mindfulness-based programs.** *Clinical Psychological Science* 9(6). doi:10.1177/2167702621996340. [core]
96 participants in 8-week MBCT, assessed by independent interview (MedEx-I). Meditation-related effects with negative valence: 58%. With negative impact on functioning: 37%. **Lasting bad effects: 6–14%**, linked to hyperarousal and dissociation. The authors say the rates are similar to other psychological treatments. Evidence: moderate (careful measurement, modest n).
Verified via https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2167702621996340 and https://www.brown.edu/index%2Ephp/news/2021-05-18/adverse-effects.

**Farias, M., Maraldi, E., Wallenkampf, K. C., & Lucchetti, G. (2020). Adverse events in meditation practices and meditation-based therapies: a systematic review.** *Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica* 142(5). [core]
83 studies and 6,703 participants. The pooled prevalence of adverse events was **8.3%**: 3.7% in experimental studies vs 33.2% in observational ones. Anxiety and depression were most common, and events occurred in people without prior mental-health history. The lead author says 8.3% may be an underestimate. Evidence: moderate (heterogeneous data).
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32820538/ and https://www.coventry.ac.uk/news/2020/research-finds-meditation-and-mindfulness-practices-can-lead-to-depression-and-anxiety/.

**Schlosser, M., Sparby, T., Vörös, S., Jones, R., & Marchant, N. L. (2019). Unpleasant meditation-related experiences in regular meditators: Prevalence, predictors, and conceptual considerations.** *PLOS ONE* 14(5): e0216643.
An online survey of 1,232 regular meditators: **25.6%** reported particularly unpleasant experiences they attributed to meditation. These were more likely with repetitive negative thinking, deconstructive-only practice (e.g., vipassana) and retreat attendance, and less likely in women and religious participants. Evidence: preliminary (single self-report question).
Verified via https://doaj.org/article/4e75f62474f247a78c6531868b06de8f and https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10073649.

## 6. Stress, body, emotion

**Sapolsky, Robert M. (1994; 3rd ed. 2004). Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers.** W. H. Freeman (1994); Henry Holt/Owl (3rd ed., 2004). [core]
The best popular-science explanation of the stress response, and of why *chronic* stress, not acute stress, harms humans. It gives the book its basic physiology. Evidence: n/a (popular synthesis by a leading researcher; some details are dated).
Verified via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Why_Zebras_Don%27t_Get_Ulcers and https://catalog.oslri.net/Record/428701.

**McEwen, B. S. (1998). Protective and damaging effects of stress mediators.** *New England Journal of Medicine* 338(3): 171–179. doi:10.1056/NEJM199801153380307. [core]
Introduces "allostatic load", the cumulative wear and tear when stress responses are too frequent or don't switch off. Evidence: strong as a framework. Specific allostatic-load indices vary across studies.
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9428819/.

**Barrett, Lisa Feldman (2017). How Emotions Are Made: The Secret Life of the Brain.** Houghton Mifflin Harcourt. ISBN 9780544133310. [core] [care]
The theory of constructed emotion: emotions are built by the brain's predictions from bodily signals, concepts and culture, not triggered by dedicated "circuits". [care] This is one side of an active debate with basic-emotion theorists, so present it as a strong theory, not settled fact. Evidence: contested (theory).
Verified via https://www.publishersweekly.com/978-0-544-13331-0 and https://search.worldcat.org/title/970641543.

**Barrett, L. F., & Simmons, W. K. (2015). Interoceptive predictions in the brain.** *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 16(7): 419–429. doi:10.1038/nrn3950.
A technical version of the interoception argument: the brain predicts the body's state, and disrupted predictions may underlie mental and physical illness. It links body-scan practice to a scientific model. Evidence: n/a (opinion/theory paper).
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4731102/.

**Porges, Stephen W. (2011). The Polyvagal Theory: Neurophysiological Foundations of Emotions, Attachment, Communication, and Self-regulation.** W. W. Norton. ISBN 9780393707007. [care]
Hugely popular in therapy and yoga circles ("ventral vagal safety", "dorsal vagal shutdown"). [care] Its core premises are rejected by many autonomic physiologists (see Grossman 2023, 2026). Present it as a cultural phenomenon and a cautionary tale. Porges's 2026 rebuttal (*Clinical Neuropsychiatry* 23(1): 113–128; see Porges 2026 below) is conceptual and contains no new data. Evidence: contested, and largely refuted on its physiological premises.
Verified via https://wwnorton.co.uk/books/9780393707007-the-polyvagal-theory and https://search.worldcat.org/oclc/668193704.

**Grossman, P. (2023). Fundamental challenges and likely refutations of the five basic premises of the polyvagal theory.** *Biological Psychology* 180: 108589. doi:10.1016/j.biopsycho.2023.108589. [core]
Argues that each of polyvagal theory's five premises is untenable or highly implausible. For example, respiratory sinus arrhythmia is not unique to mammals, and treating RSA as "vagal tone" is a category mistake. Evidence: n/a (expert critique). It reflects the dominant view among ANS physiologists.
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37230290/ and https://zenodo.org/records/7955274.

**Grossman, P., Ackland, G. L., Allen, A. M., et al. (39 authors) (2026). Why the polyvagal theory is untenable. An international expert evaluation of the polyvagal theory and commentary upon Porges, S.W. (2025).** *Clinical Neuropsychiatry* 23(1): 100–112. doi:10.36131/cnfioritieditore20260110. [core]
Grossman invited 39 experts in the physiology and evolution of the vagus nerve and in vertebrate social behavior, and all but one accepted, so the byline has 39 names: Grossman and 38 co-authors. They conclude that the theory "is untenable, because it is not defensible based on existing neurophysiological and evolutionary evidence". The dorsal vagus mainly affects the gut, not massive heart-rate slowing, and freezing and dissociation are regulated by other brain centres. Open access (CC BY-NC-SA). Count (plain-language pass, 2026-10-19): the abstract says 39 experts were *invited* by the first author and one declined; the byline lists 39 names including Grossman. "Estresse" therefore says "Grossman e outros 38 especialistas" (EN "Grossman and 38 other experts"). Evidence: n/a (expert evaluation).
Verified via the PDF at https://www.clinicalneuropsychiatry.org/download/why-the-polyvagal-theory-is-untenable-an-international-expert-evaluation-of-the-polyvagal-theory-and-commentary-upon-porges-s-w-2025-polyvagal-theory-current-status-clinical-applications-and/ (`sources/grossman-2026-polyvagal-untenable-en.pdf`; volume, pages and author count from its citation box, (revisão 2026-10-19)) and https://insightplus.mja.com.au/2026/9/polyvagal-theory-debunked-in-comprehensive-new-study. Answered by Porges 2026 (below).

## 7. Self, mind, craving and habit

**Metzinger, Thomas (2003). Being No One: The Self-Model Theory of Subjectivity.** MIT Press.
The technical philosophy: the phenomenal self is the content of a "transparent self-model", a process rather than a thing. Cite it for depth; it is not for beginners. Evidence: n/a (philosophy).
Verified via https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/philosophy-of-science/article/thomas-metzinger-being-no-one-the-selfmodel-theory-of-subjectivity-cambridge-ma-the-mit-press-2003-xiv-699-pp-5500-cloth/48D4780FED5E40B4D2E3BABBC8C85EA4.

**Metzinger, Thomas (2009). The Ego Tunnel: The Science of the Mind and the Myth of the Self.** Basic Books. [core]
The popular version: "there is no such thing as a self", only a model the brain cannot see as a model. It is the scientific counterpart to *anattā*, but note that Metzinger's claim and the Buddhist one are not identical. Evidence: n/a (philosophy).
Verified via https://hachettebookgroup.com/titles/thomas-metzinger/the-ego-tunnel/9780786744428.

**Thompson, Evan (2015). Waking, Dreaming, Being: Self and Consciousness in Neuroscience, Meditation, and Philosophy.** Columbia University Press. ISBN 9780231137096. [core]
Self as process, not illusion. It draws on sleep, dreaming, lucid dreaming and meditation research with Indian philosophy, and its last chapter is "Knowing: is the self an illusion?" (Thompson answers no). This is the book's best nuanced alternative to "the self is an illusion". Evidence: n/a (philosophy/science synthesis).
Verified via https://wellcomecollection.org/works/f6gwk8kb and https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BD12094925.

**Wright, Robert (2017). Why Buddhism Is True: The Science and Philosophy of Meditation and Enlightenment.** Simon & Schuster. ISBN 9781439195451. [care]
A best-selling evolutionary-psychology case that Buddhist diagnoses of the mind (craving, not-self) are "true". [care] It is a popular book whose title overstates its case. Thompson (2020) answers it directly, and it argues for a secularized, Westernized Buddhism. Evidence: n/a (popular argument).
Verified via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Why_Buddhism_Is_True.

**Harris, Sam (2014). Waking Up: A Guide to Spirituality Without Religion.** Simon & Schuster. [care]
The secular, neuroscience-flavored case for meditation and the illusion of self, which is influential through the Waking Up app. [care] It is a popular and personal account with a strong anti-religion framing. Evidence: n/a (popular).
Verified via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waking_Up:_A_Guide_to_Spirituality_Without_Religion.

**Brewer, Judson (2017). The Craving Mind: From Cigarettes to Smartphones to Love — Why We Get Hooked and How We Can Break Bad Habits.** Yale University Press. [care]
Reinforcement learning ("habit loops") read alongside Buddhist craving (*taṇhā*). It is very accessible. [care] The author also sells apps based on the approach, so treat it as a conflict of interest and check claims against the trials. Evidence: n/a (popular; the smoking trials behind it are now contested: see Jackson et al. 2022, Garrison et al. 2020).
Verified via https://faith.yale.edu/people/judson-brewer and https://www.buddhistinquiry.org/person/judson-brewer-md-phd/.

**Brewer, Judson (2021). Unwinding Anxiety: New Science Shows How to Break the Cycles of Worry and Fear to Heal Your Mind.** Avery/Penguin Random House. [care]
Applies the habit-loop model to anxiety. The same caveat applies (popular book; author's commercial app). Evidence: n/a (popular).
Verified via https://faith.yale.edu/people/judson-brewer.

**Brewer, J. A., Mallik, S., Babuscio, T. A., et al. (2011). Mindfulness training for smoking cessation: results from a randomized controlled trial.** *Drug and Alcohol Dependence* 119(1–2): 72–80.
88 smokers: mindfulness vs the American Lung Association's Freedom From Smoking program. Abstinence at end of treatment was 36% vs 15% (a non-significant trend), and at 17 weeks 31% vs 6% (p = .012). The evidence base behind *The Craving Mind*. Evidence (revisão 2026-10-19): contested. The single small RCT was not confirmed by the Cochrane review (Jackson et al. 2022) or by Brewer's own full-scale app trial (Garrison et al. 2020). (Previously "preliminary (single small RCT)".)
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3191261.

**Wood, Wendy (2019). Good Habits, Bad Habits: The Science of Making Positive Changes That Stick.** Farrar, Straus and Giroux. ISBN 9781250159076. [core]
A leading habit researcher explains that habits are context–response associations, and that changing the environment beats willpower. It is the most evidence-based popular habit book. Evidence: n/a (popular synthesis by a primary researcher).
Verified via https://kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/wendy-wood/good-habits-bad-habits and https://newbooksnetwork.com/wendy-wood-good-habits-bad-habits-the-science-of-making-positive-changes-that-stick-farrar-straus-and-giroux-2019.

**Lally, P., van Jaarsveld, C. H. M., Potts, H. W. W., & Wardle, J. (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world.** *European Journal of Social Psychology* 40(6): 998–1009. doi:10.1002/ejsp.674. [core] [care]
96 volunteers repeated a daily behaviour for 12 weeks. Time to 95% of maximum automaticity ranged from **18 to 254 days**, with a central estimate of about 66 days. Missing one day did not derail the process. [care] Popular use has turned "66 days" into a new magic number. The model fit well for only 39 people, and the range is the real message. Consistent with Singh et al. 2024 (medians 59–66 days, range 4–335) and Buyalskaya et al. 2023 (months for the gym, weeks for handwashing). Evidence: preliminary (small, self-report automaticity).
Verified via https://repositorio.ispa.pt/handle/10400.12/3364 and https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit.

**Gardner, B., Lally, P., & Wardle, J. (2012). Making health habitual: the psychology of "habit-formation" and general practice.** *British Journal of General Practice* 62(605): 664–666. doi:10.3399/bjgp12X659466.
Practical habit advice (same context, repeat, be patient). It explicitly traces the "21 days" myth to Maltz's anecdotes about plastic-surgery patients, and cites Lally's average of about 66 days. Evidence: n/a (evidence-based commentary).
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3505409/ (fetched).

## 8. Attention

**Posner, M. I., & Petersen, S. E. (1990). The attention system of the human brain.** *Annual Review of Neuroscience* 13: 25–42. doi:10.1146/annurev.ne.13.030190.000325. [core]
The classic proposal of distinct attention networks: alerting, orienting and executive. It gives the book a simple model for "what meditation trains". Evidence: strong (influential, well-supported framework).
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2183676/.

**Petersen, S. E., & Posner, M. I. (2012). The attention system of the human brain: 20 years after.** *Annual Review of Neuroscience* 35: 73–89. doi:10.1146/annurev-neuro-062111-150525.
An update of the networks model. Evidence: n/a (review).
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3413263.

**Jha, A. P., Stanley, E. A., Kiyonaga, A., Wong, L., & Gelfand, L. (2010). Examining the protective effects of mindfulness training on working memory capacity and affective experience.** *Emotion* 10(1): 54–64. doi:10.1037/a0018438.
Marines before deployment (31 trained, 17 military controls, 12 civilians). Working memory fell in controls under stress, and in the trained group it rose only among those who **practiced more**. A good "dose matters" story. [care] It was not randomized, the sample was small, and the dose effect is correlational. Evidence: preliminary. Note (plain-language pass, 2026-10-19): no chapter cites it now ("Atenção, emoção e hábito" dropped it; "Atenção na respiração" uses Jha et al. 2015).
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20141302/ and https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/building-fit-minds-under-stress-penn-neuroscientists-examine-protective-effects-mindfulness-tra.

**MacLean, K. A., Ferrer, E., Aichele, S. R., ... Saron, C. D. (2010). Intensive meditation training improves perceptual discrimination and sustained attention.** *Psychological Science* 21(6): 829–839. doi:10.1177/0956797610371339.
The Shamatha Project: 60 participants (30 trained first, 30 waitlist), about 3 months at about 5 h/day of retreat practice. Improved visual discrimination and vigilance. Note the dose, which is far beyond an app. Follow-up: Zanesco et al. 2018 (gains partly maintained up to 7 years, moderated by continued practice; correlational, preliminary). Evidence: moderate (randomized waitlist, intensive dose).
Verified via https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/intensive-meditation-training-improves-perceptual-discrimination-/ and https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/07/100714121737.htm.

**Slagter, H. A., Lutz, A., Greischar, L. L., Francis, A. D., Nieuwenhuis, S., Davis, J. M., & Davidson, R. J. (2007). Mental training affects distribution of limited brain resources.** *PLoS Biology* 5(6): e138. doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.0050138.
17 practitioners on a 3-month vipassana retreat vs 23 novices (EEG analysis: 10 vs 12). The attentional blink became smaller, and the people who allocated fewer resources to the first target improved most. Evidence: preliminary (small, non-randomized).
Verified via https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.0050138 (fetched).

**Tang, Y.-Y., Ma, Y., Wang, J., et al. (2007). Short-term meditation training improves attention and self-regulation.** *PNAS* 104(43): 17152–17156.
40 Chinese undergraduates in the experimental group (corrected 2026-10-11: not the whole sample) given 5 days × 20 minutes of integrative body–mind training, vs relaxation training. Better executive attention (ANT), better mood, and lower cortisol. The method "comes from traditional Chinese medicine and incorporates aspects of other meditation and mindfulness training" (abstract), so it is not a standard mindfulness course. [care] Small, very short, and the authors call for larger studies. Evidence: preliminary.
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17940025/.

## 9. Compassion and loving-kindness

**Fredrickson, B. L., Cohn, M. A., Coffey, K. A., Pek, J., & Finkel, S. M. (2008). Open hearts build lives: Positive emotions, induced through loving-kindness meditation, build consequential personal resources.** *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 95(5): 1045–1062. [care]
139 working adults with a waitlist control. Loving-kindness practice raised daily positive emotions, which built resources (mindfulness, purpose, social support) that predicted life satisfaction and fewer depressive symptoms. [care] The "broaden-and-build" framework and related positivity-ratio work from Fredrickson's program have faced criticism, so cite this specific trial and not the wider claims. Evidence: preliminary-to-moderate.
Verified via https://www.wisebrain.org/media/Papers/Metta&PosEmot.pdf and https://ggia.berkeley.edu/practice/practice_as_pdf/loving_kindness_meditation.

**Klimecki, O. M., Leiberg, S., Ricard, M., & Singer, T. (2014). Differential pattern of functional brain plasticity after compassion and empathy training.** *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience* 9(6): 873–879. doi:10.1093/scan/nst060. [core]
Empathy training *increased* negative affect and insula/aMCC activity. Subsequent compassion training reversed this and engaged reward/affiliation regions. It is the source of the useful "empathic distress vs compassion" distinction. Evidence: preliminary (small, sequential design).
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4040103.

**Weng, H. Y., Fox, A. S., Shackman, A. J., Stodola, D. E., Caldwell, J. Z. K., Olson, M. C., Rogers, G. M., & Davidson, R. J. (2013). Compassion training alters altruism and neural responses to suffering.** *Psychological Science* 24(7): 1171–1180. doi:10.1177/0956797612469537.
41 adults (compassion n = 20 vs reappraisal training n = 21; 30 minutes a day for 2 weeks). Compassion training increased money given to a victim in an economic game, linked to changes in parietal/DLPFC activity. 15 of 56 completers were excluded for suspicion of the game. Evidence: preliminary.
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3713090/ and https://foxlab.ucdavis.edu/publications/Weng_PsychScience_2013.pdf (PDF read).

**Singer, T., Kok, B. E., Bornemann, B., Zurborg, S., Bolz, M., & Bochow, C. (2016). The ReSource Project: Background, design, samples, and measurements.** Max Planck Institute for Human Cognitive and Brain Sciences, Leipzig. [core]
The design document for the largest longitudinal secular meditation study: 9 months of three modules (Presence, Affect, Perspective) in Leipzig and Berlin, 2012–2016. It shows that different practices train different things. Evidence: n/a (protocol).
Verified via https://social.mpg.de/forschung/plastizitaet-des-sozialen-gehirns/resource-projekt/publikationen and https://www.resource-project.org/en/about-the-project.

**Valk, S. L., Bernhardt, B. C., ... Singer, T. (2017). Structural plasticity of the social brain: Differential change after socio-affective and cognitive mental training.** *Science Advances* 3(10): e1700489. [care]
ReSource MRI: different modules produced different cortical-thickness changes, which tracked gains in compassion and perspective-taking. [care] It conflicts in spirit with Kral 2022's null for MBSR. Possible reasons are the longer (3-month modules) and more specific training. Its caveat now has a concrete rival: the Age-Well RCT (Chételat et al. 2022; 18 months, null on volume). Treat it as preliminary. Evidence: preliminary.
Verified via `valk-2017-social-brain-plasticity-pubmed-abstract-en.txt` (PMID 28983507; (revisão 2026-10-19)), https://www.cbs.mpg.de/press-release/improving-social-skills and https://fis.tu-dresden.de/portal/en/publications/structural-plasticity-of-the-social-brain(11c675fb-c2f1-4da7-9615-b7d9eb99d116).html.

**Engert, V., Kok, B. E., Papassotiriou, I., Chrousos, G. P., & Singer, T. (2017). Specific reduction in cortisol stress reactivity after social but not attention-based mental training.** *Science Advances* 3(10): e1700495. doi:10.1126/sciadv.1700495.
In ReSource, cortisol responses to a social stress test fell after the social (compassion or perspective) modules, but **not** after the attention/body-awareness module. Useful nuance: "meditation" is not one intervention. Qualified by Puhlmann et al. 2021 (same project): hair cortisol (long-term exposure) fell under all practice types, including the attention-based Presence module, so the "attention changes self-report, not cortisol" contrast holds for acute reactivity only. Evidence: preliminary-to-moderate.
Verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28983508/. Note: the result details were confirmed through later papers that cite it, not from the full text.

**Kok, B. E., & Singer, T. (2017). Effects of contemplative dyads on engagement and perceived social connectedness over 9 months of mental training: a randomized clinical trial.** *JAMA Psychiatry* 74(2): 126–134. doi:10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2016.3360.
362 participants. Partner-based ("dyadic") practices were as engaging as solitary meditation and increased felt social closeness. Relevant to loneliness and to practising with others. Evidence: moderate.
Verified via https://www.globalcompassioncoalition.org/effects-of-contemplative-dyads-on-engagement-and-perceived-social-connectedness-over-9-months-of-mental-training-a-randomized-clinical-trial/.

## 10. Methods and hype literacy

**Open Science Collaboration (2015). Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science.** *Science* 349(6251): aac4716. doi:10.1126/science.aac4716. [core]
100 replications: 97% of originals were significant vs **36%** of replications, and effect sizes were about half. This is the reason the book labels evidence strength at all. Updated by Tyner et al. 2026 (SCORE): about half replicate. Evidence: strong.
Verified via https://osf.io/ezcuj/wiki and https://acawiki.org/Estimating_the_reproducibility_of_psychological_science.

**Button, K. S., Ioannidis, J. P. A., Mokrysz, C., Nosek, B. A., Flint, J., Robinson, E. S. J., & Munafò, M. R. (2013). Power failure: why small sample size undermines the reliability of neuroscience.** *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 14(5): 365–376. doi:10.1038/nrn3475. [core]
Median statistical power in neuroscience was about 21%, so small studies miss real effects *and* inflate the effects they do find. This is the key concept for explaining why n = 16 brain studies don't settle anything. Reinforced for brain–behaviour correlations by Marek et al. 2022. Evidence: strong (the exact median is debated).
Verified via https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/serious-power-failure-threatens-entire-field-neuroscience.

**Poldrack, R. A. (2006). Can cognitive processes be inferred from neuroimaging data?** *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 10(2): 59–63. doi:10.1016/j.tics.2005.12.004. [core]
"Reverse inference": inferring a mental process from activation (e.g., "the amygdala lit up, so fear") is weak evidence unless the region is selective. Essential for reading meditation-fMRI claims. Evidence: n/a (methodological argument; widely accepted).
Verified via https://ibl.kb.nl/articles/0000000011312/10/2 and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240863.

**Bennett, C. M., Baird, A. A., Miller, M. B., & Wolford, G. L. (2010). Neural correlates of interspecies perspective taking in the post-mortem Atlantic salmon: An argument for proper multiple comparisons correction.** *Journal of Serendipitous and Unexpected Results* 1: 1–5.
A dead salmon "responded" to social photos in fMRI without multiple-comparison correction. It won the 2012 Ig Nobel (Neuroscience Prize) and makes a perfect beginner-level illustration. Evidence: n/a (methods demonstration).
Ig Nobel verified (revisão 2026-10-19) from the primary list, https://improbable.com/ig/winners/ (`improbable-2012-ig-nobel-neuroscience-en.txt`). Verified via https://w.mriquestions.com/uploads/3/4/5/7/34572113/bennett_salmon_journal_serendipity.pdf and https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/scicurious-brain/ignobel-prize-in-neuroscience-the-dead-salmon-study.

**Eklund, A., Nichols, T. E., & Knutsson, H. (2016). Cluster failure: Why fMRI inferences for spatial extent have inflated false-positive rates.** *PNAS* 113(28): 7900–7905. doi:10.1073/pnas.1602413113. [care]
Common fMRI software settings produced false-positive rates up to 70% for cluster inference. [care] Media overstated it as "15 years of fMRI invalid", and the authors issued a correction for two overstated sentences. Evidence: strong (for the specific statistical problem).
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4948312.

**Boot, W. R., Simons, D. J., Stothart, C., & Stutts, C. (2013). The pervasive problem with placebos in psychology: why active control groups are not sufficient to rule out placebo effects.** *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 8(4): 445–454. doi:10.1177/1745691613491271. [core]
Active controls only rule out placebo if they create *equal expectations* of benefit, and meditation trials rarely measure this. It is the key to the "expectation" problem. Evidence: n/a (methods argument).
Verified via https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/1745691613491271/ and https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/the-pervasive-problem-with-placebos-in-psychology-why-active-cont/.

**MacCoon, D. G., Imel, Z. E., Rosenkranz, M. A., ... Lutz, A. (2012). The validation of an active control intervention for Mindfulness Based Stress Reduction (MBSR).** *Behaviour Research and Therapy* 50(1): 3–12.
Built the Health Enhancement Program (HEP) as a matched control. 63 participants: MBSR and HEP improved distress and symptoms **equally**. The MBSR advantage on heat pain held only "in the mindfulness- but not the HEP-related instruction condition". Shows that many "mindfulness" benefits are non-specific. HEP is the control used in Kral 2022. Evidence (revisão 2026-10-19): preliminary (one study, n = 63; previously "preliminary-to-moderate").
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3257026 and PMID 22137364 (`maccoon-2012-active-control-mbsr-pubmed-abstract-en.txt`).

**Davidson, R. J., & Kaszniak, A. W. (2015). Conceptual and methodological issues in research on mindfulness and meditation.** *American Psychologist* 70(7): 581–592. doi:10.1037/a0039512.
An insider's list of the problems: blinding is impossible, control conditions are hard, self-report measures are weak, and definitions are fuzzy. Evidence: n/a (methods review).
Verified via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4627495.

**Goldberg, S. B., & Tucker, R. P. (2020; online 2019). Allegiance effects in mindfulness-based interventions for psychiatric disorders: a meta-re-analysis.** *Psychotherapy Research* 30(6): 753–762. doi:10.1080/10503307.2019.1664783. [core]
60 comparisons (n = 5,627). MBIs' small edge over other bona fide treatments (g = 0.13) was linked to **researcher allegiance**, meaning investigators who favour mindfulness found bigger effects. The RA–outcome link is a between-study association (meta-regression), not an experiment. Evidence: moderate.
Verified via https://digitalscholar.lsuhsc.edu/som_facpubs/1960 and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6602541.

**Wampold, B. E., & Imel, Z. E. (2015). The Great Psychotherapy Debate: The Evidence for What Makes Psychotherapy Work (2nd ed.).** Routledge. ISBN 9780805857092.
The "contextual model": common factors (alliance, expectations, a credible rationale) explain much of the benefit across therapies. It helps explain why MBSR ≈ HEP and MBCT ≈ other good treatments. Evidence: moderate (influential, but contested by "specific factors" researchers).
Verified via https://www.routledge.com/The-Great-Psychotherapy-Debate/Wampold-Imel/p/book/9780805857092.

## 11. Sources used only for myth-busting

**Scientific American (2008, Feb 7). Do People Only Use 10 Percent of Their Brains?** https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/do-people-only-use-10-percent-of-their-brains/
Neurologist Barry Gordon calls the claim "so wrong it is almost laughable". The brain is about 3% of body weight and uses about 20% of the body's energy, and most of it is active almost all the time. Evidence: strong (scientific consensus).
Verified via the URL above. Note: the author byline was not confirmed in search results.

**Nielsen, J. A., Zielinski, B. A., Ferguson, M. A., Lainhart, J. E., & Anderson, J. S. (2013). An evaluation of the left-brain vs. right-brain hypothesis with resting state functional connectivity magnetic resonance imaging.** *PLOS ONE* 8(8): e71275. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0071275.
1,011 people aged 7–29. Lateralization is connection-by-connection, and no one is globally "left-brained" or "right-brained". Evidence: moderate.
Verified via https://doaj.org/article/93e38a4098db4fc6b48bfb4f6bf84a30 and https://uofuhealth.utah.edu/newsroom/news/2013/no-evidence-right-brained-vs-left-brained.

**Yuval-Greenberg, S., Tomer, O., Keren, A. S., Nelken, I., & Deouell, L. Y. (2008). Transient induced gamma-band response in EEG as a manifestation of miniature saccades.** *Neuron* 58(3): 429–441. doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2008.04.020.
Showed that a common scalp-EEG "gamma" response can be produced by tiny involuntary eye movements. It is a reason for caution with any EEG-gamma headline, including meditation ones. It does not directly refute Lutz 2004, which used different conditions. Evidence: strong (for the artifact); its relevance to meditation studies is inferential.
Verified via https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/transient-induced-gamma-band-response-in-eeg-as-a-manifestation-o/.

## Popular claims that are false or overstated

1. **"Meditation grows your brain (grey matter) in 8 weeks."** This is overstated, and in healthy adults it did not replicate. It came from small studies (Hölzel 2011: n = 16 vs 17 waitlist, not randomized). The largest RCT (Kral et al. 2022, n = 218, with active and waitlist controls) found **no** MBSR-specific structural change. Morphometry meta-analysis (Fox 2014) flags publication bias. MRI structural-plasticity claims in general are fragile (Thomas & Baker 2013). Structural change has been shown only for years-long learning (Woollett & Maguire 2011). Eighteen months of meditation in older adults did not change ACC or insula volume either (Chételat et al. 2022, Age-Well), and the one meta-analysis claiming RCT-based structural change (Siew & Yu 2023) was retracted in 2025 for excluding null studies. *Correctors: Kral 2022; Chételat 2022; Thomas & Baker 2013; Fox 2014; Siew & Yu 2023 (retraction note).*

2. **"We only use 10% of our brains."** False. *Corrector: Scientific American 2008.*

3. **"It takes 21 days to form a habit."** False. The figure comes from Maltz's 1960 anecdotes about plastic-surgery patients getting used to a new self-image, not about habits. Measured time to automaticity ranged from 18 to 254 days (about 66 on average), and "66 days" should not become a new rule either. *Correctors: Lally et al. 2010; Gardner, Lally & Wardle 2012; Singh et al. 2024; Buyalskaya et al. 2023.*

4. **"Monks' gamma waves prove enlightenment / meditation produces a special brain state."** Overstated. Lutz 2004 is small and cross-sectional, and the paper itself flags muscle artifact above about 42 Hz. Scalp gamma is vulnerable to eye-movement artifacts (Yuval-Greenberg 2008). Mapping "gamma" onto "enlightenment" is reverse inference (Poldrack 2006). *Correctors: Yuval-Greenberg 2008; Poldrack 2006; Van Dam 2018.*

5. **"Mindfulness works better than antidepressants."** Overstated. For relapse prevention MBCT is about **equal** to maintenance antidepressants (PREVENT: 44% vs 47%). For anxiety, MBSR was non-inferior to escitalopram in one trial (Hoge 2023). Meditation programs did not beat active treatments (Goyal 2014), and MBIs ≈ evidence-based treatments (Goldberg 2018), with an edge linked to researcher allegiance (Goldberg & Tucker 2020). *Correctors: Kuyken 2015; Goyal 2014; Goldberg 2018.*

6. **"Meditation is always safe / has no side effects."** False. Pooled adverse-event prevalence is about 8.3% (Farias 2020). In MBCT, 37% reported some negative impact on functioning and 6–14% reported lasting bad effects (Britton 2021). 25.6% of regular meditators report particularly unpleasant experiences (Schlosser 2019). *Correctors: Farias 2020; Britton 2021; Lindahl 2017.*

7. **"Mindfulness in schools improves teen mental health."** Not supported for universal delivery. MYRIAD (84 schools, 8,376 students) found no benefit vs usual teaching at 1 year. *Correctors: Kuyken et al. 2022; Galla et al. 2024; Holst et al. 2026; Webb et al. 2026.*

8. **"Meditation lengthens telomeres / reverses aging / boosts immunity."** Preliminary at best. The reviews call the findings tentative and heterogeneous, and the immune measures were mostly secondary outcomes. The first direct RCT (Age-Well, 18 months) found no group effect on telomere length, and the preregistered RCT-only biomarker synthesis found g = −0.11. *Correctors: Black & Slavich 2016; Conklin et al. 2019; Kaliman et al. 2025; Grasmann et al. 2023.*

9. **"Polyvagal theory explains how meditation and breathing 'activate the ventral vagus' to make you feel safe."** Its physiological premises are rejected by a broad group of autonomic specialists (Grossman and 38 co-authors in 2026); no source here counts the whole field, so the book does not say "most". *Correctors: Grossman 2023; Grossman et al. 2026.*

10. **"New neurons grow in your adult brain when you meditate / learn."** Unsupported for meditation, and whether adult human hippocampal neurogenesis happens at all is contested. *Correctors: Sorrells 2018 vs Boldrini 2018 / Moreno-Jiménez 2019; Kempermann 2018; Sorrells 2021; Franjic 2022; Zhou 2022; Dumitru 2025.*

11. **"Buddhism is a science of mind, not a religion" / "the Buddha anticipated neuroscience."** A modern construction. *Correctors: Lopez 2008, 2012; McMahan 2008; Thompson 2020; Sharf 1995, 2015.*

12. **"People are left-brained or right-brained."** False. *Corrector: Nielsen et al. 2013.*

13. **"Mindfulness helps you quit smoking."** Not demonstrated (added 2026-10-19). Brewer 2011 was small; the Cochrane review found no clear benefit; the developer's own app RCT was null. *Correctors: Jackson et al. 2022; Garrison et al. 2020.*

### Methods and hype literacy [§10]

**Tyner, A. H., Abatayo, A. L., Daley, M., Field, S., et al. (2026). Investigating the replicability of the social and behavioural sciences.** *Nature* 652: 143–150. doi:10.1038/s41586-025-10078-y. [core]

The authors note that some decline is expected because only positive results were replicated (regression to the mean). It supersedes OSC 2015 as the headline replication figure for the behavioural sciences. Evidence: strong.

**Goldberg, S. B., Riordan, K. M., Sun, S., & Davidson, R. J. (2022; online 2021). The Empirical Status of Mindfulness-Based Interventions: A Systematic Review of 44 Meta-Analyses of Randomized Controlled Trials.** *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 17(1): 108–130. doi:10.1177/1745691620968771. [core] [care]
Umbrella review: 160 effect sizes in 44 meta-analyses (336 RCTs, N = 30,483). MBIs beat passive controls across most populations and outcomes (ds = 0.10–0.89). Effects were smaller and less often significant against active controls, and "similar or superior" to specific active controls and evidence-based treatments. Against evidence-based treatments the exceptions are smoking at post-treatment (d = 0.42, from 4 studies and 587 participants, Goldberg 2018 data; 95% CI 0.20–0.64; not long-term abstinence) and MBCT relapse prevention at follow-up (d = 0.17); MBIs may be weaker for anxiety (d = −0.18, CI −0.41 to 0.06). Results were "generally robust to publication bias", but other sources of bias were found, adverse-effect reporting was inconsistent, and mean power was .52 for active-control comparisons vs .78 for passive. [care] Written from inside the field: the authors are at the Center for Healthy Minds (University of Wisconsin–Madison), and Davidson declares Healthy Minds Innovations (see the Goldberg 2018 record in VERIFIED.md). The conclusions are self-critical, but say who wrote it. Evidence: moderate (overall picture); contested for the smoking advantage (consistent with Jackson et al. 2022).
Verified via PubMed PMID 33593124 and full text PMC8364929 (`goldberg-2022-empirical-status-44-pubmed-abstract-en.txt`, duplicate `goldberg-2022-empirical-status-mbis-pubmed-abstract-en.txt`, `goldberg-2022-empirical-status-44-pmc-en.xml`). **Cuijpers, P., Miguel, C., Harrer, M., Ciharova, M., & Karyotaki, E. (2024). [core]
333 RCTs of psychotherapy for adult depression (41,480 participants). Effects were g = 0.95 against a waitlist vs g = 0.63 against care as usual, and the difference survived all sensitivity analyses. It concerns psychotherapy, not meditation, but the design issue is identical. Evidence: strong.
Verified via PubMed PMID 39501660 (PMC11561681). Used in: "Como saber se algo funciona".

**Coronado-Montoya, S., Levis, A. W., Kwakkenbos, L., Steele, R. J., Turner, E. H., & Thombs, B. D. (2016). Reporting of Positive Results in Randomized Controlled Trials of Mindfulness-Based Mental Health Interventions.** *PLoS One* 11(4): e0153220. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0153220. [core]

[care] Healthy participants, acute lab-induced pain, small arms, one lab (Zeidan is senior author here and on Zeidan 2015). ### Neuroplasticity and brain structure [§2, §3]

**Chételat, G., Lutz, A., Klimecki, O., et al.; Medit-Ageing Research Group (2022). Effect of an 18-Month Meditation Training on Regional Brain Volume and Perfusion in Older Adults: The Age-Well Randomized Clinical Trial.** *JAMA Neurology* 79(11): 1165–1174. doi:10.1001/jamaneurol.2022.3185. [core] [care]
137 cognitively unimpaired adults aged 65 and over, randomized to 18 months of meditation (n = 45), structurally matched English training (n = 46) or no intervention (n = 46). No difference in ACC or insula volume change; perfusion differences not significant. Meditation beat language training on a composite of attention regulation, socioemotional and self-knowledge capacities (d = 0.52). The longest randomized test of structural change from meditation; it answers Kral 2022's "much longer training" suggestion for older adults. [care] A single trial, in older adults only, with two target regions. One author (Klimecki) reports teaching meditation, which for a null structural result would bias against the finding, not toward it. Evidence: the claim that 18 months of meditation changes regional volume in older adults is **contested** (this trial did not find it), as the chapter labels it.

### Mind wandering, rumination and the default mode network [§3]

**Kawashima, I., Hinuma, T., & Tanaka, S. C. (2023). Ecological momentary assessment of mind-wandering: meta-analysis and systematic review.** *Scientific Reports* 13(1): 2873. doi:10.1038/s41598-023-29854-9. [core]
25 daily-life (EMA) studies reviewed, 17 meta-analysed: about 34.5% of daily life spent mind-wandering, lower than Killingsworth & Gilbert's 46.9%. Smartphone use, frequent sampling and long duration changed the reports, which suggests reactivity. Evidence: strong that mind-wandering is frequent; the exact share depends on the method.
Verified via PubMed PMID 36801931 (`kawashima-2023-ema-mind-wandering-pubmed-abstract-en.txt`). Used in: "A mente que divaga".

**Zanesco, A. P., Denkova, E., & Jha, A. P. (2025). Mind-wandering increases in frequency over time during task performance: an individual-participant meta-analytic review.** *Psychological Bulletin* 151(2): 217–239. doi:10.1037/bul0000424.
68 reports, almost half a million probe responses, more than 10,000 people. Mind-wandering increases with time on task, linked to boredom and declining performance. Evidence: moderate (large pooled dataset, lab tasks).
Verified via PubMed PMID 38421788 (`zanesco-2025-mind-wandering-time-pubmed-abstract-en.txt`). Used in: "A mente que divaga".

**Kam, J. W. Y., Wong, A. Y., Thiemann, R. F., Hasan, F., Andrews-Hanna, J. R., & Mills, C. (2024). On the relationship between unprompted thought and affective well-being: a systematic review and meta-analysis.** *Psychological Bulletin* 150(5): 621–641. doi:10.1037/bul0000428. [core]
76 studies reviewed; 386 effect sizes from 23,168 participants in 64 studies. Overall r = −.18 between unprompted thought and affective well-being; size and direction change with content, intentionality and method. The book's main source for the wandering–mood claim. Evidence: moderate for a small average association; correlational, so it does not establish cause.
Verified via PubMed PMID 38619477 (`kam-2024-unprompted-thought-wellbeing-pubmed-abstract-en.txt`). Used in: "A mente que divaga".

**Poerio, G. L., Totterdell, P., & Miles, E. (2013). Mind-wandering and negative mood: does one thing really lead to another?** *Consciousness and Cognition* 22(4): 1412–1421. doi:10.1016/j.concog.2013.09.012.
Seven-day smartphone experience sampling. Sadness tended to precede mind-wandering; mind-wandering itself did not predict later mood, and predicted feeling worse only when its content was negative. Contradicts Killingsworth & Gilbert's time-lag direction. Evidence: preliminary (single study; sample size not recorded).

### Craving, addiction and habit [§7]

Animal study proposing retrospective causal learning: mesolimbic dopamine release "conveys causal associations but not RPE", challenging the reward-prediction-error account. The book's source for "dopamine as prediction error is debated". Evidence: preliminary (one high-profile challenge; the debate is live).

40 RCTs (35 in meta-analysis, 2,825 participants); tobacco excluded. Against no treatment the evidence is uncertain; against other treatments MBIs "may slightly reduce days with substance use". All studies at high risk of performance bias. Evidence: preliminary.
